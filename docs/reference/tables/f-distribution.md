# \(F\) distribution table

The \(F\) statistic is a ratio of two independent variance estimates, each divided
by its degrees of freedom:

\[ F=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2} \sim F_{\nu_1,\nu_2} \quad\text{under } H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2 . \]

It has two degrees of freedom because there are two variances. The table prints the
**upper-tail** critical value

\[ F_{\alpha,\nu_1,\nu_2}\quad\text{with}\quad P(F_{\nu_1,\nu_2}\ge F_{\alpha,\nu_1,\nu_2})=\alpha , \]

where \(\nu_1\) is the **numerator** df and \(\nu_2\) the **denominator** df. The
order is not cosmetic: \(F_{0.05,3,40}=2.839\) but \(F_{0.05,40,3}=8.594\). Reading
the table transposed is the classic \(F\) error.

## Choosing the two degrees of freedom

| Design | \(\nu_1\) (numerator) | \(\nu_2\) (denominator) |
|---|---|---|
| One-way ANOVA, \(k\) groups, \(N\) observations | \(k-1\) | \(N-k\) |
| Two-way ANOVA, factor A with \(a\) levels | \(a-1\) | \(N-ab\) (with interaction) |
| Interaction in a two-way design | \((a-1)(b-1)\) | \(N-ab\) |
| Ratio of two variances, sample sizes \(n_1,n_2\) | \(n_1-1\) | \(n_2-1\) |
| Compare full and reduced regression models | extra parameters | \(n-\) parameters in the full model |
| Repeated-measures ANOVA | effect df | subject by effect df |

The numerator df is **always the number of restrictions you are testing**: three
extra group means is \(k-1=3\); one extra regression term is 1. The denominator df
is the df of the residual variance you divide by. If you can say what the residual
is, you can say \(\nu_2\).

## One-way ANOVA: worked example

Four teaching methods, 11 students each, \(N=44\), one-way ANOVA gives
\(F=7.53\). Then

\[ \nu_1=k-1=3,\qquad \nu_2=N-k=44-4=40 . \]

From the 5 % table, \(F_{0.05,3,40}=2.839\); from the 1 % table, \(4.313\).
Since \(7.53>4.313\), reject the null of equal means at 1 % as well as 5 %
(exact \(p=0.0004\)).

If your denominator df falls between two tabled rows — say \(\nu_2=44\) — read the
**smaller** row (40), because the critical value falls as \(\nu_2\) grows; using
\(\nu_2=40\) is then the slightly conservative choice, and software gives the exact
boundary when you need it.

Three things the boundary does not tell you, and the report must:

- **Which** methods differ. Run Tukey's HSD or planned contrasts; the omnibus test
  is a gate, not an answer.
- **How much** the means differ, in the units of the outcome, with intervals.
- **How much of the variation** is explained: \(\eta^2\) here is about 0.34, so a
  third of the variability in marks is between methods — a large effect for an
  educational intervention, and one worth checking for confounding by class.

The assumptions behind the reference distribution are independence, normal
residuals and equal variances. Check them with a residual plot and Levene's test;
if variances differ materially, use Welch's ANOVA, whose df are fractional and
whose critical value must come from software.

## Variance comparison: worked example

Two production lines: line 1 has \(n_1=10\), \(s_1^2=9.0\); line 2 has \(n_2=12\),
\(s_2^2=3.1\). Test \(H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2\).

\[ F=\frac{9.0}{3.1}=2.90,\qquad \nu_1=9,\qquad \nu_2=11 . \]

- One-sided (line 1 more variable, decided in advance):
  \(F_{0.05,9,11}=2.896\). The statistic is \(2.903\), which clears the boundary by
  seven thousandths — a rejection that is technically correct and practically
  meaningless. Report the interval for the ratio instead.
- Two-sided at 5 %: read the \(2.5\) % column, \(F_{0.025,9,11}=3.588\). Now the
  same statistic does not reject, because a two-sided question spends half the
  error on each direction.

That contrast is the whole reason the tail convention matters. The lower boundary
is never tabled: use \(F_{1-\alpha,\nu_1,\nu_2}=1/F_{\alpha,\nu_2,\nu_1}\).

## Reading the conclusion

1. Identify \(\nu_1\), \(\nu_2\) and the test direction **before** computing.
2. Compute the ratio, always putting the larger variance on top when the test is
   one-sided.
3. Compare with the boundary; if it is close, quote the exact \(p\)-value.
4. Report effect size (for ANOVA, \(\eta^2\) or \(\omega^2\); for variances, the
   ratio with its interval) and the assumption checks.

## Self-check

1. Give \(F_{0.05,3,40}\) and \(F_{0.01,3,40}\).
2. Five groups, 20 observations each: what are \(\nu_1\) and \(\nu_2\)?
3. Why is \(F_{0.05,44,3}\ne F_{0.05,3,44}\)?
4. A two-sided variance test at 5 % with \(\nu_1=9,\nu_2=11\): which column?
5. \(F=2.90\) with \(\nu_1=9,\nu_2=11\): significant one-sided at 5 %?

**Answers.** (1) 2.839 and 4.313. (2) \(\nu_1=4\), \(\nu_2=100-5=95\). (3) Because
the ratio is inverted: the two df play different roles and the distribution is
skewed, so swapping them gives a different quantile. (4) The 0.025 column: 3.588.
(5) Marginally: \(2.90>2.896\), so yes, and the honest report says "borderline,
with the interval for the ratio spanning 1".
