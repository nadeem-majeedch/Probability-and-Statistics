## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The posterior distribution is proportional to
A) prior + likelihood
B) likelihood / prior
C) prior only
D) prior × likelihood
* D
Why: Bayes' theorem multiplies prior belief by the evidence's likelihood and normalises. Adding would not produce a distribution over the parameter.

Q2. A Beta prior is conjugate to
A) the normal likelihood
B) the Poisson likelihood
C) the exponential likelihood
D) the binomial likelihood
* D
Why: A Beta prior with a binomial likelihood yields a Beta posterior, so updating is just adding successes and failures to the parameters.

Q3. A 95 % credible interval means
A) the posterior mean is correct 95 % of the time
B) given the model and prior, the parameter lies in the interval with probability 0.95
C) 95 % of repeated samples produce intervals containing the parameter
D) 95 % of the data lie in the interval
* B
Why: Credible intervals are posterior probability statements about the parameter. The repeated-sampling interpretation belongs to confidence intervals.

Q4. The MAP estimate is
A) the prior mean
B) the maximum likelihood estimate always
C) the posterior mode
D) the posterior mean
* C
Why: MAP maximises the posterior density. It differs from the posterior mean unless the posterior is symmetric, and from the MLE when the prior is informative.

Q5. A Bayes factor compares
A) two p-values
B) the marginal likelihoods of two models
C) two posterior means
D) prior and posterior
* B
Why: \(BF_{10}=P(data\mid M_1)/P(data\mid M_0)\) quantifies relative evidence. Values above 1 favour \(M_1\); the scale is not a probability.

Q6. With a flat prior, the posterior is
A) undefined
B) proportional to the likelihood
C) uniform always
D) equal to the prior
* B
Why: A constant prior multiplies the likelihood without changing its shape, so the posterior mode equals the MLE. Flat priors are not literally uniform on every transformation of the parameter.

Q7. The posterior predictive distribution is used to
A) compute a p-value
B) test normality
C) predict new observations, integrating over posterior uncertainty in the parameters
D) estimate the prior
* C
Why: It averages the sampling distribution over the posterior, so predictions include both parameter uncertainty and observation noise.

Q8. MCMC is used because
A) most posteriors are not analytically tractable
B) it eliminates the need for a prior
C) it guarantees exact answers
D) it replaces the likelihood
* A
Why: Markov chain Monte Carlo draws samples from the posterior when the normalising constant is unavailable. It is approximate and requires convergence diagnostics.

Q9. Ridge regression can be viewed as
A) a normal prior on the coefficients with a penalty on their size
B) an uninformative prior
C) a nonparametric model
D) a form of cross-validation
* A
Why: Minimising squared error plus \(\lambda\|\beta\|^2\) is the posterior mode under a normal prior. The penalty is a prior belief that coefficients are small.

Q10. Hierarchical models are useful because they
A) eliminate prior sensitivity
B) partially pool information across groups, shrinking noisy estimates
C) remove the need for data
D) assume all groups are identical
* B
Why: Partial pooling borrows strength from the population of groups, stabilising estimates for small groups while allowing real differences.

Q11. Compared with a frequentist confidence interval, a Bayesian credible interval
A) requires no assumptions
B) cannot be computed from the same data
C) has a direct probability interpretation given the model
D) is always narrower
* C
Why: It states posterior probability for the parameter. Both require model assumptions, and the intervals often agree numerically under weak priors.

Q12. Prior sensitivity analysis is important because
A) priors are always wrong
B) the likelihood is unreliable
C) it reduces computation
D) conclusions should be robust to reasonable prior choices
* D
Why: If the posterior changes materially across plausible priors, the data are not informative enough to settle the question, and that must be reported.

Q13. Sequential Bayesian updating is coherent because
A) the posterior after one batch becomes the prior for the next
B) the prior is discarded
C) the likelihood is cumulative only
D) each update is independent of the data
* A
Why: This follows from the product rule for likelihoods of conditionally independent observations and gives the same result as one combined update.

Q14. In decision theory, the optimal action minimises
A) posterior expected loss
B) the prior variance
C) the p-value
D) the likelihood
* A
Why: Average the loss over the posterior for each action and choose the smallest. This is how Bayesian inference connects to decisions under uncertainty.

Q15. Naive Bayes is "naive" because it
A) ignores the likelihood
B) requires numeric features only
C) assumes features are conditionally independent given the class
D) uses no priors
* C
Why: The independence assumption is usually false, yet the classifier often works well because classification needs only the ranking of posterior probabilities.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain the roles of the prior, likelihood and posterior in a Bayesian analysis.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The prior encodes belief before the data; the likelihood measures how well each parameter value
explains the observed data; the posterior combines them via Bayes' theorem and is the complete
description of belief after observing. All inference — estimates, intervals, predictions — is
derived from the posterior.
</details>

**S2.** What does conjugacy buy you, and what is its cost?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Conjugacy gives a closed-form posterior from the same family as the prior, so updating is
arithmetic and interpretation is easy. The cost is flexibility: real prior knowledge may not fit
the conjugate family, and the choice can distort beliefs.
</details>

**S3.** Why is a credible interval easier to explain than a confidence interval?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A credible interval directly states the posterior probability that the parameter lies in the
range, which is what most people intuitively want. A confidence interval's guarantee refers to
repeated sampling procedures, not to the particular interval computed.
</details>

**S4.** How does a Bayesian analysis handle multiple comparisons without a correction?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because all parameters are estimated jointly in one posterior, the joint distribution already
accounts for the multiplicity: statements about the maximum effect or about which parameters
exceed a threshold are derived from that joint posterior. Shrinkage from hierarchical priors
further reduces spurious extremes.
</details>

**S5.** What is the difference between the posterior mean and the MLE?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The MLE maximises the likelihood and depends only on the data. The posterior mean averages the
parameter over the posterior, which incorporates the prior and typically shrinks the estimate
toward the prior mean, especially with small samples.
</details>

**S6.** Why can a flat prior still be informative?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Flatness is not invariant under reparameterisation: a uniform prior on a probability is not
uniform on the log-odds, and a uniform prior on a variance is not uniform on the standard
deviation. The implied prior on a transformed quantity may concentrate mass in surprising places.
</details>

**S7.** What does a Bayes factor of 5 mean, and what does it not mean?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The data are about five times more likely under the alternative than the null — moderate evidence.
It is not the posterior probability of the alternative, and it depends on the prior distributions
specified under each model.
</details>

**S8.** How do hierarchical priors produce shrinkage, and when is that desirable?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Group effects are modelled as draws from a common distribution, so a group with little data is
pulled toward the overall mean. This reduces variance and improves prediction when groups share
structure, but over-shrinks genuine outliers if the hierarchy is misspecified.
</details>

**S9.** Why do Bayesian methods need calibration checks in applied Data Science?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The posterior is only as good as the model and prior. Posterior predictive checks, calibration
curves and coverage of credible intervals on held-out data reveal misspecification; a
self-consistent posterior can still be confidently wrong.
</details>

**S10.** Give a Data Science situation where a Bayesian approach is clearly preferable, and say
why.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Early-stage A/B testing with small samples: a posterior over the lift gives a direct probability
that the variant is better and supports a decision rule based on expected loss, while sequential
monitoring is coherent. Frequentist sequential testing needs alpha-spending adjustments to remain
valid.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** Prior \(\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)\), 7 successes in 10 trials. Give the posterior parameters and
its mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Posterior \(\mathrm{Beta}(2+7,\,2+3)=\mathrm{Beta}(9,5)\); mean \(=9/14=0.643\). The MLE is
0.70, so the prior pulled the estimate slightly toward 0.5.
</details>

**N2.** Using N1, compute the posterior mode (MAP) and the posterior predictive probability of
success on a new trial.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
MAP \(=(\alpha-1)/(\alpha+\beta-2)=8/12=0.667\). Predictive
\(=(\alpha+k)/(\alpha+\beta+n)=9/14=0.643\), equal to the posterior mean for a Bernoulli
model.
</details>

**N3.** A Beta(1,1) prior is updated with 3 successes and 9 failures. Give the posterior and the
posterior mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Posterior \(\mathrm{Beta}(4,10)\); mean \(4/14=0.286\). With a uniform prior the posterior
mean equals the MLE \(3/12=0.25\) only in the limit; here the prior's two pseudo-observations
shift it.
</details>

**N4.** Prior odds of a hypothesis are 1:4; the Bayes factor is 6. Compute the posterior odds and
probability.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Posterior odds \(=6\times1/4=1.5\), so probability \(=1.5/2.5=0.60\). Evidence has moved
belief from 20 % to 60 %.
</details>

**N5.** A normal prior \(N(0,1)\) and likelihood \(N(x;2,1)\) for the mean (single observation).
Compute the posterior mean and variance.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Precision form: prior precision 1, likelihood precision 1, so posterior variance
\(=1/(1+1)=0.5\); mean \(=0.5(0+2)=1.0\). The posterior sits halfway between prior and
data, as equal precisions imply.
</details>

**N6.** Using N5, compute a 95 % credible interval (normal approximation).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Posterior \(N(1,0.5)\), SD \(=0.707\); interval \(1\pm1.96(0.707)=(-0.386,2.386)\), i.e.
**−0.39 to 2.39**. The interval is wide because there is a single observation.
</details>

**N7.** With a Beta(3,7) posterior, compute the prior-implied mean and the effect of adding 10
successes and 10 failures.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Initial mean \(=3/10=0.30\). After adding: \(\mathrm{Beta}(13,17)\), mean
\(13/30=0.433\), moving toward 0.5 as data dominate the prior.
</details>

**N8.** A model has marginal likelihood \(2\times10^{-5}\) and a simpler model
\(5\times10^{-6}\). Compute the Bayes factor in favour of the complex model and interpret.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(BF=2\times10^{-5}/5\times10^{-6}=4.0\). The data are four times more likely under the complex
model — weak-to-moderate evidence, and the complexity penalty is already embedded in the marginal
likelihood.
</details>

**N9.** Posterior expected losses are 0.2 for action A and 0.5 for action B. Which action is
optimal, and what is the expected loss of choosing wrongly?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Choose **A** (0.2 < 0.5). The regret of choosing B is \(0.5-0.2=0.3\). Decision theory
compares posterior expected losses, not p-values.
</details>

**N10.** A spam classifier has prior \(P(\text{spam})=0.3\), likelihood ratio 10 for the word
"offer". Compute the posterior probability of spam.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Prior odds \(=0.3/0.7=0.4286\); posterior odds \(=4.286\); probability
\(=4.286/5.286=0.811\). The word raises belief from 30 % to 81 %.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A startup tests a new checkout with 200 users per arm and sees 9 versus 14 conversions.
Explain how a Bayesian analysis changes the decision compared with a p-value.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
A Beta(1,1) prior per arm gives posteriors Beta(10,192) and Beta(15,187). The posterior
probability that B is better is about 0.85, and the expected lift is modest with a wide interval.
A p-value near 0.29 says the data are compatible with no difference; the posterior says B is
probably better but the evidence is weak — both support "keep testing", while the posterior
quantifies the chance of being wrong.
</details>

**P2.** A company wants per-city conversion estimates, but small cities have very few users.
Recommend a modelling approach.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
A hierarchical Beta-Binomial model: city rates drawn from a common distribution with estimated
spread. Small cities shrink toward the overall rate, large cities keep their own signal. Report
posterior intervals and check calibration on held-out cities.
</details>

**P3.** A regulator asks for the probability that a new drug's effect exceeds a clinically
meaningful threshold. Which framework answers directly, and how?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
A Bayesian analysis: compute \(P(\text{effect}>threshold\mid\text{data})\) from the posterior, or
the posterior expected loss of approving. A frequentist analysis answers a different question
(compatibility with a null), and converting a p-value into this probability requires a prior.
</details>

**P4.** An analyst reports a 95 % credible interval that is identical to the frequentist
confidence interval. Is that suspicious?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
No — with a flat or weakly informative prior and a normal approximation, the two often coincide
numerically. The interpretations still differ: coverage over repeated samples versus posterior
probability. Coincidence is expected in large samples with little prior information.
</details>

**P5.** A fraud model must decide whether to block a transaction. Sketch the decision rule.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Estimate the posterior probability of fraud, then block when
\(p\cdot c_{\text{miss}}>(1-p)\cdot c_{\text{false block}}\), i.e. when the posterior expected
loss of allowing exceeds that of blocking. Calibrate probabilities, set thresholds from costs, and
review the error rates at the chosen operating point.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive the Beta–Binomial posterior.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Prior \(\pi(\theta)\propto\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}\); likelihood for \(k\)
successes in \(n\) trials \(\propto\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\). Multiplying gives
\(\theta^{\alpha+k-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+n-k-1}\), which is
\(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+k,\beta+n-k)\). The normalising constant is the ratio of beta functions,
so the posterior is available in closed form.
</details>

**C2.** Show that the posterior mean under a normal prior and normal likelihood is a
precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the observation.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
With prior \(N(\mu_0,\tau^2)\) and observation \(x\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)\), the posterior
precision is \(1/\tau^2+1/\sigma^2\). The posterior mean is
\(\frac{\mu_0/\tau^2+x/\sigma^2}{1/\tau^2+1/\sigma^2}\), a weighted average with weights
proportional to precisions. As \(\tau^2\to\infty\) (vague prior) the mean tends to \(x\); as
\(\sigma^2\to\infty\) it tends to \(\mu_0\).
</details>

**C3.** Explain why a Bayes factor penalises complexity automatically, using marginal likelihoods.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
The marginal likelihood \(p(x\mid M)=\int p(x\mid\theta,M)p(\theta\mid M)d\theta\) averages the
likelihood over the prior. A complex model spreads prior mass over many possible datasets, so it
predicts each observed dataset less strongly unless the data really require that flexibility;
a simple model concentrates its predictions. The ratio therefore balances fit against spread of
predictions — an automatic Occam factor.
</details>
