## A. Problem statement

A team has deployed a churn classifier that outputs a probability for each of 500
customers. The model's AUC is a respectable 0.78, and the team plans to act on the
probabilities directly: offer discounts to everyone above 0.7, and treat the score as the
expected churn rate in each segment. Before rollout, the analytics lead asks whether those
probabilities mean what they say, and where the model is most overconfident.

## B. Background and context

Discrimination and calibration are different properties. A model can rank customers
perfectly (AUC = 1) while predicting 0.9 for everyone who churns — useless for expected-value
calculations, budgeting or threshold setting. Machine-learning practice often reports AUC and
accuracy and stops; this case shows why calibration (reliability) is the property that makes
probabilities usable, and how to measure it with the Brier score, log loss and a reliability
diagram. It also shows how segment-level miscalibration hides inside a good overall score.

## C. Dataset description

`model_predictions.csv` has 500 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `case_id` | integer | 1–500 |
| `segment` | text | `new` (204) or `returning` (296) |
| `y_true` | integer | Observed outcome (churn 1/0) |
| `predicted_prob` | float | Model's predicted probability |

**Generation method.** A latent log-odds is drawn normal(0, 1.1) with a +0.3 shift for
returning customers. The true outcome is Bernoulli with the logistic of that latent value.
The model's predicted probability is the logistic of **1.4 times** the latent log-odds plus
noise — i.e. the model exaggerates confidence by 40 %, which is how real overfit or
over-regularised models behave. The realised outcome rate is 47.8 %.

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

## F. Mathematical formulation

\[
\mathrm{Brier}=\frac1n\sum_i (p_i-y_i)^2,\qquad
\mathrm{LogLoss}=-\frac1n\sum_i\left[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\right].
\]

Reliability table: for probability bin \(b\) with \(n_b\) cases, mean forecast
\(\bar p_b\) and observed frequency \(\bar y_b\). Expected calibration error:

\[
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_b \frac{n_b}{n}\left|\bar y_b-\bar p_b\right|.
\]

Calibration slope from regressing \(y\) on the logit of \(p\): a slope of 1 means calibrated; a
slope below 1 means overconfident (predictions too extreme).

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Compare the base rate with the mean predicted probability.
2. Compute AUC, Brier and log loss; compare with a base-rate forecast.
3. Bin the predictions and build the reliability table with counts.
4. Compute ECE and plot the calibration curve.
5. Regress the outcome on the logit of the prediction to estimate the calibration slope.
6. Repeat the reliability analysis by segment.
7. Evaluate the 0.7 threshold: how many flagged, what realised churn rate.
8. Recommend recalibration and a reporting standard.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, brier_score_loss, log_loss
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

pred = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/model_predictions.csv")
print(pred.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print(f"cases: {len(pred)}, base rate: {pred.y_true.mean():.3f}, "
      f"mean prediction: {pred.predicted_prob.mean():.3f}")

print(f"AUC = {roc_auc_score(pred.y_true, pred.predicted_prob):.3f}")
print(f"Brier = {brier_score_loss(pred.y_true, pred.predicted_prob):.4f} "
      f"(base-rate Brier = {brier_score_loss(pred.y_true, np.full(len(pred), pred.y_true.mean())):.4f})")
print(f"log loss = {log_loss(pred.y_true, pred.predicted_prob):.4f}")

pred["bin"] = pd.cut(pred.predicted_prob, np.arange(0, 1.01, 0.2))
reliability = pred.groupby("bin", observed=True).agg(
    n=("y_true", "size"),
    mean_pred=("predicted_prob", "mean"),
    observed=("y_true", "mean"),
).round(3)
print("\nreliability table:")
print(reliability.to_string())

ece = ((reliability.observed - reliability.mean_pred).abs() * reliability.n).sum() / len(pred)
print(f"\nECE = {ece:.4f}")

pred["logit_p"] = np.log(pred.predicted_prob / (1 - pred.predicted_prob))
slope = smf.logit("y_true ~ logit_p", data=pred).fit(disp=0)
print(f"calibration slope = {slope.params.logit_p:.3f} "
      f"(95% CI {slope.conf_int().loc['logit_p'].round(3).tolist()}); "
      "a slope < 1 means overconfidence")

print("\nby segment:")
print(pred.groupby("segment").agg(
    n=("y_true", "size"), mean_pred=("predicted_prob", "mean"),
    observed=("y_true", "mean"), brier=("predicted_prob", "mean")).round(3).to_string())

flagged = pred.predicted_prob > 0.7
print(f"\nthreshold 0.7: {int(flagged.sum())} flagged, "
      f"realised churn {pred.loc[flagged, 'y_true'].mean():.3f}, "
      f"mean predicted {pred.loc[flagged, 'predicted_prob'].mean():.3f}")
print(f"accuracy at 0.5: {((pred.predicted_prob > 0.5) == pred.y_true).mean():.3f}")
```

**How to read it.** The reliability points lie above the diagonal at low probabilities and
below it at high probabilities: the model says 0.89 for the top bin but only 0.78 churn. That
is the signature of overconfidence — predictions pushed away from 0.5. The right-hand panel
shows why AUC still looks good: the two distributions are shifted relative to each other,
so ranking works even though the absolute levels are wrong.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Base rate 47.8 %, mean prediction 51.8 %: the model is slightly high overall.
- AUC 0.784 — good discrimination.
- Brier 0.1927 against a base-rate Brier of about 0.2495; log loss 0.575.
- Reliability: predicted 0.108 → observed 0.170; 0.297 → 0.267; 0.513 → 0.436; 0.709 →
  0.676; 0.893 → 0.776.
- ECE 0.064, and the miscalibration is concentrated in the extremes.
- Calibration slope 0.734 (95 % CI 0.585–0.883), confirming overconfidence: predictions
  should be shrunk toward 0.5.
- Segment calibration is similar (new: 0.442 predicted vs 0.431 observed; returning: 0.570
  vs 0.510), so the problem is global rather than segment-specific.
- At threshold 0.7, 175 customers are flagged with a realised churn rate of 0.771 against a
  mean predicted 0.845 — the model overstates their risk, so discount costs are overestimated.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — base rate and mean.** 47.8 % churned, and the mean prediction is 51.8 %. A model
that is calibrated should have these equal; a 4-point gap means the model's probabilities are
shifted upward overall.

**Step 2 — proper scores.** Brier 0.1927 versus 0.2495 for always predicting the base rate:
the model adds real information (a 23 % reduction in squared error). Log loss 0.575 versus
0.693 for a coin flip, again a clear gain. Both scores reward calibration and resolution
together, which is why they are preferred to accuracy.

**Step 3 — reliability.** The five bins show the pattern: at the bottom the model
under-predicts (0.108 vs 0.170), at the top it over-predicts (0.893 vs 0.776). That is
exactly what putting a 1.4 multiplier on the log-odds does — it stretches the distribution
away from 0.5. ECE of 0.064 means the average absolute gap between forecast and reality is
6.4 percentage points, which is large enough to matter for budgeting.

**Step 4 — calibration slope.** Regressing the outcome on the predicted logit gives a slope
of 0.734 (95 % CI 0.585–0.883): to calibrate, the log-odds should be shrunk by multiplying
by roughly 0.73 (equivalently, refitting on held-out data, or applying isotonic regression). A slope of 1
with intercept 0 is the definition of perfect calibration.

**Step 5 — segment check.** New and returning customers are miscalibrated in the same
direction and by similar amounts, so a single global recalibration (Platt scaling or isotonic
regression) is appropriate. Had one segment been calibrated and the other not, a
segment-specific correction would be needed — and the difference itself would be worth
investigating.

**Step 6 — the threshold decision.** At 0.7, 175 customers are flagged. The model predicts
their mean probability at 0.845, but only 77 % actually churn. A retention offer costing 20
units per customer and saving 200 units of revenue per retained churner still looks
profitable at 77 %, but the margin is much thinner than the raw probabilities suggest — and
if the offer also recruits non-churners, the arithmetic can flip. This is the practical
cost of miscalibration: the decision rule is only as good as the probability it consumes.

**Step 7 — reporting standard.** Report AUC **and** Brier/log loss **and** a reliability
diagram, plus the calibration slope with an interval. Accuracy alone is the weakest of the
four because it discards the probability and depends on an arbitrary threshold. For any
model whose output feeds a financial calculation, calibration is not optional.

**Limitations.** These predictions come from a single sample of 500; the reliability bins
contain 78–116 cases each, so the observed frequencies carry ±4–6 pp of sampling error. A
proper calibration assessment uses a held-out set and reports intervals, and would test
whether the recalibration generalises across time and segments.
