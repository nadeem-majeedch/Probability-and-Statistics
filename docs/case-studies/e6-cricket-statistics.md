## A. Problem statement

A cricket analyst has twenty innings from each of six batters and must select a squad.
The coach wants more than a batting average: how consistent is each player, how reliable
is the average given only twenty innings, and does the team's home ground help or hinder
scoring? The analyst also suspects that runs per innings are far more variable than a
Poisson model would suggest, which matters for predicting collapse risk.

## B. Background and context

Cricket is a natural laboratory for statistics because the sport is a sequence of
discrete, low-scoring events with high variance: a batter can be out for a duck or make a
century. Batting averages are sample means with substantial uncertainty, and the
difference between two averages of 36 and 27 may be noise. Sports analytics is also where
students most easily fall into the "hot hand" and "small sample" traps, so this case
combines honest descriptive work with confidence intervals and a check of the Poisson
assumption that is often used for count data in sport.

## C. Dataset description

`cricket_innings.csv` has 120 rows: six batters × twenty innings.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `batter` | text | Six invented players |
| `innings_no` | integer | 1–20 |
| `runs` | integer | Runs scored |
| `balls_faced` | integer | Balls faced |
| `dismissed` | text | `yes` / `no` |
| `venue` | text | `home` / `away` |

**Generation method.** Each batter has a scoring rate drawn per innings from a negative
binomial with mean \(r\) (36, 28, 22, 18, 12, 8) and variance \(1.6r\), so the data are
over-dispersed relative to Poisson by design. Balls faced are runs divided by a strike
rate drawn uniformly from 0.8 to 1.6, clipped at one ball minimum. Venue is a fair coin
flip, independent of runs, which makes the home-ground comparison a deliberate null.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. Summarise runs per innings for each batter: mean, median, standard deviation, range.
2. How precise are the batting averages? Give a 95 % confidence interval for each.
3. Is the difference between the top two batters statistically meaningful with 20 innings?
4. Are runs over-dispersed relative to a Poisson model? What does that mean for collapse
   risk?
5. Do batters score more at home?
6. Compute strike rates and decide whether a high strike rate compensates for a low
   average.

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Mean, median, standard deviation for skewed count data.
- Standard error of the mean and the t-based confidence interval.
- Two-sample comparison with unequal variances.
- Poisson distribution: mean equals variance, additivity, rare events.
- Over-dispersion and the variance-to-mean ratio.
- The effect of sample size on the width of an interval.
- Multiple comparisons when six players are ranked.

## F. Mathematical formulation

Sample mean and standard error:

\[
\bar{r}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i,\qquad
\mathrm{SE}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad
\bar{r}\pm t_{0.975,\,n-1}\,\mathrm{SE}.
\]

Variance-to-mean ratio (index of dispersion) for counts:

\[
\mathrm{VMR}=\frac{s^2}{\bar{r}};\qquad
\mathrm{VMR}\approx 1 \text{ under Poisson, } \mathrm{VMR}>1 \text{ over-dispersed.}
\]

A dispersion test uses
\(X^2=\sum_i (r_i-\bar r)^2/\bar r \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\) under the Poisson model.

Strike rate: \(\mathrm{SR}=100\times\text{runs}/\text{balls faced}\).

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Load the file; check row counts per batter (should be 20 each).
2. Summarise runs by batter; note the skew and the distribution of scores.
3. Compute 95 % t-intervals for each batter's mean.
4. Compare the top two batters with Welch's test and with the overlap of intervals.
5. Compute the variance-to-mean ratio per batter and the dispersion test.
6. Compare home and away runs (paired by innings order is not meaningful — use a
   two-sample comparison and say why).
7. Compute strike rate; plot runs against strike rate.
8. Recommend a squad with an explicit uncertainty statement.

## H. Student tasks

1. Build the summary table for runs by batter.
2. Give 95 % intervals for two chosen batters. Which intervals overlap?
3. Run the dispersion test for the squad as a whole and for the top scorer.
4. Is the over-dispersion large enough to matter for predicting a low score?
5. Test the home-versus-away difference; report the CI and interpret a null result.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

batting = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/cricket_innings.csv")
print(batting.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("rows:", len(batting), "| innings per batter:",
      batting.groupby("batter").size().unique().tolist())

summary = batting.groupby("batter")["runs"].agg(
    innings="size", mean="mean", median="median", sd="std",
    best="max", total="sum").round(2).sort_values("mean", ascending=False)
print("\nbatting summary:")
print(summary.to_string())

print("\n95% confidence intervals for mean runs:")
for batter, group in batting.groupby("batter"):
    runs = group["runs"]
    lo, hi = stats.t.interval(0.95, len(runs) - 1, runs.mean(), stats.sem(runs))
    print(f"  {batter:8s} mean={runs.mean():6.2f}  95% CI [{lo:5.2f}, {hi:5.2f}]")

top, second = summary.index[0], summary.index[1]
a = batting.loc[batting.batter == top, "runs"]
b = batting.loc[batting.batter == second, "runs"]
print(f"\n{top} vs {second}: Welch t = {stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False).statistic:.2f}, "
      f"p = {stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False).pvalue:.3f}")

for batter, group in batting.groupby("batter"):
    runs = group["runs"]
    chi2 = ((runs - runs.mean()) ** 2).sum() / runs.mean()
    p = stats.chi2.sf(chi2, df=len(runs) - 1)
    print(f"{batter:8s} VMR={runs.var(ddof=1) / runs.mean():5.2f}  "
          f"dispersion chi2={chi2:6.1f}  p={p:.3f}")

print(f"\noverall VMR = {batting.runs.var(ddof=1) / batting.runs.mean():.2f}")
home = batting.loc[batting.venue == "home", "runs"]
away = batting.loc[batting.venue == "away", "runs"]
print(f"home mean {home.mean():.2f} (n={len(home)}) vs away {away.mean():.2f} (n={len(away)}), "
      f"t-test p = {stats.ttest_ind(home, away, equal_var=False).pvalue:.3f}")

batting["strike_rate"] = 100 * batting["runs"] / batting["balls_faced"]
print("\nmean strike rate by batter:")
print(batting.groupby("batter")["strike_rate"].mean().round(1).sort_values(ascending=False).to_string())
```

**How to read it.** The box plot shows six clearly separated medians, but the boxes are
wide: the best batter's interquartile range still spans about 15 runs, and every batter
has a low score. The histogram is right-skewed with a long tail to 55, not the symmetric
bell shape that a naive analyst might assume. The distribution of scores looks closer to a
geometric/negative-binomial shape, which is why the mean alone is a poor description.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Means run from 36.75 (A. Khan) down to 7.70 (F. Noor); standard deviations are 3.3 to
  8.4 and rise with the mean.
- A 95 % interval for A. Khan is roughly 32.8–40.7; for B. Ali roughly 24.1–30.6. They do
  not overlap, but the margin is thinner than the raw averages suggest.
- The squad's variance-to-mean ratio is 6.45, far above 1: runs are heavily
  over-dispersed, so Poisson-based "probability of scoring under 10" calculations are
  wrong.
- Home and away means are essentially identical (20.59 vs 20.32, p ≈ 0.9): no evidence of
  a home advantage in this dataset.
- Batter mean strike rates run from about 114 to 130 on a scale whose individual innings
  span 80 to 167; the highest average and the highest strike rate are not the same player.
- Rankings based on twenty innings carry wide intervals; a selection decision should use
  the intervals, not the point estimates alone.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — summary.** A. Khan: 36.75 mean, 8.37 SD, best 55, 735 runs. B. Ali: 27.35,
6.95. C. Raza: 21.65, 5.82. D. Iqbal: 19.55, 5.27. E. Shah: 9.75, 3.32. F. Noor: 7.70,
3.36. The standard deviation tracks the mean because the generating process has variance
proportional to the mean — a signature of over-dispersed counts.

**Step 2 — precision.** The standard error for A. Khan is 8.37/√20 = 1.87, so the 95 %
interval is 36.75 ± 2.09 × 1.87 = [32.8, 40.7]. For B. Ali: 6.95/√20 = 1.55, interval
[24.1, 30.6]. The intervals do not overlap, but the gap between the lower bound of the
best and the upper bound of the second is under three runs. With 20 innings, a difference
of five runs in average is at the edge of what the data can resolve.

**Step 3 — the test.** Welch's t for A. Khan versus B. Ali is 3.86 with p ≈ 0.0004, so
the difference is statistically detectable. The honest statement is "A. Khan is
probably the better scorer, though the advantage could be as small as two runs per
innings".

**Step 4 — over-dispersion.** For the pooled data, mean 20.46 and variance 131.9 give
VMR = 6.45. Per batter the ratios run from 1.13 (E. Shah) to 1.91 (A. Khan), and the
dispersion chi-square rejects Poisson for every batter. Interpretation:
runs are clustered — good days and bad days — so the Poisson assumption of independent,
equally likely scoring events fails. Practically, a Poisson model would badly understate
the chance of a very low score and of a very high one.

**Step 5 — home advantage.** Home 20.59 versus away 20.32, t-test p ≈ 0.9. No evidence.
The generator made venue independent of runs, so this is the correct answer. Reporting it
as "no detectable home advantage in 120 innings" is more useful than a story about
familiar conditions; the confidence interval for the difference is roughly −4 to +3.5
runs, so a small effect cannot be excluded.

**Step 6 — strike rate.** Strike rates average 123.5 across innings, ranging from 80 to
167, with batter means between 114.0 and 130.0.
The player with the highest average is not necessarily the fastest scorer: a high strike
rate with a modest average can be more valuable in a limited-overs setting, while a high
average with a slow strike rate suits a longer format. The correct comparison depends on
the format and on the team's situation, which is a decision-theory question rather than a
statistical one.

**Selection memo (model answer).** "On twenty innings each, A. Khan (36.8 ± 4) and
B. Ali (27.4 ± 3) are the two strongest candidates and their intervals do not overlap.
C. Raza and D. Iqbal are statistically indistinguishable at this sample size. E. Shah and
F. Noor are well below the others. Because scores are over-dispersed (VMR ≈ 6), expect
frequent low scores from every player; selection should weigh the ability to bat through
an innings, not just the average. Home and away records show no difference, so venue
should not enter the decision."

**Warnings.** (i) Ranking players by average and ignoring intervals is the most common
error in sports analytics. (ii) Fitting a Poisson model to runs because they are counts
would be a modelling mistake; check the dispersion first. (iii) Twenty innings is a small
sample: the intervals, not the point estimates, carry the information.
