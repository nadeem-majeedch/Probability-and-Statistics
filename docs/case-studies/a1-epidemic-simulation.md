## A. Problem statement

A public-health modelling unit runs 200 stochastic epidemic simulations on a population of
100,000, varying the transmission rate β and the recovery rate γ. Each run records the
basic reproduction number \(R_0=\beta/\gamma\), the peak number infected, the total ever
infected and the duration. The unit must describe the uncertainty in the outbreak size,
quantify how strongly \(R_0\) controls the peak, and explain why a single "expected
outbreak" figure is misleading for planning.

## B. Background and context

The SIR model is the simplest compartmental description of an epidemic: susceptible,
infectious and recovered populations, with transmission proportional to \(S\cdot I\) and
recovery at a constant per-capita rate. Its deterministic version is a pair of differential
equations; its stochastic version adds the randomness of individual contact and recovery
events. Planners care about the distribution of outcomes, not the mean, because hospital
capacity is breached by the bad tail. Simulating many parameter sets and summarising the
distribution is exactly what a data scientist does with any stochastic model, and the case
is an introduction to simulation-based uncertainty analysis.

## C. Dataset description

`epidemic_runs.csv` has 200 rows, one per simulation.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `run` | integer | 1–200 |
| `beta` | float | Transmission rate, uniform 0.25–0.55 per day |
| `gamma` | float | Recovery rate, uniform 0.10–0.20 per day |
| `r0` | float | \(\beta/\gamma\), ranging 1.32–5.22 |
| `peak_infected` | integer | Maximum simultaneously infectious |
| `total_infected` | integer | Cumulative infections at the end |
| `duration_days` | integer | Days until fewer than one infectious person remains |

**Generation method.** Each run draws β and γ uniformly in the ranges above, starts with
50 infectious people in a population of 100,000, and iterates a discrete-time SIR update:
new infections \(=\beta SI/N\) per day, recoveries \(=\gamma I\) per day. The simulation stops
when \(I<1\) or after 400 days. Because \(R_0\) varies across runs, the dataset is a sample
from the *model's* output distribution, conditional on uniform priors for the parameters.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. Describe the distribution of \(R_0\), peak infections and total infections.
2. How large is the variability in peak infections, and what are the 5th and 95th
   percentiles?
3. How strongly does \(R_0\) predict the peak? Fit a regression and report \(R^2\).
4. What share of simulated outbreaks peak above 10,000 infectious people?
5. What is the mean attack rate (total infected / population)?
6. How long do outbreaks last, and how variable is the duration?
7. What would you tell a hospital planning for the 95th percentile rather than the mean?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Deterministic versus stochastic models.
- Simulation-based estimation of a distribution.
- Expectation and variance of a nonlinear function of parameters.
- Simple linear regression and \(R^2\) as a description of a simulated relationship.
- Quantiles and tail risk.
- Sensitivity analysis: how output varies with inputs.
- Monte Carlo error and the effect of the number of runs.

## F. Mathematical formulation

The deterministic SIR system is

\[
\frac{dS}{dt}=-\frac{\beta SI}{N},\qquad
\frac{dI}{dt}=\frac{\beta SI}{N}-\gamma I,\qquad
\frac{dR}{dt}=\gamma I,
\]

with \(R_0=\beta/\gamma\). The final-size relation (implicit) is

\[
\ln\frac{S_\infty}{S_0}=R_0\left(\frac{S_\infty}{N}-1\right),
\]

so the attack rate \(1-S_\infty/N\) increases with \(R_0\).

The stochastic simulation replaces the flow terms with random draws; here the discrete
update is

\[
S_{t+1}=S_t-\mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(\frac{\beta S_t I_t}{N}\right),\qquad
I_{t+1}=I_t+\text{new infections}-\mathrm{Poisson}(\gamma I_t).
\]

Regression of peak on \(R_0\):

\[
\text{peak}_i=a+bR_{0,i}+\varepsilon_i,\qquad
R^2=1-\frac{\sum \hat\varepsilon_i^2}{\sum(\text{peak}_i-\overline{\text{peak}})^2}.
\]

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Summarise the inputs (\(\beta\), \(\gamma\), \(R_0\)) to confirm the simulation design.
2. Summarise the outputs: mean, SD, median, 5th and 95th percentiles of peak, total and
   duration.
3. Plot the distribution of peak infections; identify the tail.
4. Regress peak on \(R_0\); report slope, \(R^2\) and the residual SD.
5. Compute the attack rate distribution and its quantiles.
6. Compute the probability that a run exceeds a capacity threshold.
7. Estimate Monte Carlo error for the mean peak (SD/√200) and discuss whether 200 runs
   suffice.
8. Translate the results into a planning recommendation.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

runs = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/epidemic_runs.csv")
print(runs.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("runs:", len(runs))

for column in ("r0", "peak_infected", "total_infected", "duration_days"):
    q = np.percentile(runs[column], [5, 50, 95])
    print(f"{column:16s} mean={runs[column].mean():10.1f} sd={runs[column].std(ddof=1):9.1f} "
          f"p5={q[0]:8.1f} p50={q[1]:8.1f} p95={q[2]:8.1f}")

print("\nattack rate: mean {:.3f}, p5 {:.3f}, p95 {:.3f}".format(
    (runs.total_infected / 100_000).mean(),
    np.percentile(runs.total_infected / 100_000, 5),
    np.percentile(runs.total_infected / 100_000, 95)))

print(f"\nP(R0 > 2) = {(runs.r0 > 2).mean():.3f}")
print(f"P(peak > 10,000) = {(runs.peak_infected > 10_000).mean():.3f}")
print(f"P(peak > 20,000) = {(runs.peak_infected > 20_000).mean():.3f}")

model = smf.ols("peak_infected ~ r0", data=runs).fit()
print(f"\npeak ~ R0: slope = {model.params.r0:,.0f}, R2 = {model.rsquared:.3f}, "
      f"residual SD = {np.sqrt(model.mse_resid):,.0f}")
print(f"correlation = {np.corrcoef(runs.r0, runs.peak_infected)[0, 1]:.3f}")

mean_peak = runs.peak_infected.mean()
se_peak = runs.peak_infected.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(runs))
print(f"\nmean peak {mean_peak:,.0f} with 95% Monte Carlo CI "
      f"[{mean_peak - 1.96 * se_peak:,.0f}, {mean_peak + 1.96 * se_peak:,.0f}]")

capacity = 25_000
print(f"probability of exceeding {capacity:,} beds: "
      f"{(runs.peak_infected > capacity).mean():.3f}")
```

**How to read it.** The peak distribution is right-skewed with a long upper tail: most runs
peak between 15,000 and 35,000, but the top decile reaches 45,000. The scatter against \(R_0\)
is almost deterministic — the points lie on a tight curve — because \(R_0\) is the dominant
driver in a homogeneous SIR model. That tightness is a property of the model, not evidence
that real epidemics are predictable.

## K. Expected conclusions

- \(R_0\) ranges 1.32–5.22 with mean 2.81; 79.5 % of runs exceed \(R_0=2\).
- Peak infections: mean 27,451, SD 11,889, p5 8,769, median 27,672, p95 45,477.
- Total infected averages 88.5 % of the population (attack rate), so most simulated
  outbreaks burn through nearly everyone.
- Peak is almost a linear function of \(R_0\): slope ≈ 13,451 per unit, \(R^2=0.974\), residual
  SD ≈ 1,900.
- Duration averages 139 days (p5 98, p95 188).
- 90.5 % of runs exceed a 10,000-bed threshold and 59 % exceed 25,000, so the mean is a
  poor planning figure: the 95th percentile is 64 % higher than the median.
- The 95 % Monte Carlo interval for the mean peak is roughly ±1,650 — small relative to the
  spread, so 200 runs suffice to estimate the mean, but not the extreme tail.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — the design.** β ∈ [0.25, 0.55], γ ∈ [0.10, 0.20], so \(R_0\) spans a factor of
four. This is a sensitivity experiment, not a forecast: the uniform priors are arbitrary
and a real analysis would calibrate them to contact-survey data.

**Step 2 — the output distribution.** Mean peak 27,451 with SD 11,889, i.e. a coefficient of
variation of 0.43. The median (27,672) is close to the mean, but the p95 is 45,477, 66 %
above the median. For capacity planning the relevant number is the upper tail, because a
hospital that sizes for the mean is overrun in half of the scenarios by construction.

**Step 3 — attack rate.** Total infected averages 88.5 % of 100,000. This is the final-size
behaviour of SIR at these \(R_0\) values: even at \(R_0 = 1.3\) the model infects a large share
because the epidemic runs until susceptibles are exhausted. A real population has
heterogeneity and interventions, which is exactly what the model omits.

**Step 4 — the regression.** Peak = 13,451·\(R_0\) + intercept, \(R^2 = 0.974\), residual SD
1,900. A one-unit increase in \(R_0\) adds about 13,500 to the peak — enormous. The near-perfect
fit is a warning as much as a result: when a single parameter explains 97 % of the variance,
the model's structure, not the data, is doing the work. Adding stochastic contact
heterogeneity would break the tight relationship, which is why real epidemic forecasts have
wide intervals.

**Step 5 — Monte Carlo error.** The standard error of the mean peak is
11,889/√200 = 841, so the 95 % interval is 27,451 ± 1,649. That is adequate for the mean but
useless for the 99th percentile, which depends on a handful of extreme runs. Estimating tail
quantiles reliably needs hundreds or thousands more simulations — a general rule: the tail
converges far more slowly than the centre.

**Step 6 — planning translation.** If the region has 25,000 beds and the model says the peak
exceeds that in 59 % of runs, the plan must either (i) target \(R_0\) reduction — since
peak ≈ 13,451·\(R_0\), cutting \(R_0\) from 3 to 2 saves about 13,500 beds — or (ii) build
surge capacity for the p95. Both are model-based statements, contingent on the model being
right; the honest framing is "under these assumptions, in this many simulations".

**Step 7 — what the model ignores.** Homogeneous mixing (real contacts are clustered),
age structure, asymptomatic transmission, behavioural change as deaths rise, reporting
delay, and waning immunity. Each would change the peak and its uncertainty, mostly by
widening the distribution. The exercise is therefore about *reasoning with a stochastic
model*, not about predicting any real outbreak.

**A note on language.** Reports of this kind should say "in 200 simulations of an SIR model
with uniform priors on β and γ", never "we predict 27,000 infections". The distinction
between simulating a model and estimating a reality is the central professional habit this
case is meant to build.
