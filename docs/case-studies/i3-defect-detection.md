## A. Problem statement

A plant inspects samples from 40 production batches and counts defectives. Quality
management wants to know whether the night shift is worse than the day shift, whether the
process is drifting, and how much protection a 200-unit sample gives against accepting a
bad lot. They also want a control chart they can keep using next month.

## B. Background and context

Defect counts are binomial: a fixed number of inspected units, each defective with some
probability. That makes this case the natural home of the binomial distribution, of
proportion tests, and of the p-chart — the control chart for fraction defective. The
second half of the case is acceptance sampling, which answers a different question: given
a sampling plan (inspect \(n\), reject if defectives exceed \(c\)), what is the probability of
accepting a lot at each true defect rate? This is the operating-characteristic curve, and
it is how suppliers and customers agree on inspection.

## C. Dataset description

`production_batches.csv` has 40 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `batch_id` | integer | 1–40, in production order |
| `shift` | text | `day` or `night` |
| `inspected` | integer | Units inspected, 180–240 |
| `defective` | integer | Units found defective |

**Generation method.** Inspection size is uniform between 180 and 240. The true defect
rate is 0.018 on the day shift, 0.027 on the night shift, plus an extra 0.012 for batches
after the 25th (a slow tool-wear drift). Defectives are binomial given the batch's rate.
The realised totals are 191 defectives in 8,318 inspections, i.e. 2.30 %.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What is the overall defect rate, and how does it split by shift?
2. Is the night shift's higher rate statistically significant?
3. Does the defect rate drift after batch 25?
4. What are the 3-sigma control limits for a p-chart, and does any batch breach them?
5. For a plan that inspects 200 units and rejects above 8 defectives, what is the
   probability of accepting a lot with a 2 % rate? With a 5 % rate?
6. What sample size would detect a shift from 2 % to 3 % with 80 % power?
7. What should the plant do next month?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Binomial distribution: mean \(np\), variance \(np(1-p)\).
- Sampling distribution of a proportion; standard error with estimated \(p\).
- Two-proportion z-test and the chi-square equivalent.
- Control charts: centre line, 3-sigma limits, out-of-control rules.
- Acceptance sampling and the operating-characteristic curve.
- Power and sample size for proportions.
- Multiple testing across 40 batches.

## F. Mathematical formulation

Pooled defect rate and its standard error:

\[
\bar p=\frac{\sum_i d_i}{\sum_i n_i},\qquad
\mathrm{SE}(\bar p)=\sqrt{\frac{\bar p(1-\bar p)}{\bar n}}.
\]

p-chart limits for subgroup size \(n\):

\[
UCL=\bar p+3\sqrt{\frac{\bar p(1-\bar p)}{n}},\qquad
LCL=\max\!\left(0,\ \bar p-3\sqrt{\frac{\bar p(1-\bar p)}{n}}\right).
\]

Two-proportion z-test:

\[
z=\frac{\hat p_2-\hat p_1}{\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}.
\]

Acceptance probability for a plan \((n,c)\) at true rate \(p\):

\[
P(\text{accept}\mid p)=\sum_{k=0}^{c}\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.
\]

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Aggregate defectives and inspections overall and by shift.
2. Test the shift difference with a two-proportion z-test; report the CI.
3. Compare the first 25 batches with the last 15.
4. Build the p-chart with limits from all 40 batches; flag breaches and runs.
5. Compute the OC curve for the plan (n = 200, c = 8).
6. Estimate the power of the current inspection volume to detect 2 % → 3 %.
7. Recommend a monitoring rule and an inspection plan.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

batches = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/production_batches.csv")
print(batches.head(4).to_string(index=False))

total_def, total_ins = batches.defective.sum(), batches.inspected.sum()
p_bar = total_def / total_ins
print(f"\noverall: {total_def} defectives in {total_ins:,} inspections = {p_bar:.4f}")

by_shift = batches.groupby("shift")[["inspected", "defective"]].sum()
by_shift["rate"] = by_shift.defective / by_shift.inspected
print("\nby shift:")
print(by_shift.round(4).to_string())

d = by_shift.loc["day"]
n_ = by_shift.loc["night"]
se = np.sqrt(p_bar * (1 - p_bar) * (1 / d.inspected + 1 / n_.inspected))
z = (n_.rate - d.rate) / se
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))
print(f"night - day = {n_.rate - d.rate:+.4f}, z = {z:.2f}, p = {p_value:.3f}")

early = batches.loc[batches.batch_id <= 25, ["inspected", "defective"]].sum()
late = batches.loc[batches.batch_id > 25, ["inspected", "defective"]].sum()
p_early, p_late = early.defective / early.inspected, late.defective / late.inspected
se_diff = np.sqrt(p_early * (1 - p_early) / early.inspected
                  + p_late * (1 - p_late) / late.inspected)
print(f"\nbatches 1-25: {p_early:.4f}; batches 26-40: {p_late:.4f}; "
      f"difference {p_late - p_early:+.4f} (95% CI "
      f"{p_late - p_early - 1.96 * se_diff:+.4f} to {p_late - p_early + 1.96 * se_diff:+.4f})")

n_bar = batches.inspected.mean()
ucl = p_bar + 3 * np.sqrt(p_bar * (1 - p_bar) / n_bar)
lcl = max(0.0, p_bar - 3 * np.sqrt(p_bar * (1 - p_bar) / n_bar))
batches["rate"] = batches.defective / batches.inspected
breaches = batches.loc[batches.rate > ucl, "batch_id"].tolist()
print(f"\np-chart: centre {p_bar:.4f}, UCL {ucl:.4f}, LCL {lcl:.4f}, "
      f"mean n {n_bar:.0f}")
print("batches above UCL:", breaches)

print("\nacceptance probability (n=200):")
for rate in (0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.05):
    print(f"  true rate {rate:.0%}: accept P(X<=8) = {stats.binom.cdf(8, 200, rate):.3f}")

# sample size per shift comparison, 2% vs 3%, alpha 0.05 two-sided, power 0.80
from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize
effect = proportion_effectsize(0.03, 0.02)
n_needed = NormalIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=effect, alpha=0.05, power=0.8)
print(f"\ninspections per shift for 80% power (2% vs 3%): {n_needed:,.0f}")
```

**How to read it.** The p-chart oscillates around 2.3 % with limits at 0 and 5.4 %; no
batch breaches the upper limit, and the average after batch 25 is visibly but not
decisively higher. The flat lower limit is a consequence of the small expected count — a
batch with zero defectives is not evidence of improvement here. The OC curve falls from
near 1 at a 1 % rate to about 0.2 at 5 %, which is the protection the sampling plan
actually offers.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Overall defect rate 2.30 % (191 defectives in 8,318 inspections).
- Day shift 2.17 %, night shift 2.63 %; z = 1.25, p = 0.21. Not significant at 5 %, but the
  confidence interval for the difference still allows a night-shift penalty of up to
  about 1.2 pp.
- Batches 1–25 run at 1.85 %, batches 26–40 at 2.98 % — a rise of 1.1 pp that is worth
  investigating even though the 3-sigma chart does not flag it.
- p-chart limits are 0 to 5.41 %; no batch breaches them, because the subgroup of ~208
  units is too small to resolve a 1 pp shift.
- The sampling plan (n = 200, c = 8) accepts a 1 % lot with probability 1.00, a 2 % lot
  with 0.98, a 3 % lot with 0.85 and a 5 % lot with 0.33.
- About 3,800 inspections per group are needed for 80 % power against a 2 % vs 3 %
  difference — far more than one shift's output, so the comparison must be accumulated.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — overall rate.** 191/8,318 = 2.30 %. The standard error at the mean subgroup
size (208) is √(0.023 × 0.977/208) = 0.0104, so a single batch's rate has a 3-sigma range
of roughly −0.8 % to 5.4 %, truncated at zero. That width is the key limitation of this
chart.

**Step 2 — shift comparison.** Day: 130/5,996 = 2.17 %. Night: 61/2,322 = 2.63 %. The
pooled rate is 2.30 %, the standard error of the difference is
√(0.023 × 0.977 × (1/5,996 + 1/2,322)) = 0.0037, so z = 0.0046/0.0037 = 1.25 and p = 0.21.
The correct statement: "the night shift's defect rate is 0.46 pp higher, which is not
statistically distinguishable from zero with this much data." The interval runs from about
−0.26 pp to +1.19 pp, so a real penalty of up to one point cannot be excluded.

**Step 3 — drift.** First 25 batches: 96/5,098 = 1.88 %. Last 15: 95/3,220 = 2.95 %.
The difference is 1.07 pp with a 95 % interval of 0.37 to 1.76 pp — it excludes zero, so
the increase is real even though the control chart does not flag any single batch. This is the classic distinction between a shift that is visible in aggregate
and one that is large relative to a single subgroup's noise.

**Step 4 — the p-chart.** Centre 0.0230, UCL 0.0541, LCL 0. No batch exceeds the UCL. The
chart is therefore "in control" by the 3-sigma rule, and reporting "the process is stable"
would be technically correct and substantively misleading, because the process is stable
at a rate that is rising and that is above the target. A chart is a tool for detecting
special causes, not for proving the absence of drift; with 208 units per subgroup, only a
shift of about 3 pp would be visible.

**Step 5 — operating characteristic.** With n = 200 and c = 8: at p = 1 % the probability
of 8 or fewer defectives is 1.000; at 2 %, 0.980; at 3 %, 0.850; at 5 %, 0.327. So the
plan almost always accepts genuinely good lots but lets a third of 5 % lots through. The
producer's risk (rejecting a good lot) is negligible; the consumer's risk (accepting a
5 % lot) is 33 %, too high for a safety-critical part and the reason to tighten c.

**Step 6 — power and sample size.** To detect 2 % versus 3 % at 80 % power with a
two-sided 5 % test requires about 3,800 inspections per group. The plant inspects roughly
208 per batch, so it needs about eighteen batches per shift to reach that power — which is
precisely why the aggregate comparison (5,996 versus 2,322 inspections) is more
informative than any single batch, and why the night-shift question should be revisited
monthly rather than settled from today's data.

**Recommendation for next month.** (i) Keep the p-chart but recompute limits from the
first 20 batches and add a run rule: seven consecutive points above the centre line
triggers investigation, which would have caught the post-25 drift earlier. (ii) Inspect at
least 1,100 units per shift before drawing a shift conclusion. (iii) Tighten the
acceptance plan for safety-critical lots to c = 5, accepting a 2 % lot with probability
0.787 while cutting the 5 % acceptance probability to 0.062.

**Multiple-testing note.** With 40 batches, the chance that at least one exceeds a
3-sigma limit purely by chance is about 1 − 0.9973⁴⁰ ≈ 10 %, so a single breach would not
by itself be alarming. Flagging "the worst batch" after looking at all 40 is a selection
effect; the chart's limits assume you look at each batch in sequence, not that you hunt for
the extreme one.
