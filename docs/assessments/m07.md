## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. Bayes' theorem expresses the posterior as
A) likelihood / prior
B) evidence × prior
C) likelihood × prior / evidence
D) prior / likelihood
* C
Why: The posterior is proportional to prior times likelihood, normalised by the marginal probability of the data. Dropping the evidence leaves an unnormalised density that does not integrate to 1.

Q2. In a population where 1 % have a disease, a test with 90 % sensitivity and 95 % specificity is used. The share of positives who truly have the disease is about
A) 50 %
B) 90 %
C) 15 %
D) 95 %
* C
Why: In 10,000 people, 90 true positives and 495 false positives give 90/585 ≈ 15.4 %. Sensitivity alone does not determine the predictive value; the base rate does.

Q3. The positive predictive value of a test depends on
A) prevalence, sensitivity and specificity
B) the sample size only
C) sensitivity only
D) specificity only
* A
Why: PPV = sensitivity × prevalence divided by the total positive rate, which includes false positives from the healthy majority. Changing prevalence changes PPV even when the test is unchanged.

Q4. A test's specificity is the probability that
A) a diseased person tests positive
B) a healthy person tests negative
C) a positive result is correct
D) a negative result is correct
* B
Why: Specificity is the true-negative rate among the healthy. "A positive is correct" is PPV, and "a diseased person tests positive" is sensitivity.

Q5. If the prior odds are 1 : 99 and the positive likelihood ratio is 18, the posterior odds are
A) 1 : 1782
B) 19 : 99
C) 18 : 18
D) 18 : 99
* D
Why: Bayes in odds form multiplies the prior odds by the likelihood ratio: 1/99 × 18 = 18/99, i.e. about 15 % probability. Posterior odds are not a probability until converted.

Q6. "Base-rate neglect" means
A) judging \(P(\text{disease}\mid\text{positive})\) by sensitivity alone, ignoring prevalence
B) using too large a sample
C) conditioning on the wrong variable deliberately
D) ignoring the specificity of a test
* A
Why: It is the confusion between \(P(\text{positive}\mid\text{disease})\) and \(P(\text{disease}\mid\text{positive})\), which differ by the base rate through Bayes' theorem.

Q7. Two tests are applied in sequence and interpreted as independent given disease status. The
A) the larger of the two
B) the sum of the sensitivities
C) the product of the sensitivities
D) the average
* C
Why: Conditional independence multiplies the detection probabilities: \(0.9\times0.9=0.81\). The specificity combines similarly for the false-positive rate.

Q8. The likelihood ratio for a positive test is
A) specificity / sensitivity
B) (1 − sensitivity) / specificity
C) prevalence / (1 − prevalence)
D) sensitivity / (1 − specificity)
* D
Why: LR+ compares the probability of a positive result in diseased and healthy people. Values above 1 increase the odds of disease; the inverse expression is LR−.

Q9. A disease has prevalence 0.5 %, sensitivity 99 % and specificity 99 %. The PPV is closest to
A) 50 %
B) 33 %
C) 5 %
D) 99 %
* B
Why: Per 10,000: 49.5 true positives and about 99.5 false positives, giving 49.5/149 ≈ 33 %. Excellent sensitivity and specificity still yield a modest PPV at low prevalence.

Q10. Conditional independence of \(A\) and \(B\) given \(C\) means
A) \(A\) and \(B\) are independent
B) \(P(A\cap B\mid C)=P(A\mid C)P(B\mid C)\)
C) \(P(A\mid B,C)=P(A\mid C)\)
D) both B and C, which are equivalent
* D
Why: The two statements are equivalent forms of the same condition. Marginal independence need not hold — a shared cause can make \(A\) and \(B\) dependent overall while independent within levels of \(C\).

Q11. The denominator in Bayes' theorem, \(P(\text{positive})\), equals
A) sensitivity
B) \(P(+|D)P(D)+P(+|D^c)P(D^c)\)
C) specificity
D) \(P(D)\)
* B
Why: It is the total probability of a positive test, weighting the true- and false-positive rates by prevalence. It normalises the posterior so the two cases sum to 1.

Q12. If a test has sensitivity 80 % and specificity 90 %, its false-positive rate is
A) 20 %
B) 10 %
C) 80 %
D) 90 %
* B
Why: The false-positive rate is 1 − specificity = 10 %. The false-negative rate is 1 − sensitivity = 20 %; mixing these two is a common error.

Q13. A high prior odds of disease combined with a negative test result can still leave
A) the negative likelihood ratio is close to 1
B) prevalence is very low
C) specificity is 100 %
D) the test is very sensitive
* A
Why: The posterior odds are prior odds × LR−. If LR− is near 1 the test barely changes belief, so a strong prior survives a negative result.

Q14. Which statement about \(P(A\mid B)\) and \(P(B\mid A)\) is correct?
A) They are always equal
B) They are equal only if \(P(A)=P(B)\)
C) Their ratio equals \(P(A)/P(B)\)
D) They are reciprocals
* C
Why: \(P(A\mid B)/P(B\mid A)=P(A)/P(B)\) from the definition. Equality requires equal marginal probabilities, which is why confusion between the two is dangerous in screening.

Q15. Updating a posterior twice is valid because
A) the likelihood is ignored on the second update
B) posteriors cannot be updated twice
C) the second posterior becomes the prior for the next update, provided the likelihoods are conditionally independent
D) the prior is always uniform
* C
Why: Sequential Bayesian updating is coherent: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. The result equals a single update with the product likelihood when observations are conditionally independent given the parameter.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain in words what a prior, a likelihood and a posterior represent.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The prior encodes belief about the parameter before seeing the data; the likelihood says how
probable the observed data are for each parameter value; the posterior combines them and
describes belief after seeing the data. The posterior is proportional to prior × likelihood,
normalised so it integrates to 1.
</details>

**S2.** Why is a 99 % sensitive test not "99 % accurate" for a patient who tests positive?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Sensitivity is \(P(+\mid D)\), but the patient needs \(P(D\mid +)\). Those differ by the base
rate: when disease is rare, the many healthy people generate enough false positives to dominate
the positive results. Accuracy also depends on specificity and prevalence.
</details>

**S3.** Explain why increasing prevalence raises the PPV of the same test.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
PPV = \(\frac{se\cdot\pi}{se\cdot\pi+(1-sp)(1-\pi)}\). Raising \(\pi\) increases the numerator
(true positives) faster than the denominator's false-positive contribution, because the healthy
pool shrinks. Screening in a high-risk group therefore yields more reliable positives.
</details>

**S4.** What does the odds form of Bayes' theorem make easy that the probability form does not?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It separates prior belief from evidence strength: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.
You can update by multiplication, chain several test results by multiplying likelihood ratios,
and see immediately how strong a test must be to overcome a prior.
</details>

**S5.** Why do two independent tests improve PPV much more than repeating one test?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Repeating the same test usually repeats the same error, so the results are correlated and add
little. Two tests whose errors are conditionally independent multiply the false-positive rate
down (0.05² = 0.0025) while keeping most of the sensitivity, so the positive group is much
cleaner.
</details>

**S6.** Give an example where the direction of conditioning reverses the conclusion.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Most people who own a bicycle are not cyclists, but most cyclists own a bicycle; most patients
with a rare disease test positive, but most positives do not have the disease. The two
conditional probabilities are related by the ratio of marginals, not by equality.
</details>

**S7.** What does it mean for two tests to be conditionally independent given disease status,
and why is that assumption often violated?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It means that once you know whether the patient is diseased, the two test results carry no
further information about each other. It is violated when both tests detect the same biological
marker, so a diseased patient is likely to test positive on both for the same reason, which
makes the combined evidence weaker than the product suggests.
</details>

**S8.** How would you explain a 15 % PPV to a patient without using formulas?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
"Out of 100 people like you who test positive, about 15 actually have the condition and 85 do
not. The test is good at finding the disease when it is there, but because the disease is
uncommon, most positives come from healthy people." Natural frequencies avoid the base-rate
confusion.
</details>

**S9.** Why is a p-value not \(P(H_0\mid\text{data})\)?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A p-value is \(P(\text{data at least this extreme}\mid H_0)\), computed under the null. Turning
it into \(P(H_0\mid\text{data})\) requires a prior over hypotheses and Bayes' theorem. Conflating
them produces the same base-rate error as confusing sensitivity with PPV.
</details>

**S10.** Describe one situation where a Bayesian update would be preferable to a single
hypothesis test.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When evidence accumulates sequentially and decisions must be made at each step — for example
monitoring a manufacturing line or a clinical trial. The posterior after each batch is a
complete summary of belief, can be updated without multiple-testing penalties, and supports
decision thresholds directly.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** Prevalence 1 %, sensitivity 90 %, specificity 95 %. Compute the PPV.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Per 10,000: 100 diseased → 90 test positive; 9,900 healthy → 5 % = 495 false positives. Total
positives 585, so PPV \(=90/585=0.1538\)** (15.4 %).
</details>

**N2.** Prevalence 5 %, sensitivity 80 %, specificity 90 %. Compute the PPV.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Per 10,000: 500 diseased → 400 positive; 9,500 healthy → 950 false positives. PPV
\(=400/1{,}350=0.2963\)** (29.6 %). Raising prevalence from 1 % to 5 % roughly doubles the PPV.
</details>

**N3.** Using N1, compute the negative predictive value.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
True negatives = 9,900 − 495 = 9,405; false negatives = 10. Total negatives = 9,415, so
NPV \(=9{,}405/9{,}415=0.9989\)**. A negative result is highly reassuring even though a
positive one is not.
</details>

**N4.** Prior odds of disease are 1 : 99 and LR+ = 18. Compute the posterior probability.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Posterior odds \(=18/99=0.1818\), so probability \(=0.1818/(1+0.1818)=0.1538\)**, matching
N1. This is Bayes' theorem in odds form, and it shows why the update is multiplicative.
</details>

**N5.** Two conditionally independent tests each have sensitivity 0.9 and false-positive rate
0.05. Both are positive. Compute the PPV at 1 % prevalence.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Combined sensitivity \(=0.81\); combined false-positive rate \(=0.0025\). Per 10,000: 81 true
positives and 24.75 false positives, so PPV \(=81/105.75=0.766\). Requiring both tests
raises PPV from 15 % to 77 % at the cost of missing 19 % of cases.
</details>

**N6.** Using N1, compute the probability of disease given a negative test.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
False negatives \(=100-90=10\) out of 10,000; all negatives \(=9{,}415\), so
\(P(D\mid-)=10/9{,}415=0.00106\)** (0.11 %). This is \(1-\mathrm{NPV}\).
</details>

**N7.** A factory has three lines producing 50 %, 30 % and 20 % of output, with defect rates
1 %, 2 % and 5 %. A defective item is found. Find the probability it came from the third line.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Defect probability \(=0.5(0.01)+0.3(0.02)+0.2(0.05)=0.005+0.006+0.010=0.021\). Posterior for
line 3 \(=0.010/0.021=0.476\). The smallest line produces nearly half of the defects because
its rate is five times the first line's.
</details>

**N8.** Prevalence 10 %, sensitivity 95 %, specificity 80 %. Compute PPV.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Per 1,000: 100 diseased → 95 positive; 900 healthy → 180 false positives. PPV
\(=95/275=0.345\). A specificity of only 80 % is costly whenever the healthy group is large.
</details>

**N9.** Compute LR− for a test with sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.95.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{LR}-=(1-se)/sp=0.10/0.95=0.105\). A negative result multiplies the odds of disease
by about 0.1, roughly a tenfold reduction — much stronger evidence than LR+ = 18 is in the other
direction.
</details>

**N10.** Prior probability 0.5, sensitivity 0.9, false-positive rate 0.05. Compute the
posterior after a positive test.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Numerator \(=0.5\times0.9=0.45\); denominator \(=0.45+0.5\times0.05=0.475\); posterior
\(=0.45/0.475=0.947\). With an even prior, a good test is highly informative — the same test
gave 15 % at 1 % prevalence.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A hospital proposes screening all adults for a disease with 0.2 % prevalence using a test
with 99 % sensitivity and 98 % specificity. Compute the PPV and advise.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Per 100,000: 200 diseased → 198 true positives; 99,800 healthy → 1,996 false positives. PPV
\(=198/2{,}194=0.090\)**, about 9 %. Nine in ten positives are false, each triggering anxiety
and confirmatory tests. Advise targeted screening of high-risk groups (higher prevalence), or a
second confirmatory test, and report the numbers honestly.
</details>

**P2.** A model flags fraudulent transactions with 70 % precision at 40 % recall. The fraud team
can investigate 500 cases a month. How many true frauds would they catch if 2,000 transactions
are flagged?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
At 70 % precision, 2,000 flags contain about 1,400 true frauds; investigating only 500 (ranked by
score) catches roughly 350 if precision holds at the top of the ranking. But precision usually
falls down the list, so measure precision at the operating threshold actually used, and report
recall against the total number of frauds.
</details>

**P3.** A spam filter is tuned to specificity 99.9 % on a stream where 0.1 % of emails are spam.
What happens to the flag rate, and how should the threshold be chosen?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Of 1,000 emails, 1 is spam and 999 are legitimate; a 0.1 % false-positive rate flags about 1
legitimate email, comparable to the number of true spams. Flagging is nearly useless at this
base rate. Choose the threshold from the cost of a missed spam versus a lost legitimate email,
and evaluate precision at the operating point.
</details>

**P4.** Two analysts disagree: one says "the test is 95 % accurate, so a positive means a 95 %
chance of disease"; the other says "it depends on prevalence". Who is right, and how would you
demonstrate it?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The second. Show a 10,000-person table at two prevalences (say 1 % and 20 %) with the same
sensitivity and specificity, computing PPV for each; the PPV changes from about 16 % to about
70 %. The word "accuracy" hides which conditional probability is meant.
</details>

**P5.** A monitoring system updates a fraud probability after each transaction. Explain how to
combine yesterday's posterior with today's evidence, and what assumption makes this valid.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Use yesterday's posterior as today's prior and multiply by today's likelihood ratio, provided the
transactions are conditionally independent given the customer's fraud status and the behaviour
is stationary. If behaviour shifts (new attack pattern), the model must adapt — for example with
discounting of old evidence or periodic refitting.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive Bayes' theorem from the definition of conditional probability and state the
conditions required.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
For \(P(B)>0\), \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\). Symmetrically,
\(P(B\mid A)=P(A\cap B)/P(A)\), so \(P(A\cap B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)\). Substituting gives
\(P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)/P(B)\). If \(A_1,\dots,A_k\) partition the space, the denominator is
\(\sum_i P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)\), giving the usual form. Requirements: \(P(B)>0\) and, for the
summed denominator, a partition with positive probabilities.
</details>

**C2.** Show that PPV is increasing in prevalence for fixed sensitivity and specificity, and find
the limiting PPV as prevalence tends to 0.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Let \(a=se\), \(b=1-sp\). PPV \(=a\pi/(a\pi+b(1-\pi))\). Dividing numerator and denominator by
\(\pi\) gives \(a/(a+b(1-\pi)/\pi)\), which increases as \(\pi\) increases because the subtracted
term falls. As \(\pi\to0\), PPV \(\to a\pi/(b)\to0\): without disease in the population, every
positive is false.
</details>

**C3.** A test is applied twice. Compare the PPV when the two results are (i) conditionally
independent and (ii) perfectly correlated, at 1 % prevalence with sensitivity 0.9 and
false-positive rate 0.05.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
(i) Independent errors: combined sensitivity 0.81, combined false-positive rate 0.0025, so PPV
\(=0.0081/(0.0081+0.002475)=0.766\). (ii) Perfect correlation: the second test adds no
information, so PPV stays at \(0.009/(0.009+0.0495)=0.154\). The gain from repeat testing
comes entirely from independent errors, which is why correlated tests are often not worth
repeating.
</details>
