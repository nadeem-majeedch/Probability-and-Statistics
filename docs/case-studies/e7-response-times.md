## A. Problem statement

An online retailer monitors the latency of its four main endpoints. Operations wants to
know whether the service meets its 500 ms target, which endpoint is slowest, and whether
the cache is earning its keep. The CTO has heard that "average latency is 125 ms" and
believes the service is healthy; the engineering lead suspects the average is hiding a
long tail that users actually feel.

## B. Background and context

Web latency is lognormal: a few requests are extremely slow, and the mean is dragged
upward by them. Service-level objectives are therefore written on percentiles (p95, p99),
not means, because a user experiences one request, not an average. This case introduces
the practical difference between mean and percentile, the lognormal distribution, and
the reason a log transformation makes latency talk tractable. It also shows how to test
whether a caching change is associated with a real reduction in latency rather than
noise.

## C. Dataset description

`response_times.csv` has 1,200 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `request_id` | integer | Request identifier |
| `endpoint` | text | `/search` (481), `/product` (341), `/checkout` (189), `/profile` (189) |
| `cache_hit` | text | `yes` (756) or `no` (444) |
| `latency_ms` | float | Total response time in milliseconds |
| `status` | integer | HTTP status code |

**Generation method.** Each endpoint has a base median latency (search 120 ms, product
180 ms, checkout 260 ms, profile 90 ms). A cache hit multiplies the median by 0.55.
Latency is then lognormal with that median and σ = 0.55 on the log scale, so the
distribution is right-skewed by construction. Status codes are drawn with 96 % 200s and a
small share of 404s and 500s.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. Describe the latency distribution: mean, median, standard deviation, skewness.
2. What are the 90th, 95th and 99th percentiles, and how do they compare with the mean?
3. Does the service meet a 500 ms p95 objective?
4. Which endpoint is slowest, and by how much?
5. How much faster are cached requests, and is the difference large relative to the
   spread?
6. Does a log transformation make the distribution approximately normal, and why does
   that help?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Right-skewed continuous distributions; mean versus median.
- Quantiles and percentiles as service-level metrics.
- The lognormal distribution: parameters on the log scale, median \(=e^{\mu}\), mean
  \(=e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\).
- Log transformation and its effect on skewness and on variance stabilisation.
- Comparing groups with a t-test on logs (a ratio interpretation).
- Tail probabilities and threshold exceedance.

## F. Mathematical formulation

For lognormal latency with log-mean \(\mu\) and log-SD \(\sigma\), the median is \(e^{\mu}\) and
the mean is \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\), so the mean exceeds the median whenever \(\sigma>0\).
The \(p\)-quantile is

\[
Q(p)=\exp\!\left(\mu+\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p)\right).
\]

The share of requests above a threshold \(t\) is

\[
P(X>t)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln t-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
\]

A ratio comparison of two groups uses the difference of log means:
\(\ln(\bar{m}_1/\bar{m}_2)=\mu_1-\mu_2\), tested with a two-sample t-test on \(\ln x\).

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Load the file; check the status codes and the latency range.
2. Summarise latency overall: mean, median, SD, skewness, minimum, maximum.
3. Compute p50, p90, p95 and p99.
4. Compare each percentile with the 500 ms objective.
5. Break latency down by endpoint and by cache status.
6. Transform to logs; recompute skewness and the group comparison on the log scale.
7. Plot a histogram on both scales to show the effect of the transformation.
8. Write the operations summary in the language of percentiles.

## H. Student tasks

1. Report mean, median and SD. Which is larger and why?
2. Compute p90, p95 and p99. Does the service meet the p95 objective?
3. What proportion of requests exceed 500 ms? 1,000 ms?
4. Which endpoint has the highest median and which the highest p95? Are they the same?
5. Compute the median latency ratio for cache hits versus misses. Express it as a
   percentage reduction.
6. Run a t-test on log latency and explain why it tests a ratio rather than a difference.
7. Rewrite "average latency is 125 ms" as a sentence an operations team can act on.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

logs = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/response_times.csv")
print(logs.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("rows:", len(logs), "| status codes:", logs.status.value_counts().to_dict())

latency = logs["latency_ms"]
print(f"\nmean = {latency.mean():.1f} ms, median = {latency.median():.1f} ms, "
      f"sd = {latency.std(ddof=1):.1f} ms, skew = {stats.skew(latency):.2f}")
for p in (90, 95, 99):
    print(f"p{p} = {np.percentile(latency, p):.1f} ms")
print(f"share above 500 ms: {(latency > 500).mean() * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"share above 1000 ms: {(latency > 1000).mean() * 100:.2f}%")

print("\nby endpoint:")
print(logs.groupby("endpoint")["latency_ms"]
      .agg(count="size", median="median", mean="mean",
           p95=lambda s: np.percentile(s, 95)).round(1).to_string())

print("\nby cache status:")
print(logs.groupby("cache_hit")["latency_ms"]
      .agg(count="size", median="median", mean="mean").round(1).to_string())

logs["log_latency"] = np.log(logs["latency_ms"])
print(f"\nlog-scale: mean = {logs.log_latency.mean():.3f}, "
      f"sd = {logs.log_latency.std(ddof=1):.3f}, skew = {stats.skew(logs.log_latency):.2f}")
print(f"implied lognormal mean = {np.exp(logs.log_latency.mean() + logs.log_latency.std(ddof=1) ** 2 / 2):.1f} ms")

hit = logs.loc[logs.cache_hit == "yes", "log_latency"]
miss = logs.loc[logs.cache_hit == "no", "log_latency"]
test = stats.ttest_ind(hit, miss, equal_var=False)
ratio = np.exp(hit.mean() - miss.mean())
print(f"\ncache ratio (hit vs miss) = {ratio:.3f}, i.e. {(1 - ratio) * 100:.1f}% faster")
print(f"t-test on logs: t = {test.statistic:.1f}, p = {test.pvalue:.2e}")
```

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

axes[0].set(title="Latency (linear scale)", xlabel="Milliseconds", ylabel="Requests")
axes[0].legend(frameon=False)

axes[1].hist(logs["log_latency"], bins=45, color="#55a868", edgecolor="white")
axes[1].set(title="Log latency", xlabel="ln(milliseconds)", ylabel="Requests")
fig.tight_layout()
```

**How to read it.** On the linear scale the distribution is a spike near 80–150 ms with
a long tail past 700 ms; the mean sits to the right of the median, exactly as a
right-skewed distribution dictates. On the log scale the same data look close to a
symmetric bell, which is why log-scale confidence intervals and tests are appropriate. The
500 ms line in the left panel shows how few requests breach the target even though the
mean looks acceptable.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Mean latency is 124.6 ms but the median is 94.3 ms: 30 ms of "average" is tail.
- p90 = 256.8 ms, p95 = 341.8 ms, p99 = 510.4 ms: the p95 objective of 500 ms is met, but
  the p99 is breached.
- 1.25 % of requests (15 of 1,200) exceed 500 ms; essentially none exceed 1,000 ms.
- Checkout is the slowest endpoint (median 165 ms, p95 about 435 ms); profile is fastest
  (median 56.8 ms).
- Cached requests have a median of 78.0 ms against 130.4 ms uncached, a ratio of about
  0.60 — the cache removes roughly 40 % of latency.
- Log latency is nearly symmetric (skew 0.21 versus 2.14), confirming the lognormal model
  and justifying analyses on the log scale.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — the average is misleading.** Mean 124.6 and median 94.3 differ by 30 ms. In a
symmetric distribution they would coincide; here the gap is the signature of the tail. The
standard deviation, 101.1 ms, is nearly as large as the mean, another symptom of a
heavy right tail. Reporting "average latency 125 ms" is technically true and operationally
useless.

**Step 2 — percentiles.** p90 = 256.8, p95 = 341.8, p99 = 510.4. The p95 objective of
500 ms is comfortably met; the p99 is breached by 2 %. If the service-level agreement is
written on p95, the team is compliant; if it is written on p99, it is not. This is exactly
why the percentile must be stated in any latency claim.

**Step 3 — tails.** 1.25 % of requests take longer than 500 ms — 15 of 1,200 — and no
request exceeds 1,000 ms. The practical reading: most users see a fast site, and roughly
one in eighty waits half a second or more. Reducing the tail usually means attacking a
specific cause (cold cache, large payload, slow query) rather than tuning the median.

**Step 4 — endpoints.** Median latencies: checkout 165.0, product 113.4, search 79.9,
profile 56.8. The checkout endpoint handles the most business-critical interaction and is also the slowest: a p95 of about 435 ms
means one in twenty checkout attempts waits nearly half a second. Search has the highest traffic share (40 %) but is not the slowest, so
optimisation effort should follow business value, not request volume.

**Step 5 — cache.** Uncached median 130.4 ms, cached 78.0 ms. The ratio of geometric
means is 0.60, so caching removes about 40 % of typical latency; the t-test on logs gives
t ≈ −12.8 with p < 10⁻³⁰. Because the test is on logs, the effect size is multiplicative:
the cache does not remove a fixed number of milliseconds, it scales latency down.

**Step 6 — lognormal check.** Log-latency mean 4.568, SD 0.706, skew 0.21. The implied
lognormal mean is exp(4.568 + 0.706²/2) ≈ 124.0 ms, which closely matches the observed
124.6 ms — a good consistency check on the model. The lognormal quantile formula then
predicts p99 = exp(4.568 + 2.326 × 0.706) ≈ 497 ms, close to the observed 510 ms; small
discrepancies come from the mixture of endpoints, each with its own median.

**Step 7 — how to report it.** "Median latency is 94 ms and p95 is 342 ms, so 95 % of
requests complete within about a third of a second; 1.2 % exceed 500 ms, concentrated in
the checkout endpoint. Cached requests are about 40 % faster. The mean of 125 ms is not a
good summary because latency is lognormal." That sentence is actionable; the mean alone is
not.

**Modelling note.** Because the data are a mixture of four endpoints, the pooled
distribution is not exactly lognormal — the histogram on the log scale is slightly
flattened. Per-endpoint lognormal fits are tighter. This is a useful lesson: a
distributional model can be a good approximation at the aggregate level and a better one
within homogeneous groups.
