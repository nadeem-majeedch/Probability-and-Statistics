## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. A researcher records the number of hours each student studies per week. The measurement scale is
A) ratio
B) nominal
C) ordinal
D) interval
* A
Why: Hours have a true zero (no study time at all) and equal intervals, so "twice as many hours" is meaningful. Interval scales lack a true zero, ordinal scales only rank, and nominal scales only label.

Q2. The average mark of all 4,200 students enrolled in a course is 66.4. In statistical language, 66.4 is
A) a variable
B) a statistic
C) a parameter
D) a sample
* C
Why: A parameter describes the whole population; a statistic is computed from a sample. The number 4,200 is the population size, not a statistic.

Q3. A company measures the satisfaction of 300 customers and reports "average satisfaction was 3.8 out of 5". This report is
A) a hypothesis test
B) a probability model
C) purely inferential
D) purely descriptive
* D
Why: It summarises the observed sample without generalising beyond it. Inference would begin when the company used those 300 to estimate satisfaction for all customers, with an interval.

Q4. Which variable is measured on an ordinal scale?
A) Blood group (A, B, AB, O)
B) Waiting time in minutes
C) Service rating (poor, fair, good, excellent)
D) Number of claims filed
* C
Why: The rating categories are ordered but the gaps between them are not equal or measurable. Blood group is nominal, the other two are ratio-scaled.

Q5. A survey is mailed to 1,000 households; 120 replies arrive. The main threat to inference is
A) sampling error only
B) non-response bias
C) measurement error in the questionnaire
D) the use of a simple random sample
* B
Why: The 12 % who replied are probably not a random subset of the 1,000 — interested or affected households answer more often — so the estimate is biased, not merely imprecise.

Q6. Random assignment of subjects to treatment and control groups is used mainly to
A) guarantee a normal distribution
B) increase the sample size
C) remove sampling error
D) balance unmeasured confounders between groups
* D
Why: Randomisation makes the groups comparable in expectation, including on variables nobody measured. It does not enlarge the sample, eliminate sampling error, or make the data normal.

Q7. A university wants 10 % of each department in a survey, drawn randomly within departments. This design is
A) stratified sampling
B) cluster sampling
C) convenience sampling
D) simple random sampling
* A
Why: The population is divided into strata (departments) and a random sample is drawn inside each, which guarantees representation and usually reduces variance.

Q8. Which pair correctly distinguishes a sample from a population?
A) Sample = a parameter; population = a statistic
B) Sample = all students; population = 50 students
C) Sample = 50 students; population = all students
D) Sample = the variable; population = the parameter
* C
Why: The sample is the subset actually observed; the population is the whole group about which inference is made.

Q9. "Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths rise together across the year." The most plausible explanation is
A) drowning causes ice-cream sales
B) both are driven by hot weather
C) the correlation is a calculation error
D) ice cream causes drowning
* B
Why: A third variable (seasonal temperature) drives both, producing a spurious association. This is confounding, not causation in either direction.

Q10. Which of the following is a discrete variable?
A) Time to failure of a component
B) Number of defective items in a batch
C) Weight of a parcel
D) Body temperature
* B
Why: A count takes separated whole-number values; the others can take any value in an interval.

Q11. A histogram of incomes is strongly right-skewed. Which summary pair is most informative?
A) Mean and standard deviation
B) Median and interquartile range
C) Mode and range
D) Mean and range
* B
Why: The median and IQR resist the pull of the long right tail, whereas the mean and standard deviation are inflated by a few very large incomes.

Q12. A study finds that students who eat breakfast score higher on average. Before concluding that breakfast raises marks, the analyst should first ask
A) whether breakfast eaters differ in other ways that also affect marks
B) whether the marks were normally distributed
C) whether the sample was large enough
D) whether the result is statistically significant
* A
Why: Breakfast habits correlate with income, routine and parental involvement, so the comparison may reflect confounding. Sample size and significance address precision, not bias.

Q13. The main advantage of a census over a sample is that it
A) is always cheaper
B) eliminates measurement error
C) removes sampling error
D) guarantees causal conclusions
* C
Why: Measuring everyone removes the error from not observing part of the population, but measurement, coverage and processing errors remain — and a census is usually more expensive.

Q14. In the expression "the average height of 30 randomly chosen students is 168 cm", the value 168 is
A) a statistic
B) a population
C) a sample
D) a parameter
* A
Why: It is computed from the 30 observed students, so it is a sample statistic used to estimate the unknown population parameter.

Q15. Which statement about descriptive statistics is correct?
A) They require a probability model to be computed.
B) They are only valid for normal data.
C) They always support causal claims.
D) They summarise the observed data without generalising beyond it.
* D
Why: Description is summarising what was measured; inference is the separate step of generalising, and it needs assumptions about how the sample arose.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Define population, sample, parameter and statistic, and give one example of each in a
hospital setting.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The population is every unit of interest — all patients treated in the hospital this year.
The sample is the subset observed — 200 randomly selected discharged patients. A parameter is
a numerical summary of the population, such as the true 30-day readmission rate; it is usually
unknown. A statistic is the corresponding summary in the sample, such as the readmission rate
among the 200, used to estimate the parameter.
</details>

**S2.** Why is a sample often preferable to a census, even when a census is possible?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Cost, speed and quality. Measuring everyone is expensive and slow, and large operations
introduce their own errors (non-response, poor training, data entry). A well-drawn probability
sample gives a quantifiable margin of error at a fraction of the cost, and it can be finished
while the information is still useful.
</details>

**S3.** Distinguish nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio scales, with one example each.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Nominal: labels with no order (blood group). Ordinal: ordered categories with unequal or
unknown gaps (satisfaction: poor/fair/good). Interval: equal gaps but an arbitrary zero
(temperature in °C). Ratio: equal gaps and a true zero, so ratios are meaningful (waiting
time, income, number of claims).
</details>

**S4.** What is the difference between a variable and a data value?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A variable is a characteristic that can differ between units (height, treatment group); it is
the column. A data value is one observation of that variable for one unit — the entry in a
cell. A dataset is variables as columns and values as rows.
</details>

**S5.** Explain the difference between random sampling and random assignment, and state which
one supports a causal conclusion.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Random sampling chooses which units enter the study, supporting generalisation from sample to
population. Random assignment decides which treatment each unit receives, balancing confounders
across groups and supporting causal claims about the treatment. A study can use either, both,
or neither.
</details>

**S6.** A report says "the average customer is 34 years old". Why can this sentence be
misleading even if the calculation is correct?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
"Average" hides the distribution. If the customer base is a mass of 20-year-olds plus a group
of older customers, the mean can sit where almost no customer is, and it is pulled by a few
extreme values. The median, the spread, and the shape of the age distribution are needed before
the sentence means anything operational.
</details>

**S7.** Give an example of confounding and explain why randomisation removes it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Suppose students who use a study app also sleep more; sleep raises marks, so the app's apparent
benefit is partly sleep's. Randomising who gets the app makes sleep, motivation and every other
confounder similar in both groups on average, so a difference in marks can be attributed to the
app.
</details>

**S8.** What is the difference between a discrete and a continuous variable, and why does the
distinction matter for choosing a distribution?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A discrete variable takes separated values, usually counts (number of claims); a continuous
variable takes any value in an interval (waiting time). Discrete variables are modelled with
probability mass functions (binomial, Poisson); continuous ones with densities (normal,
exponential), and probabilities are areas rather than point masses.
</details>

**S9.** Why should a data scientist examine a dataset before computing any statistic?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
To catch problems that silently corrupt every later result: missing values, impossible ranges,
duplicated rows, inconsistent categories ("M"/"male"/"Male"), unit mismatches, and sampling
problems. A mean computed on uncleaned data is precise-looking and wrong.
</details>

**S10.** State two properties a good research question should have, and illustrate with a
bad-to-good rewrite.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It should be answerable with available data and specific enough to measure. "Is our app good?"
is unanswerable; "Does the redesigned checkout increase the conversion rate among mobile users
within four weeks?" names a population, an outcome, a comparison and a time frame.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** The weekly study hours of eight students are 4, 7, 7, 9, 12, 15, 15, 15. Compute the
mean, median, mode, range and interquartile range (use the median-of-halves rule).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Sum = 84, so mean = 84/8 = **10.5 hours**. With n = 8 the median is the average of the 4th and
5th values, (9 + 12)/2 = **10.5**. The mode is **15** (three times). Range = 15 − 4 = **11**.
Lower half 4, 7, 7, 9 has median 7; upper half 12, 15, 15, 15 has median 15; IQR = 15 − 7 =
**8** hours. The mean and median agree here because the data are nearly symmetric.
</details>

**N2.** A factory employs 2,000 workers. A safety survey samples 250. What is the sampling
fraction, and what weight should each sampled worker carry?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Sampling fraction = 250/2,000 = **0.125, or 12.5 %**. Each sampled worker represents
1/0.125 = **8 workers**, so the design weight is 8. A survey count of 30 injured workers would
scale to an estimated 30 × 8 = 240 injuries in the workforce.
</details>

**N3.** A university has 1,200 first-year, 800 second-year and 500 third-year students. A
proportional stratified sample of 500 is drawn. How many students come from each year?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Total = 1,200 + 800 + 500 = 2,500, so the sampling fraction is 500/2,500 = 0.2. First year:
1,200 × 0.2 = **240**. Second year: 800 × 0.2 = **160**. Third year: 500 × 0.2 = **100**.
Check: 240 + 160 + 100 = 500.
</details>

**N4.** Group A has 30 students with mean mark 62; group B has 20 students with mean mark 78.
Compute the combined mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Combined mean = (30 × 62 + 20 × 78)/50 = (1,860 + 1,560)/50 = 3,420/50 = **68.4**. The
unweighted average of the two means, 70, is wrong because it ignores the different group sizes.
</details>

**N5.** A day is 25 °C at noon and 50 °C in a heat chamber. A journalist says "it is twice as
hot". Evaluate the claim using the Kelvin scale.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Celsius is an interval scale: 0 °C is an arbitrary reference, so "twice as hot" is undefined on
it. In kelvin, 25 °C = 298.15 K and 50 °C = 323.15 K, so the ratio is 323.15/298.15 =
**1.084** — about 8 % hotter in absolute temperature. The doubling claim counts from an
arbitrary zero.
</details>

**N6.** In a sample of 180 customers, 45 report a problem with delivery. Express this as a
proportion, a percentage and odds.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Proportion = 45/180 = **0.25**. Percentage = **25 %**. Odds = 45/135 = **1 : 3**, i.e. one
reporting customer for every three who do not. Note that the odds (0.333) and the probability
(0.25) are different numbers.
</details>

**N7.** For the values 2, 4, 4, 6, 9 compute the mean and the mean absolute deviation.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean = (2 + 4 + 4 + 6 + 9)/5 = 25/5 = **5**. Deviations are −3, −1, −1, +1, +4; absolute
deviations 3, 1, 1, 1, 4 sum to 10, so MAD = 10/5 = **2**. The mean absolute deviation is in
the same units as the data, unlike the variance.
</details>

**N8.** Two datasets of salaries (thousands): A = 12, 14, 15, 16, 18; B = 12, 14, 15, 16, 95.
Compute the mean and median of each and comment.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
A: mean = 75/5 = **15.0**, median = **15**. B: mean = 152/5 = **30.4**, median = **15**. One
extreme salary triples the mean while leaving the median untouched — the reason median income
is reported for skewed data such as salaries.
</details>

**N9.** A questionnaire is sent to 400 alumni; 60 return it. Compute the response rate and
explain the direction of the likely bias if satisfied alumni are more likely to reply.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Response rate = 60/400 = **15 %**, so non-response is 85 %. If satisfied alumni reply more
often, the respondents over-represent satisfied graduates and the satisfaction estimate is
biased **upward**. A larger sample does not fix this: the bias is in who answers, not how many.
</details>

**N10.** A dataset records a nominal variable "region" with four categories. How many indicator
(dummy) variables are needed for a regression, and why not four?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
**Three.** Four dummies would sum to 1 in every row, which is perfectly collinear with the
intercept, so the design matrix has no unique solution (the "dummy variable trap"). One
category is the reference, and each coefficient measures the difference from it.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A university wants to know the average time students spend on the learning platform per
week. It can afford 400 responses. Compare two designs — an email invitation to all 6,000
students, and a random sample of 400 with reminders — and recommend one.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The open invitation gives an unknown, probably biased sample: engaged students answer, so the
mean is likely overstated, and the "margin of error" is not meaningful because selection is not
random. The random sample of 400, with reminders to raise response, has a known design and a
quantifiable standard error (roughly
\(\text{SD}/\sqrt{400}\)); if SD ≈ 3 hours, the standard error is 0.15 hours. Recommend the
random sample and report the response rate and any weighting.
</details>

**P2.** A hospital reports that the mean waiting time in its emergency department is 42
minutes, while the median is 25 minutes. A manager proposes staffing to the mean. What do you
advise?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The gap signals a right-skewed distribution: most patients wait around 25 minutes, and a small
number wait very long. Staffing to the mean would leave roughly half of patients waiting longer
than the mean, which is poor service. Examine the 90th percentile and the tail; staffing
decisions should target a percentile (for example, 90 % seen within 40 minutes) rather than the
mean.
</details>

**P3.** An analyst finds that customers who use the mobile app spend 30 % more than those who
do not, and concludes the app drives spending. List two alternative explanations and one study
design that would settle the question.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Alternatives: (i) heavy spenders choose the app (self-selection); (ii) app users are also
loyalty-programme members, and loyalty drives spending. A randomised encouragement trial —
randomly invite customers to install the app, then compare spending intent-to-treat — would
separate the app's effect from selection.
</details>

**P4.** You are handed a spreadsheet with a "date" column containing entries such as
`2026-01-05`, `05/01/2026` and `Jan 5`, and a "sales" column with some blanks. Describe your
first five actions before any modelling.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
(1) Parse dates with an explicit format and flag ambiguous day/month entries. (2) Check the
range for impossible dates. (3) Count and inspect missing sales values; decide whether they are
missing at random, and record the decision. (4) Check for duplicate rows and zero/negative
sales. (5) Produce a data dictionary with units, then summarise the cleaned series before any
model. Document every change so the cleaning is reproducible.
</details>

**P5.** A student says: "My sample of 30 friends shows the average screen time is 6 hours, so
students at the university average 6 hours." Identify the design and inference problems in one
paragraph.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Friends are a convenience sample, not a probability sample, so the estimate has no valid
margin of error and is likely biased toward the student's own peer group. Thirty observations
are also far too few to characterise a heterogeneous population. The correct statement is
descriptive of those 30 people; generalising would require a random sample of the university
population and an interval.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Show that the sample mean minimises the sum of squared deviations
\(\sum_i (x_i-c)^2\) over all choices of \(c\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Let \(S(c)=\sum_i (x_i-c)^2\). Differentiating,
\(S'(c)=-2\sum_i (x_i-c)=-2(n\bar x-nc)=-2n(\bar x-c)\). Setting \(S'(c)=0\) gives
\(c=\bar x\). The second derivative \(S''(c)=2n>0\), so this stationary point is a minimum.
Hence the mean is the least-squares centre, and the minimum value is
\(\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2\).
</details>

**C2.** Prove that the median is unchanged by any strictly increasing transformation, and
explain why the mean is not.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Sort the data \(x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}\). If \(g\) is strictly increasing then
\(g(x_{(1)})\le\cdots\le g(x_{(n)})\), so the order statistics — and therefore the middle
value(s) — are the same objects, only relabelled: the median of \(g(x)\) is \(g\)(median of
\(x\)). The mean is a sum, and for a nonlinear \(g\),
\(\overline{g(x)}\neq g(\bar x)\) in general (Jensen's inequality gives the direction when
\(g\) is convex or concave). Example: for 1, 2, 9 the mean is 4 and the median is 2; squaring
gives 1, 4, 81 with mean 28.67 and median 4, and \(4^2=16\neq 28.67\).
</details>

**C3.** A variable is rescaled as \(y=a+bx\) with \(b\neq 0\). Derive how the mean, variance,
standard deviation and interquartile range change, and state which are equivariant.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Mean: \(\bar y=a+b\bar x\) (linearity of sums). Variance:
\(\mathrm{Var}(y)=b^2\mathrm{Var}(x)\) because deviations scale by \(b\) and constants vanish.
Standard deviation: \(s_y=|b|s_x\). IQR: quantiles transform as \(Q_y(p)=a+bQ_x(p)\) for
\(b>0\) (reversed for \(b<0\)), so \(\mathrm{IQR}_y=|b|\,\mathrm{IQR}_x\). Mean, SD and IQR are
therefore equivariant under changes of units; variance scales by \(b^2\), which is why it is
not in the original units.
</details>
