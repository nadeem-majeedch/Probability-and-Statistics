## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The weak law of large numbers says that as \(n\to\infty\), the sample mean
A) stays random
B) converges in probability to the population mean
C) becomes exactly normal
D) converges to the sample variance
* B
Why: The LLN is about the value the average approaches; the CLT describes the shape of the fluctuations around it. They answer different questions.

Q2. The central limit theorem describes the distribution of
A) the sample variance exactly
B) the data
C) the standardised sample mean, approximately, for large \(n\)
D) the population
* C
Why: It is a statement about the sampling distribution of the mean or sum, not about the raw observations, which keep their original shape.

Q3. The CLT requires
A) a symmetric population
B) \(n<30\)
C) normally distributed data
D) finite variance and independent observations
* D
Why: Independence and finite variance are the standard conditions; the population shape is unrestricted. Strong skew or heavy tails slow convergence but do not break the theorem.

Q4. If \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the variance of
A) \(\sigma^2/n\)
B) \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\)
C) \(\sigma^2 n\)
D) \(\sigma^2\)
* A
Why: Variances of independent variables add, and dividing by \(n\) squares the scale factor. \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) is the standard error, the square root of this variance.

Q5. The usual "\(n\ge30\)" rule of thumb is
A) a theorem
B) a rough guide that depends on the skewness of the population
C) required for the LLN
D) sufficient for any distribution
* B
Why: Symmetric populations may need only a handful of observations, very skewed ones hundreds. The threshold is a heuristic, not a guarantee.

Q6. For a sum of independent normal variables, the distribution of the sum is
A) approximately normal
B) exactly normal
C) never normal
D) exponential
* B
Why: Normality is closed under addition exactly, for any \(n\). Approximation is needed only for non-normal populations.

Q7. A continuity correction is applied when
A) approximating a discrete distribution with a continuous one
B) computing a mean
C) estimating a variance
D) data are normal
* A
Why: It adjusts the boundary by 0.5 to cover the probability mass of the discrete value, improving the normal approximation to binomial or Poisson counts.

Q8. The LLN justifies
A) the exact normality of the data
B) exactly 95 % coverage of intervals for any \(n\)
C) zero sampling error
D) using a sample mean as an estimate of \(\mu\)
* D
Why: It guarantees the average converges to the parameter, which is why averaging is a sensible estimation strategy. It does not supply the distribution needed for intervals.

Q9. Monte Carlo integration error typically decreases like
A) \(e^{-n}\)
B) \(1/n\)
C) \(1/\sqrt{n}\)
D) \(1/n^2\)
* C
Why: Simulation error is a standard error of an average, so it shrinks with the square root of the number of draws. Halving the error needs four times the simulations.

Q10. Chebyshev's inequality gives
A) a distribution-free bound on deviations from the mean
B) the CLT
C) an unbiased variance estimate
D) an exact normal probability
* A
Why: It bounds \(P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le1/k^2\) for any distribution with finite variance. The bound is loose but needs no shape assumption.

Q11. A histogram of 10,000 sample means from a strongly skewed population will look
A) uniform
B) bimodal
C) as skewed as the population
D) approximately normal
* D
Why: The CLT applies to the sampling distribution of the mean. The population remains skewed, but the averages concentrate symmetrically around \(\mu\).

Q12. Increasing \(n\) from 100 to 400 changes the standard error of the mean by a factor of
A) 1/2
B) 1/4
C) 4
D) 2
* A
Why: \(\sqrt{400}/\sqrt{100}=2\), so the standard error halves. The variance falls by a factor of 4, the SE by 2.

Q13. The normal approximation to \(\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)\) is poorest when
A) \(p=0.5\) and \(n=10\)
B) \(p=0.5\) and \(n\) is large
C) \(p\) is near 0 or 1 and \(n\) is small
D) \(n=1{,}000\) and \(p=0.5\)
* C
Why: Extreme proportions produce a highly skewed, bounded distribution that a symmetric normal fits badly. The condition \(np,n(1-p)\ge10\) guards against this.

Q14. The law of large numbers concerns convergence of
A) the variance to zero
B) the distribution of the mean to a normal distribution
C) the sample mean to the population mean
D) the sample size to infinity in probability
* C
Why: The LLN states that the average approaches the parameter. Convergence of the standardised mean's distribution to normality is the CLT.

Q15. A simulation uses 1,000,000 draws and reports a Monte Carlo standard error of 0.001. The
A) use a better random seed
B) use 4,000,000 draws
C) use 2,000,000 draws
D) change the estimator's units
* B
Why: Error scales as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so halving it needs four times the draws. Seeds affect reproducibility, not the size of the error.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** State the law of large numbers and explain what it does not promise.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For i.i.d. observations with finite mean, the sample mean converges in probability to \(\mu\) as
\(n\to\infty\). It does not promise that the sample mean is close to \(\mu\) for any particular
finite \(n\), nor that the sample becomes representative if the sampling is biased.
</details>

**S2.** State the central limit theorem in words and identify each ingredient.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For independent, identically distributed observations with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance
\(\sigma^2\), \((\bar X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\) tends in distribution to \(N(0,1)\). The
ingredients are independence, identical distribution, finite variance, and standardisation.
</details>

**S3.** Why are the LLN and the CLT not the same statement?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The LLN says where the average goes (to \(\mu\)); the CLT says how it gets there — the
fluctuations around \(\mu\), scaled by \(\sqrt n\), look normal. The LLN alone cannot produce
intervals; the CLT alone does not say the average converges.
</details>

**S4.** Why does the CLT apply to proportions?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A proportion is a mean of Bernoulli variables: \(\hat p=\frac1n\sum I_i\) with mean \(p\) and
variance \(p(1-p)\). Applying the CLT to that average gives approximate normality with standard
error \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\), which is the basis of proportion intervals and tests.
</details>

**S5.** Why is the continuity correction needed when a normal approximates a binomial?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A binomial takes whole numbers, and the normal is continuous. The event \(X\le45\) should cover
the bar up to 45.5; using 45 ignores half a unit of probability. The correction shifts the
boundary by 0.5, which matters most for small counts.
</details>

**S6.** Explain why a strongly skewed population requires a larger sample before the CLT is
useful.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Skewness means one tail contributes rare but large deviations, so individual observations are far
from symmetric. The Berry–Esseen bound links approximation error to the third moment divided by
\(\sigma^3\sqrt n\), so heavy skew needs larger \(n\) to average out.
</details>

**S7.** What does Chebyshev's inequality add that the CLT does not?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A finite-sample, distribution-free bound: \(P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge k\sigma/\sqrt n)\le1/k^2\). It
holds for every \(n\) and any distribution with finite variance, but it is loose; the CLT gives
sharper probabilities only asymptotically and only approximately.
</details>

**S8.** Why is Monte Carlo error proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\) rather than \(1/n\)?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A simulation estimate is an average of independent draws, and the standard error of an average is
\(\sigma/\sqrt n\). Quadrupling the simulations halves the error, which is why high-precision
simulation is expensive.
</details>

**S9.** A company averages 30 daily sales figures and treats the average as normal. When is that
justified?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When the daily figures are independent and have finite variance, and 30 is enough for the
prevailing skew. Sales often have weekly seasonality and promotional spikes, violating
independence; then the average's distribution is not the CLT's and a time-series or bootstrap
approach is needed.
</details>

**S10.** Why does the CLT not rescue inference from a biased sample?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The theorem describes variability around the sample's own expectation. If the sample is biased,
that expectation is not \(\mu\), so the normal approximation is centred on the wrong value and
intervals miss systematically. No sample size fixes bias.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** A fair die is rolled 100 times. Approximate \(P(\bar X>3.7)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mu=3.5\), \(\sigma^2=35/12=2.9167\), so \(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{2.9167/100}=0.1708\).
\(z=(3.7-3.5)/0.1708=1.171\), so \(P\approx1-0.879=0.121\). The approximation is decent at
\(n=100\) despite the die's discreteness.
</details>

**N2.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(100,0.5)\). Approximate \(P(X\le45)\) with a continuity
correction.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mu=50\), \(\sigma=\sqrt{25}=5\). Using 45.5:
\(z=(45.5-50)/5=-0.9\), so \(P\approx0.184\). Without the correction, \(z=-1\) gives 0.159 —
an error of about 2.5 percentage points.
</details>

**N3.** A population has \(\sigma=3\). How large must \(n\) be for the standard error of the mean
to be at most 0.5?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(3/\sqrt n\le0.5\) gives \(\sqrt n\ge6\), so \(n\ge36\). Always round up; \(n=36\) gives
exactly 0.5.
</details>

**N4.** The sum of 50 independent exponential variables with mean 1 is approximated as normal.
Estimate \(P(\text{sum}>60)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean \(=50\), variance \(=50\) (exponential variance is \(1/\lambda^2=1\)), SD \(=7.071\).
\(z=(60-50)/7.071=1.414\), so \(P\approx0.0787\). The sum is right-skewed at \(n=50\), so the
estimate is approximate.
</details>

**N5.** Use Chebyshev to bound \(P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge2\sigma/\sqrt n)\), and compare with the normal
value.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Chebyshev: \(P\le1/2^2=0.25\). The normal approximation gives
\(2(1-\Phi(2))=0.0455\). The bound is valid for every distribution but about five times
looser than the normal value.
</details>

**N6.** A simulation estimates a proportion near 0.4 from 2,500 draws. Compute the Monte Carlo
standard error.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.4\times0.6/2{,}500}=\sqrt{0.000096}=0.0098\), about 1 percentage point.
Doubling precision would require 10,000 draws.
</details>

**N7.** In a sample of 400, the true proportion is 0.3. Approximate \(P(\hat p>0.35)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.3\times0.7/400}=0.02291\); \(z=(0.35-0.3)/0.02291=2.182\), so
\(P\approx0.0146\). The normal approximation is adequate since \(np=120\) and \(n(1-p)=280\).
</details>

**N8.** A sample of 64 has \(\mu=10\), \(\sigma=4\). Approximate \(P(\bar X<9.2)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=4/8=0.5\); \(z=(9.2-10)/0.5=-1.6\), so \(P\approx0.0548\). Note the SE uses
\(\sqrt{64}=8\), not 64.
</details>

**N9.** How many Monte Carlo draws give a standard error of 0.005 for a proportion near 0.5?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(0.005=\sqrt{0.25/n}\) gives \(0.000025=0.25/n\), so \(n=0.25/0.000025=10{,}000\). The
\(1/\sqrt n\) rate makes high precision costly.
</details>

**N10.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(25)\). Approximate \(P(X\le20)\) with a continuity correction.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mu=\sigma^2=25\), \(\sigma=5\); using 20.5, \(z=(20.5-25)/5=-0.9\), so \(P\approx0.184\).
Without the correction, \(z=-1\) gives 0.159; the exact Poisson value is about 0.185, so the
correction helps.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A/B test: 5,000 users per arm, conversion 4.0 % versus 4.5 %. Use the CLT to judge
whether the difference is distinguishable from noise.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.04(0.96)/5000+0.045(0.955)/5000}=\sqrt{0.00000768+0.0000086}=0.00404\).
The difference 0.005 is 1.24 SE, p ≈ 0.22 — consistent with chance. Report the interval
(−0.003, 0.013) and note the test was underpowered for a 0.5-point effect.
</details>

**P2.** Revenue per user is extremely skewed, yet the dashboard shows a mean with a normal-based
interval from 10,000 users. Is that reasonable?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The CLT applies to the mean, so the interval can be approximately valid at \(n=10{,}000\) even
for skewed revenue, because the SE is small relative to the mean. But the interval says nothing
about the typical user; report the median and the tail, and check with a bootstrap, since rare
large purchases can still dominate.
</details>

**P3.** A poll of 1,000 gives 47 % support with a margin of error of 3 %. A newspaper writes
"support is below half". Is that supported?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The interval is roughly 44–50 %, which includes 50 %, so the data do not establish that support
is below half. The correct statement is that the result is consistent with a tied or slightly
negative position; the margin of error already accounts for sampling variability, not for
non-response or question wording.
</details>

**P4.** You can run 1,000 or 16,000 simulation replications of a risk model. How much does the
Monte Carlo error improve, and is the extra cost worth it?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Error scales as \(1/\sqrt n\), so 16 times the replications gives 4 times smaller error (0.25 of
the original). Whether it is worth it depends on the decision: if the estimate's SE is already
small relative to the decision threshold, the extra precision buys nothing.
</details>

**P5.** A production line's hourly output has mean 500 and SD 40. Over 36 hours, what is the
probability the average output falls below 485?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=40/\sqrt{36}=6.667\); \(z=(485-500)/6.667=-2.25\), so \(P\approx0.0122\). If
the hours are autocorrelated, the effective sample size is smaller and the true probability is
higher than this.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Use Chebyshev's inequality to prove the weak law of large numbers.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(E[\bar X]=\mu\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\). Chebyshev gives, for any
\(\varepsilon>0\), \(P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)\to0\) as
\(n\to\infty\). Hence \(\bar X\) converges in probability to \(\mu\). Finite variance and
independence (for the variance of the mean) are the only requirements.
</details>

**C2.** Sketch the moment-generating-function proof of the CLT for i.i.d. variables.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Let \(Y_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma\), so \(E[Y]=0\), \(E[Y^2]=1\). The standardised sum is
\(Z_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum Y_i\). Its mgf is
\(M_{Z_n}(t)=[M_Y(t/\sqrt n)]^n\). Expanding
\(M_Y(u)=1+u^2/2+o(u^2)\) gives
\(M_{Z_n}(t)=[1+t^2/(2n)+o(1/n)]^n\to e^{t^2/2}\), the mgf of \(N(0,1)\). Convergence of mgfs
implies convergence in distribution.
</details>

**C3.** Explain why the continuity correction improves the normal approximation, and quantify the
improvement for \(P(X\le45)\) when \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(100,0.5)\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
The normal assigns probability to the interval \((-\infty,45]\) of width measured continuously,
whereas the binomial's mass at 45 occupies \([44.5,45.5]\). Using 45.5 includes that mass.
Numerically, \(z=-1\) gives 0.1587 and \(z=-0.9\) gives 0.1841; the exact binomial value is
0.1841, so the correction removes an error of about 2.5 percentage points here.
</details>
