## A. Problem statement

A college claims that "attendance drives achievement" and wants to introduce a strict
80 % attendance rule. Before doing so, the academic committee asks for evidence: how
strong is the relationship between attendance and final marks in the current cohort, how
much of the variation does attendance explain, and what would the rule actually achieve?

## B. Background and context

Attendance policies are politically sensitive: they consume staff time, punish students
with jobs, and are justified by a correlation that is easy to observe and hard to
interpret. A data-literate analyst distinguishes three questions that are often merged:
(1) are attendance and marks associated in this cohort? (2) how well does attendance
predict a mark? (3) would enforcing attendance *cause* marks to rise? Only the first two
are answerable from observational records. This case builds the habit of answering 1 and
2 precisely before anyone legislates on 3.

## C. Dataset description

`attendance_achievement.csv` has 150 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `student_id` | integer | Anonymous identifier |
| `semester` | integer | 1–4 |
| `attendance_pct` | float | Attendance, 30–100 |
| `assignment_avg` | float | Average assignment mark, 0–100 |
| `final_mark` | float | Final mark, 0–100 |

**Generation method.** A latent motivation score is drawn from normal(0, 1). Attendance is
normal(74, 12) plus 6 motivation units, clipped to 30–100. The assignment average is
58 + 7·motivation + normal(0, 8). The final mark is 45 + 8·motivation + 0.22·attendance
+ normal(0, 7). Motivation therefore feeds attendance, assignments and finals, producing
a correlation that is *not* causal attendance → marks. This is deliberate: the dataset
is a laboratory for the confounding argument.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What is the distribution of attendance and of final marks?
2. How large is the correlation between attendance and the final mark?
3. What is the estimated slope, and how much of the variation does it explain?
4. Do low-attendance students (below 70 %) actually perform worse?
5. Is there a threshold above which marks jump, or is the relationship smooth?
6. What would a strict 80 % rule plausibly change, and what can this dataset not tell us?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Univariate summaries and distribution shape.
- Scatter plot, correlation coefficient, covariance.
- Least-squares line, slope and intercept, residuals.
- Coefficient of determination and the standard error of the estimate.
- Conditional means: comparing groups defined by attendance bands.
- Confounding, reverse causation, and selection effects.

## F. Mathematical formulation

Covariance and correlation:

\[
\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}),
\qquad r=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)}{s_x s_y}\in[-1,1].
\]

Least-squares slope and intercept:

\[
\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2},
\qquad \hat\beta_0=\bar{y}-\hat\beta_1\bar{x}.
\]

Residual standard error and \(R^2\):

\[
s_e=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_i \hat\varepsilon_i^2},
\qquad R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{SS_{res}}}{\mathrm{SS_{tot}}}=r^2 \text{ (simple regression).}
\]

A slope test uses \(t=\hat\beta_1/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom.

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Load and validate the file (ranges, missing values).
2. Summarise both variables: mean, standard deviation, quartiles, skewness.
3. Plot the scatter with a fitted line and a lowess smoother; look for curvature.
4. Compute the correlation and its confidence interval (Fisher z).
5. Fit the regression, report the slope with a 95 % CI and \(R^2\).
6. Split into attendance bands (< 70, 70–84, ≥ 85) and compare mean finals.
7. Inspect residuals for heteroscedasticity and influential points.
8. State the causal caveat and what design would settle it.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

data = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/attendance_achievement.csv")
print(data.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("shape:", data.shape, "| missing:", int(data.isna().sum().sum()))

print("\ndescriptives:")
print(data[["attendance_pct", "assignment_avg", "final_mark"]].describe().round(2).to_string())

r, p_value = stats.pearsonr(data["attendance_pct"], data["final_mark"])
print(f"\nPearson r = {r:.3f} (p = {p_value:.2e}), r^2 = {r**2:.3f}")

# Fisher z confidence interval for the correlation
n = len(data)
z = np.arctanh(r)
se = 1 / np.sqrt(n - 3)
print("95% CI for r:", [round(float(np.tanh(z + s * 1.96 * se)), 3) for s in (-1, 1)])

model = smf.ols("final_mark ~ attendance_pct", data=data).fit()
print(f"\nslope = {model.params['attendance_pct']:.3f}, intercept = {model.params['Intercept']:.2f}")
print("95% CI for slope:", model.conf_int().loc["attendance_pct"].round(3).tolist())
print(f"R2 = {model.rsquared:.3f}, residual SE = {np.sqrt(model.mse_resid):.2f}")

for level in (60, 75, 90):
    print(f"predicted final at {level}% attendance: {model.predict(pd.DataFrame({'attendance_pct': [level]}))[0]:.1f}")

data["band"] = pd.cut(data["attendance_pct"], [0, 70, 85, 100],
                      labels=["<70", "70-84", ">=85"])
print("\nby band:")
print(data.groupby("band", observed=True)["final_mark"]
      .agg(["count", "mean", "std"]).round(2).to_string())
```

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

```

**How to read it.** The cloud slopes upward but is wide: at any attendance level, finals
range over roughly 40 marks. The fitted line rises by about half a mark per attendance
point, which is visible but shallow relative to the scatter. The residual plot shows no
obvious funnel or curve, so a straight line is an acceptable summary; there are a few
large residuals at both ends, which is normal for a 150-student sample.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Mean attendance is 74.2 % (SD ≈ 12) and the mean final mark is 61.3 (SD ≈ 12).
- Attendance and final marks correlate at r = 0.582, so attendance explains 33.8 % of
  the variation in finals (R² = 0.338).
- The slope is 0.543: ten percentage points more attendance is associated with about 5.4
  extra marks.
- Students below 70 % attendance average 53.7 marks (n = 60) against 72.3 for those at
  85 % or above (n = 29) — a gap of about 19 marks.
- The relationship looks smooth rather than threshold-like; an 80 % rule would not "flip"
  a hidden switch.
- Because motivation drives both attendance and marks, the 0.543 slope overstates what a
  forced attendance policy would achieve.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — descriptives.** Attendance has mean 74.2 and standard deviation about 12,
with a minimum near 30, so a minority of students miss a great deal. Final marks average
61.3 with standard deviation 12.0. Both distributions are roughly symmetric; the final
mark has a slightly longer lower tail because a small number of students score in the
thirties.

**Step 2 — correlation.** r = 0.582, p = 5.9 × 10⁻¹⁵. This is a moderately strong
association for educational data, where many unmeasured factors (prior knowledge,
assessment format, personal circumstances) also matter. The Fisher-z 95 % interval is
approximately 0.46 to 0.68, so the sign and rough magnitude are not in doubt.

**Step 3 — regression.** final = 20.94 + 0.543 × attendance. The slope means ten attendance
points correspond to 5.4 marks. The 95 % interval for the slope is 0.42 to 0.67. R² = 0.338, so two-thirds of the variation
is not attendance-related. The residual standard error is about 9.8 marks, which is the
width of the "typical" miss when predicting an individual.

**Step 4 — predictions.** At 60 % attendance the model predicts 53.5 marks; at 90 %,
69.8 marks. Both predictions carry an interval of roughly ±19 marks (95 %), so they
describe group tendencies, not individuals.

**Step 5 — bands.** Below 70 %: 60 students averaging 53.7. Between 70 and 84 %: 61
students averaging 63.4. At 85 % or more: 29 students averaging 72.3. The step from the
lowest band to the middle is larger than from the middle to the top, but the bands
also differ in size and in unobserved motivation; the underlying relationship is
monotone and roughly linear, not a threshold. A rule that treats 79 % and 80 % as
categorically different has no support in these data.

**Step 6 — the causal question.** The generator makes motivation the common cause: it
raises attendance by 6 points per unit, raises assignment marks by 7 and raises finals
directly by 8. Regressing finals on attendance alone therefore attributes to attendance
some of motivation's effect. The correct reading is: "students who attend more tend to
score higher, and attendance is a useful *screening* variable", not "adding five
attendance points adds 2.7 marks". To estimate the policy effect you would need a
randomised incentive, a regression-discontinuity design around an attendance threshold,
or a panel model with student fixed effects.

**Step 7 — residual check.** The residual plot shows no strong pattern; the largest
residuals deviate by 30 marks above and 20 marks below prediction. These are not errors — they are the irreducible noise (σ = 7 in the
generator) plus the effect of motivation not captured by attendance.

**What a good report says.** "In this cohort, attendance and final marks are moderately
associated (r = 0.58); attendance explains about a third of the variation, and students
under 70 % attendance average 18 marks below those at 85 % or above. The association is
consistent with a causal benefit of attendance but does not demonstrate one, because
motivation plausibly drives both. A pilot with randomised attendance incentives would
test the policy directly."
