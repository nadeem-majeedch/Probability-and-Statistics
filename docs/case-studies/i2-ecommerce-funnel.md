## A. Problem statement

An online store's marketing manager has 60 days of traffic data split by acquisition
channel. She wants to know the overall conversion rate, whether channels differ, whether
conversion is improving over time, and how much traffic the email channel needs before its
conversion rate can be quoted with a ±0.5 percentage-point margin of error.

## B. Background and context

Conversion rate is a proportion estimated from a denominator that is itself random, which
makes it a good vehicle for binomial reasoning, confidence intervals and chi-square tests.
E-commerce teams compare channels constantly, usually informally and usually on too little
data. This case teaches the discipline: estimate each rate with an interval, test the
difference between channels properly, check for trend before declaring success, and size a
future experiment before running it.

## C. Dataset description

`daily_sessions.csv` has 180 rows: 60 days × 3 channels.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `date` | date | Calendar day, 2026-01-01 to 2026-03-01 |
| `channel` | text | `organic`, `paid`, `email` |
| `visitors` | integer | Sessions that day in that channel |
| `conversions` | integer | Sessions that ended in a purchase |

**Generation method.** Daily visitors are Poisson with a mean that grows 0.4 % per day
from a base of 900 (organic), 540 (paid) and 360 (email). Each visitor converts
independently with probability 0.032 (organic), 0.041 (paid) and 0.055 (email). The true
rates are therefore fixed; any apparent trend is sampling noise, which is exactly the
trap the case sets.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What is the overall conversion rate, and how precise is it?
2. How do the three channels compare, with confidence intervals?
3. Is the difference between organic and paid statistically significant?
4. Is conversion improving over the 60 days?
5. Is traffic growing, and does that change the interpretation of total conversions?
6. What sample size would give the email rate a ±0.5 pp margin of error?
7. Which channel should receive more budget, and on what evidence?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Binomial model and the sampling distribution of a proportion.
- Standard error \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) and the normal approximation.
- Wilson score interval, and when the normal approximation fails.
- Difference of proportions and its standard error.
- Chi-square test of homogeneity for a 2 × 3 table.
- Testing for trend (regression of daily rate on day, or a 2 × k trend test).
- Sample-size and margin-of-error calculations.

## F. Mathematical formulation

For channel \(j\) with \(n_j\) visitors and \(x_j\) conversions, \(\hat p_j=x_j/n_j\) and

\[
\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_j)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_j(1-\hat p_j)}{n_j}}.
\]

The difference of two independent proportions has

\[
\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_1-\hat p_2)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)}{n_2}}.
\]

Chi-square homogeneity statistic for a \(2\times k\) table:

\[
X^2=\sum_{i,j}\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}},\qquad
E_{ij}=\frac{(\text{row }i\text{ total})(\text{column }j\text{ total})}{N}.
\]

The sample size for a margin of error \(m\) at confidence \(1-\alpha\) is

\[
n\approx\frac{z^2\hat p(1-\hat p)}{m^2}.
\]

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Aggregate visitors and conversions by channel.
2. Compute rates with Wilson intervals.
3. Test homogeneity across channels with chi-square; follow up with pairwise z-tests.
4. Plot the daily rate with a 7-day moving average and a fitted trend.
5. Regress the daily rate on day to test for trend, weighting by visitors.
6. Compute the sample size needed for a ±0.5 pp interval on email.
7. Write the budget recommendation, stating the evidence and its limits.

## H. Student tasks

1. Compute the overall and per-channel conversion rates.
2. Give Wilson intervals for each channel rate. Which channels' intervals overlap?
3. Run the chi-square test and interpret it.
4. Compare organic and paid with a two-proportion z-test; report the CI for the difference.
5. Fit daily conversion rate on day. Is there evidence of improvement?
6. Compute the visitors needed for ±0.5 pp on the email rate.
7. Write three sentences recommending a budget shift, with numbers.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

sessions = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/daily_sessions.csv", parse_dates=["date"])
print(sessions.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("days:", sessions.date.nunique(), "| rows:", len(sessions))

by_channel = sessions.groupby("channel")[["visitors", "conversions"]].sum()
by_channel["rate"] = by_channel.conversions / by_channel.visitors
print("\nby channel:")
print(by_channel.assign(rate=lambda d: (d.rate * 100).round(2)).to_string())

total = by_channel[["visitors", "conversions"]].sum()
print(f"\noverall: {total.conversions:,} of {total.visitors:,} = "
      f"{total.conversions / total.visitors:.4f}")


def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    denom = 1 + z**2 / n
    centre = (p + z**2 / (2 * n)) / denom
    half = z * np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z**2 / (4 * n**2)) / denom
    return centre - half, centre + half


print("\nWilson 95% intervals:")
for channel, row in by_channel.iterrows():
    lo, hi = wilson(row.conversions, row.visitors)
    print(f"  {channel:8s} {row.rate:.4f}  [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]")

organic, paid = by_channel.loc["organic"], by_channel.loc["paid"]
diff = paid.rate - organic.rate
se = np.sqrt(organic.rate * (1 - organic.rate) / organic.visitors
             + paid.rate * (1 - paid.rate) / paid.visitors)
print(f"\npaid - organic = {diff:+.4f}, 95% CI "
      f"[{diff - 1.96 * se:+.4f}, {diff + 1.96 * se:+.4f}], z = {diff / se:.2f}")

table = np.column_stack([
    by_channel.visitors - by_channel.conversions,
    by_channel.conversions,
])
chi2, p_chi, dof, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(table)
print(f"chi-square homogeneity: X2 = {chi2:.1f}, df = {dof}, p = {p_chi:.2e}")

daily = sessions.groupby("date")[["visitors", "conversions"]].sum()
daily["rate"] = daily.conversions / daily.visitors
trend = smf.ols("rate ~ day_index", data=daily.assign(day_index=np.arange(len(daily)))).fit()
print(f"\ndaily rate trend: slope = {trend.params.day_index:+.6f} per day, "
      f"p = {trend.pvalues.day_index:.3f}")

m = 0.005
p_email = by_channel.loc["email", "rate"]
n_needed = (1.96**2 * p_email * (1 - p_email)) / m**2
print(f"visitors needed for +/-0.5pp on email: {n_needed:,.0f} "
      f"(currently {by_channel.loc['email', 'visitors']:,.0f})")
```

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8))

daily["moving"] = daily.rate.rolling(7).mean()

**How to read it.** Email sits clearly highest, paid in the middle and organic lowest, and
the error bars separate email from the other two while organic and paid overlap only
partly. The daily series is noisy: individual days swing between 2 % and 6 %, and the
7-day average drifts up and down without a sustained direction. The visual impression of a
"rising trend" in the last two weeks is within the noise band.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Overall conversion is 3.89 % (4,706 of 121,092 sessions).
- By channel: organic 3.23 %, paid 3.89 %, email 5.51 %.
- Chi-square across channels is 238.7 (df = 2, p < 10⁻⁵⁰): the channels differ.
- Paid beats organic by 0.66 pp (95 % CI 0.42–0.90 pp, z = 5.3), a real but modest gap.
- Email converts at 5.51 % with a 95 % interval of 5.22–5.79 %; it is the best channel per
  session but has the smallest volume.
- Daily conversion shows no significant trend (slope +0.000026 per day, p = 0.43) — the
  apparent improvement is noise.
- Giving the email rate a ±0.5 pp interval requires about 8,000 visitors; it already has
  24,174, so its rate is known to about ±0.3 pp.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — the funnel.** Visitors: organic 60,428, paid 36,490, email 24,174. Conversions:
1,954, 1,421, 1,331. Rates: 3.23 %, 3.89 %, 5.51 %. Overall 3.89 %. Email converts 71 %
better than organic, which at this scale is a large relative difference.

**Step 2 — precision.** Wilson intervals: organic 3.09–3.38 %, paid 3.70–4.10 %, email
5.22–5.79 %. Organic and paid intervals do not overlap, so the difference is unlikely to
be chance; the paid-minus-organic z-test confirms it (z = 5.3, CI 0.42–0.90 pp). Email's interval is
slightly wider than paid's because it has fewer sessions, despite the higher rate.

**Step 3 — homogeneity test.** The 2 × 3 chi-square gives X² = 238.7, p < 10⁻⁵⁰. This is
a test of whether the three rates are equal; it rejects strongly. It does not say which
channels differ, which is why the pairwise comparison is needed. With three channels the
multiplicity correction is minor (Bonferroni threshold 0.0167), and the paid-versus-organic
p-value is far below it.

**Step 4 — trend.** The daily rate varies by about 0.4 pp around a mean of 3.9 pp, which
is what binomial noise alone would produce with roughly 2,000 sessions a day. The regression slope is +0.000026 per day (about +0.16 pp over
the whole period) with p = 0.43. The correct conclusion is "no evidence of a trend"; the
7-day average's wiggle is sampling noise. An analyst who cherry-picked the last ten days
could tell a growth story, which is precisely the error this case is designed to expose.

**Step 5 — volume versus rate.** Total conversions are organic 1,954, paid 1,421, email
1,331. Email wins on rate but loses on volume; organic wins on volume and loses on rate.
Budget decisions must multiply rate by volume and by margin per conversion, not compare
rates alone. If email can be scaled without degrading its rate — an assumption that needs
testing — it is the best marginal investment.

**Step 6 — sample size.** For a ±0.5 pp interval at 95 % confidence with p̂ = 0.0551,
\(n = 1.96^2 \times 0.0551 \times 0.9449 / 0.005^2 \approx 7{,}995\) visitors. Email already
has 24,174, so the interval is tighter than requested; the same calculation for organic
(60,428 visitors, p = 0.0323) gives about ±0.14 pp, which is why the paid–organic
difference is detectable.

**Step 7 — recommendation.** "Email converts at 5.5 % (95 % CI 5.2–5.8 %), significantly
above paid (3.9 %) and organic (3.2 %). Its volume is the smallest, so scaling it is the
highest-leverage test available, provided the rate holds as volume grows. Daily conversion
shows no trend, so the recent improvement in the 7-day average is noise and should not be
reported as progress." That is a defensible three-sentence memo.

**Caveats.** (i) Sessions are not people: the same visitor may create several sessions and
convert once, which inflates apparent rates and understates uncertainty. (ii) Channel is
not randomly assigned — email subscribers are self-selected and probably more engaged, so
the 5.5 % is not the causal effect of email marketing. (iii) Conversion does not equal
profit; margin per order matters. The A/B case study I7 shows how to test a change with
random assignment, which is the only design that supports a causal claim.
