## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The least-squares slope in simple regression is
A) \(r\,s_y/s_x\)
B) \(r\,s_x/s_y\)
C) \(r^2\)
D) \(\bar y/\bar x\)
* A
Why: The slope rescales the correlation by the ratio of standard deviations, so it carries units of \(y\) per unit of \(x\). The inverse ratio gives the slope of the reverse regression, which is a different line.

Q2. The intercept \(\hat\beta_0\) equals
A) \(\hat\beta_1\)
B) \(\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\)
C) \(\bar x-\hat\beta_1\bar y\)
D) \(\bar y\)
* B
Why: The line passes through the point of means, so the intercept follows once the slope is known. Setting it to \(\bar y\) assumes a zero slope.

Q3. \(R^2\) is
A) the slope squared
B) the residual variance
C) the proportion of variance in \(y\) explained by the regression
D) the correlation coefficient
* C
Why: \(R^2=1-\mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}\). In simple regression it equals \(r^2\), not \(r\), and it says nothing about causal validity or model adequacy.

Q4. A residual is
A) \(y_i-\hat y_i\)
B) \(\hat y_i-y_i\) always
C) the fitted value
D) the slope times \(x_i\)
* A
Why: The residual is the vertical distance from the observation to the fitted line. Its sign matters: positive means the model under-predicts.

Q5. The standard error of the slope is used to
A) remove outliers
B) test whether the slope differs from zero
C) compute \(R^2\)
D) centre the predictors
* B
Why: \(t=\hat\beta_1/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) with \(n-2\) df tests \(H_0:\beta_1=0\), and the same SE gives the slope's confidence interval.

Q6. Adding an irrelevant predictor to a regression typically
A) decreases \(R^2\)
B) increases \(R^2\) slightly without improving out-of-sample fit
C) leaves \(R^2\) unchanged
D) increases the residual variance
* B
Why: \(R^2\) cannot fall when a variable is added, because least squares fits at least as well. Adjusted \(R^2\) and cross-validation penalise the useless term.

Q7. Extrapolating far beyond the observed range of \(x\) is risky because
A) \(R^2\) becomes negative
B) the intercept changes
C) residuals become zero
D) the linear relationship may not hold outside the data
* D
Why: The model is supported only where data exist. Beyond that, curvature, saturation or a change of regime can make predictions wildly wrong, however good the in-sample fit.

Q8. A leverage point is an observation
A) with the largest \(y\)
B) at the mean of \(x\)
C) with an unusual \(x\) value
D) with a large residual only
* C
Why: Leverage depends on distance from the centre of the predictor space. A high-leverage point can pull the fitted line strongly toward it, whether or not its residual is large.

Q9. The correlation coefficient is unaffected by
A) swapping \(x\) and \(y\)
B) changing the units of \(x\)
C) both A and B
D) adding outliers
* C
Why: Correlation is symmetric and unit-free, so relabelling axes or rescaling leaves it unchanged. Outliers, however, can change it substantially.

Q10. A regression of \(y\) on \(x\) has \(R^2=0.04\) with \(n=1{,}000\). The slope's p-value is
A) The slope is zero
B) The result is a calculation error
C) The model explains little variance, though the slope may be non-zero
D) The model is excellent for prediction
* C
Why: With large \(n\), tiny effects become significant. Four per cent explained variance means the predictions are barely better than the mean, despite statistical significance.

Q11. Multicollinearity primarily affects
A) the stability and standard errors of individual coefficients
B) the fitted values severely
C) the sample size
D) the residuals' mean
* A
Why: Correlated predictors make coefficients hard to separate, inflating standard errors and flipping signs. Predictions can remain accurate even when individual coefficients are unstable.

Q12. The regression assumptions include
A) normality of the predictors
B) zero correlation between \(x\) and \(y\)
C) equal numbers of high and low \(x\)
D) linearity in parameters, independent errors, constant variance, and approximate normality for inference
* D
Why: The model assumes the mean of \(y\) is linear in the parameters and the errors are independent with constant variance; normality matters for exact small-sample tests, not for unbiasedness.

Q13. A prediction interval for a new observation is wider than a confidence interval for the mean
A) it is one-sided
B) the mean is unknown
C) it includes both estimation uncertainty and individual noise
D) it uses a smaller critical value
* C
Why: The prediction variance adds \(\sigma^2\) to the variance of the fitted mean, so it is always wider — often much wider.

Q14. Standardising both variables before regression makes the slope equal to
A) the intercept
B) the correlation coefficient
C) \(R^2\)
D) 1
* B
Why: With unit standard deviations, \(\hat\beta_1=r\,s_y/s_x=r\). This is why standardised coefficients are comparable across predictors.

Q15. A curvilinear relationship between \(x\) and \(y\) will typically produce
A) zero residuals
B) a perfect fit
C) an unbiased slope estimate with correct standard errors
D) a pattern in the residuals even if the slope is significant
* D
Why: A linear fit to a curve leaves systematic residual structure (a U-shape), signalling misspecification; add polynomial terms or transform the variables.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain what the slope means in words, including its units.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The slope is the estimated change in the mean of \(y\) for a one-unit increase in \(x\), in units
of \(y\) per unit of \(x\). It is an average rate over the observed range and does not imply that
changing \(x\) causes \(y\) to change.
</details>

**S2.** Why does the least-squares line pass through the point \((\bar x,\bar y)\)?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The normal equations force the residuals to sum to zero and to be uncorrelated with \(x\). The
first condition gives \(\bar y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\bar x\), so the fitted line passes through
the means. This is why centring predictors simplifies the interpretation of the intercept.
</details>

**S3.** What does \(R^2\) measure, and what are its limitations?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It measures the proportion of total variance in \(y\) explained by the fitted model. It can be
inflated by adding predictors, does not indicate causal validity, says nothing about whether the
functional form is right, and a low \(R^2\) does not rule out a useful relationship.
</details>

**S4.** Distinguish between a confidence interval for the mean response and a prediction interval
for a new observation.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The confidence interval describes uncertainty about the average \(y\) at a given \(x\); the
prediction interval describes uncertainty about a single future \(y\), adding the residual
variance. The prediction interval is always wider, increasingly so at the edges of the data.
</details>

**S5.** Why is extrapolation dangerous even with a high \(R^2\)?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because \(R^2\) measures fit only within the observed range. Outside it, the assumed linear form
may fail — saturation, thresholds or different mechanisms appear — and small slope errors
compound over distance.
</details>

**S6.** What is the difference between correlation and regression?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Correlation measures the strength of linear association symmetrically, with no direction or units.
Regression models the mean of one variable as a function of another, giving a slope with units, a
prediction rule, and residuals that can be checked against assumptions.
</details>

**S7.** How would you detect non-constant variance in a regression, and what would you do about
it?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Plot residuals against fitted values; a funnel shape indicates heteroscedasticity. Remedies
include transforming the response (log or square root), weighted least squares, robust standard
errors, or modelling the variance explicitly.
</details>

**S8.** Why can a regression have a significant slope but poor predictive power?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Significance reflects whether the slope differs from zero relative to its standard error, which
shrinks with \(n\). Predictive power depends on how much variance is explained and on the noise
level; a precise but tiny slope explains little and predicts poorly.
</details>

**S9.** What is the effect of a single high-leverage outlier on the fitted line, and how do you
check for it?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It can rotate the line substantially, changing the slope and intercept and inflating or deflating
\(R^2\). Check leverage (hat values), Cook's distance and studentised residuals, and refit without
the point to see whether conclusions change — never delete it silently.
</details>

**S10.** Why is it wrong to interpret an intercept as a baseline when \(x=0\) is outside the data
range?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The intercept is the fitted mean at \(x=0\), which may be an impossible or unobserved value; the
linear form may not hold there. Centre the predictor or report the mean response at a meaningful
reference value instead.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** \(r=0.6\), \(s_x=4\), \(s_y=10\). Compute the regression slope of \(y\) on \(x\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\hat\beta_1=r\,s_y/s_x=0.6\times10/4=1.5\). A one-unit increase in \(x\) is associated with
a 1.5-unit increase in the mean of \(y\).
</details>

**N2.** With N1, \(\bar x=10\) and \(\bar y=50\). Compute the intercept and predict \(y\) at
\(x=12\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\hat\beta_0=50-1.5(10)=35\). Prediction: \(35+1.5(12)=53\). The prediction at the mean
\(x=10\) returns \(\bar y=50\), as it must.
</details>

**N3.** A regression gives \(R^2=0.49\) with \(r>0\). Compute \(r\) and the proportion of
unexplained variance.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(r=\sqrt{0.49}=0.7\) (positive). Unexplained variance \(=1-0.49=0.51\), i.e. **51 %**. Half the
variation in \(y\) remains in the residuals.
</details>

**N4.** \(\hat\beta_1=2.0\), \(\mathrm{SE}=0.5\), \(n=22\). Compute the \(t\)-statistic and its
degrees of freedom.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(t=2.0/0.5=4.0\) with \(n-2=20\) df. The two-sided p-value is below 0.001, so the slope
is clearly non-zero.
</details>

**N5.** Using N4, give an approximate 95 % interval for the slope (use \(t\approx2.086\)).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(2.0\pm2.086(0.5)=2.0\pm1.043=0.957\) to \(3.043\). The interval excludes zero, consistent
with the test.
</details>

**N6.** A model predicts 80 but the observed value is 74. Compute the residual and say whether the
model over- or under-predicts.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Residual \(=74-80=-6\). A negative residual means the model over-predicts. Residuals sum to
zero across the fitted sample when an intercept is included.
</details>

**N7.** \(x\) has mean 5 and SD 2; \(y\) has mean 20 and SD 5; \(r=-0.8\). Compute the slope and
interpret the sign.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\hat\beta_1=-0.8\times5/2=-2.0\). The negative correlation means higher \(x\) is associated
with lower mean \(y\), by 2 units per unit of \(x\).
</details>

**N8.** A regression on 30 observations has \(\mathrm{SST}=500\) and \(\mathrm{SSE}=150\).
Compute \(R^2\) and the residual standard error (approximately).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(R^2=1-150/500=0.70\). Residual variance \(=150/(30-2)=5.357\), so the residual standard
error is \(\sqrt{5.357}=2.31\), in the units of \(y\).
</details>

**N9.** Two predictors have correlation 0.95. What is the variance inflation factor for each, and
what does it imply?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{VIF}=1/(1-r^2)=1/(1-0.9025)=10.26\). A VIF above 10 is conventionally considered
severe; coefficient standard errors are inflated by \(\sqrt{10.26}\approx3.2\).
</details>

**N10.** A model predicts \(y=100\) at \(x=50\), but the data range only from \(x=10\) to 30.
What is the problem?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
The prediction is an **extrapolation** 20 units beyond the largest observed \(x\). The linear
relationship may not hold there, and the prediction interval would be very wide. Report the data
range and avoid the claim.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** Attendance and marks have \(r=0.55\) across 120 students. A dean proposes a policy to
raise attendance to raise marks. What do you advise?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Correlation does not establish that attendance causes marks: motivated or better-prepared
students attend more and score higher. Also \(r^2=0.30\), so most variation is unexplained.
Suggest a randomised encouragement design or an analysis controlling for prior attainment, and
report the slope with its interval rather than assuming a policy effect.
</details>

**P2.** A house-price model has \(R^2=0.92\) but performs poorly on recent sales. Give three
plausible reasons.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
(1) Temporal drift: prices and the relationship changed after the training period. (2)
Distribution shift in features (new neighbourhoods, larger houses). (3) Leakage or an
over-represented segment in training. Validate on time-based holdouts, monitor residual drift, and
refit or add time-varying features.
</details>

**P3.** Two predictors, ad spend and season, are highly correlated. The ad-spend coefficient
changes sign between models. Explain and recommend.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Multicollinearity: the data cannot separate the two effects, so coefficients are unstable and
signs unreliable. Report joint effects or use regularisation (ridge), drop one predictor if
redundant, or design an experiment that varies spend independently of season.
</details>

**P4.** A regression of salary on experience has a positive slope, but the residuals fan out as
experience grows. What is wrong and what would you do?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Heteroscedasticity: the variance of salary increases with experience. Standard errors and
intervals are unreliable; use robust standard errors, transform salary (log), or model the
variance. Check whether seniority bands explain the fanning.
</details>

**P5.** You must report the effect of a tutoring programme on marks, controlling for prior
attainment. Outline the regression and the key caveat.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Regress final mark on programme indicator and prior mark, reporting the programme coefficient with
its interval. The caveat is selection: participation may correlate with unmeasured motivation.
Random assignment would identify the effect; otherwise state the assumption of no unmeasured
confounding and check sensitivity.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive the least-squares slope and intercept for simple linear regression.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Minimise \(S(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2\). The normal equations are
\(\sum(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)=0\) and \(\sum x_i(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)=0\). The first gives
\(\beta_0=\bar y-\beta_1\bar x\). Substituting into the second and simplifying gives
\(\beta_1=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}\), which equals \(r s_y/s_x\).
</details>

**C2.** Show that \(R^2=r^2\) in simple linear regression.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \(\mathrm{SSR}=\sum(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2=\hat\beta_1^2\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\) since
\(\hat y_i-\bar y=\hat\beta_1(x_i-\bar x)\). With
\(\hat\beta_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\), \(\mathrm{SSR}=\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}}\). Dividing by
\(\mathrm{SST}=S_{yy}\) gives \(R^2=\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}}=r^2\).
</details>

**C3.** Derive the variance of the prediction error for a new observation and explain why the
interval widens away from \(\bar x\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
For a new \(x_0\), \(\hat y_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0\) has variance
\(\sigma^2\left[\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right]\). The new observation adds its own
\(\sigma^2\), giving prediction variance
\(\sigma^2\left[1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right]\). The quadratic term in
\((x_0-\bar x)\) means precision is best at the centre of the data and deteriorates toward the
edges.
</details>
