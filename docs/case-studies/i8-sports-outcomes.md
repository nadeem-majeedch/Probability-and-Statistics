## A. Problem statement

A sports analytics startup models football matches with goals as Poisson counts. Using 180
simulated matches between six teams, the analyst must estimate the home advantage, test
whether a Poisson model fits the distribution of total goals, compute the probability of
over 2.5 goals and of both teams scoring, and explain where the model fails.

## B. Background and context

Sports modelling popularised the Poisson goal model, and it is a good teaching example of
discrete distributions, maximum likelihood and goodness-of-fit testing. It is also a
cautionary example: goals are not independent across a match, team strength varies, and
low-scoring games create dependence between the two sides. The case asks students to fit
the model, test it honestly, and describe its failure modes — exactly the habit required
when a tidy model meets messy reality.

## C. Dataset description

`football_matches.csv` has 180 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `home_team` | text | One of six invented clubs |
| `away_team` | text | One of six invented clubs |
| `home_goals` | integer | Goals scored by the home side |
| `away_goals` | integer | Goals scored by the away side |
| `result` | text | `home_win`, `draw`, `away_win` |

**Generation method.** Each match draws a home and away team without replacement from six
clubs, then samples goals as independent Poisson variables with means 1.55 (home) and 1.05
(away). The result label follows from the goals. There is no team-specific strength in the
generator, so any apparent team effect is noise — a deliberate trap.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What are the home-win, draw and away-win frequencies?
2. What are the mean goals for home and away sides, and the mean total?
3. Does a Poisson(2.556) model fit the distribution of total goals?
4. What are the estimated probabilities of over 2.5 goals and of both teams scoring?
5. Is the home advantage statistically significant?
6. Do the six teams differ, or is the variation consistent with chance?
7. Where does the Poisson independence assumption fail?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Poisson distribution: PMF, mean = variance, additivity.
- Sum of independent Poissons is Poisson.
- Maximum likelihood estimation of a rate.
- Chi-square goodness-of-fit with pooling of sparse cells.
- Binomial test for home-win proportion.
- Multiple comparisons across teams.
- Model assumptions versus data: independence, heterogeneity, over-dispersion.

## F. Mathematical formulation

Poisson PMF and its properties:

\[
P(X=k)=\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!},\qquad
E[X]=\mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda,\qquad
X+Y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_X+\lambda_Y).
\]

If home and away goals are independent Poissons with \(\lambda_H,\lambda_A\), the total is
Poisson with \(\lambda_H+\lambda_A\), and

\[
P(\text{over }2.5)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!},\qquad
P(\text{both score})=(1-e^{-\lambda_H})(1-e^{-\lambda_A}).
\]

Goodness of fit:

\[
X^2=\sum_k \frac{(O_k-E_k)^2}{E_k}\ \sim\ \chi^2_{K-1-p},
\]

with cells pooled so that expected counts are at least five, and \(p\) estimated parameters.

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Tabulate results and compute the home-win proportion with an interval.
2. Compute goal means and variances for each side and for the total.
3. Fit a Poisson to total goals; compute expected frequencies and pool sparse cells.
4. Run the chi-square goodness-of-fit test.
5. Compute over-2.5 and both-teams-score probabilities from the fitted model and compare
   with the observed frequencies.
6. Test the home advantage with a binomial test on wins versus losses (excluding draws).
7. Compare team records with a chi-square test of homogeneity.
8. Discuss which assumptions are violated and what a better model would add.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

matches = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/football_matches.csv")
print(matches.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("matches:", len(matches), "| results:",
      matches.result.value_counts(normalize=True).round(3).to_dict())

home_goals, away_goals = matches.home_goals, matches.away_goals
total = home_goals + away_goals
print(f"\nmean home {home_goals.mean():.3f}, away {away_goals.mean():.3f}, "
      f"total {total.mean():.3f}")
print(f"variance of total = {total.var(ddof=1):.3f} "
      f"(VMR = {total.var(ddof=1) / total.mean():.2f})")
print(f"over 2.5 goals observed: {(total > 2.5).mean():.3f}")
print(f"both teams scored observed: {((home_goals > 0) & (away_goals > 0)).mean():.3f}")

lam = total.mean()
observed = [int((total == k).sum()) for k in range(5)] + [int((total >= 5).sum())]
expected = [stats.poisson.pmf(k, lam) * len(total) for k in range(5)]
expected.append(len(total) - sum(expected))
print("\ntotal goals: k=0..4,5+")
print("observed:", observed)
print("expected:", [round(e, 1) for e in expected])
# one parameter (lambda) is estimated from these data, hence ddof=1
chi2, p_value = stats.chisquare(observed, expected, ddof=1)
print(f"chi-square = {chi2:.2f}, df = {len(observed) - 2}, p = {p_value:.4f}")

p_over = 1 - stats.poisson.cdf(2, lam)
p_both = (1 - np.exp(-home_goals.mean())) * (1 - np.exp(-away_goals.mean()))
print(f"\nmodel over 2.5: {p_over:.3f} vs observed {(total > 2.5).mean():.3f}")
print(f"model both score: {p_both:.3f} vs observed "
      f"{((home_goals > 0) & (away_goals > 0)).mean():.3f}")

wins = (matches.result == "home_win").sum()
losses = (matches.result == "away_win").sum()
print(f"\nhome wins {wins}, away wins {losses}")
print("binomial test home vs away wins:",
      [round(float(x), 3) for x in stats.binomtest(wins, wins + losses, 0.5).proportion_ci()],
      "p =", round(stats.binomtest(wins, wins + losses, 0.5).pvalue, 4))

home_records = matches.groupby("home_team").apply(
    lambda d: (d.home_goals > d.away_goals).sum(), include_groups=False)
print("\nhome wins by team:")
print(home_records.sort_values(ascending=False).to_string())
```

**How to read it.** The observed distribution has more 0- and 1-goal matches and a heavier
tail than the fitted Poisson, which is the classic signature of over-dispersion: matches
cluster into dull and wild games rather than behaving like independent coin flips. The
right panel shows the home side shifted one goal to the right, which is the home advantage
in raw form.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Results: 50.0 % home wins, 22.2 % draws, 27.8 % away wins.
- Mean goals: home 1.544, away 1.011, total 2.556; variance of the total is 3.39, so
  VMR = 1.33 — mildly over-dispersed.
- The Poisson fit gives χ² = 10.63 on 4 degrees of freedom, p = 0.031: rejected at 5 %, so
  the model is a rough approximation rather than an adequate description.
- The model predicts P(over 2.5) = 0.470 against an observed 0.461, and P(both score) =
  0.500 against an observed 0.483 — mild overstatement from the independence assumption.
- Of 140 decided matches, home sides won 90 (64.3 %), significantly above 50 %
  (binomial p = 0.0009).
- The six teams' home records range from 10 wins (Lions, 27 home games) to 23 (Wolves, 37),
  but a chi-square test gives p = 0.66: the variation is consistent with chance, so no team
  effect should be claimed.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — results.** 90 home wins, 40 draws, 50 away wins. The home-win share of all
matches is 50.0 %; of decided matches it is 90/140 = 64.3 %. The latter is the right
comparison for a home-advantage test, because draws are neither wins nor losses.

**Step 2 — means and variance.** Home 1.544, away 1.011. The difference of 0.53 goals per
game is the home advantage. Total goals average 2.556 with variance 3.39, so VMR = 1.33:
slightly more variable than Poisson, which forbids clustering.

**Step 3 — goodness of fit.** Observed totals (0+: 19, 1: 43, 2: 35, 3: 35, 4: 19, 5+:
29) against expected (14.0, 35.7, 45.6, 38.9, 24.8, 20.9). The model over-predicts 2-goal
games (45.6 versus 35) and under-predicts both 0-goal and 5+-goal games. χ² = 10.63 with
4 degrees of freedom (six cells, one estimated parameter), p = 0.031: the Poisson model is
rejected at the 5 % level. The residuals show the reason — too few 2-goal games and too
many thrashings, the over-dispersion noted above.

**Step 4 — derived probabilities.** From λ = 2.556: P(0) = 0.078, P(1) = 0.199, P(2) =
0.254, so P(over 2.5) = 1 − 0.531 = 0.469, against an observed 0.461. For both teams
scoring, independence gives (1 − e^{−1.544})(1 − e^{−1.011}) = 0.786 × 0.636 = 0.500,
against an observed 0.483 — a small overstatement. Independence is the weak point: real
matches have correlated scoring (a team that is losing attacks more), which inflates the
frequency of 0–0 and 1–0 relative to the independent model.

**Step 5 — home advantage.** 90 home wins against 50 away wins among 140 decided matches.
A binomial test against 0.5 gives p ≈ 0.0009, so the home advantage is real. Its size —
0.53 goals per game — is the quantity an analyst would put into a prediction model.

**Step 6 — team effects.** Home records range from 10 wins in 27 home games (Lions) to 23
in 37 (Wolves). A chi-square test of homogeneity across six teams gives χ² = 7.68 on 10
degrees of freedom, p = 0.66 — entirely consistent with the generator's lack of team
strengths. Ranking teams by raw win count
would be pure noise-chasing: with this many matches, the standard error of a win
proportion is about 0.09, so a 15 % gap between the best and worst record is expected by
chance alone.

**Step 7 — where the model fails.** (i) Independence between the two sides' goals — real
scores are negatively correlated through game state; (ii) independence across matches —
form, injuries and weather cluster; (iii) constant rates — team strength varies, which is
the largest misspecification; (iv) Poisson forbids the extra zeros that arise from
defensive matches. A better model uses team-specific attack and defence parameters (a
Poisson regression with team effects), or a bivariate Poisson / Dixon-Coles correction for
low scores, and validates on held-out seasons.

**Why this matters for a data scientist.** The Poisson goal model is a *useful
approximation*, and the case shows how to state that precisely: it reproduces the mean and
the over-2.5 probability, it slightly overstates both-teams-score, and its fit statistic is
borderline. A model is not "right" or "wrong"; it is adequate for some purposes and not
others, and the analyst's job is to say which.
