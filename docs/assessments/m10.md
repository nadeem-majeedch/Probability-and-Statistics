## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The normal distribution is determined by
A) its range
B) its skewness
C) its mean and standard deviation
D) its median only
* C
Why: \(\mu\) sets the location and \(\sigma\) the spread; every other feature follows. The normal has zero skewness, so skewness cannot be a parameter.

Q2. For a normal variable, the interval \(\mu\pm2\sigma\) contains about
A) 68 %
B) 95 %
C) 99.7 %
D) 50 %
* B
Why: The 68–95–99.7 rule: one SD holds about 68 %, two about 95 %, three about 99.7 %. The exact two-SD figure is 95.45 %.

Q3. The exponential distribution is memoryless, which means
A) it has bounded support
B) \(P(X>s+t\mid X>s)=P(X>t)\)
C) its mean equals its variance
D) it is symmetric
* B
Why: Conditional on having survived to \(s\), the remaining wait has the same distribution as a fresh wait. This is a consequence of the constant hazard rate.

Q4. For \(X\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,10)\), the mean and variance are
A) 5 and 25
B) 10 and 100
C) 5 and 2.89
D) 5 and 8.33
* D
Why: Mean \((a+b)/2=5\); variance \((b-a)^2/12=100/12=8.33\), so the SD is 2.89. Answer D gives the SD where the variance was asked.

Q5. For a continuous random variable, \(P(X=x)\) is
A) zero
B) \(F(x)\)
C) always positive
D) \(f(x)\)
* A
Why: A point has zero width, so its area under the density is zero. Probabilities are computed over intervals.

Q6. \(P(Z<1.5)\) for a standard normal variable is about
A) 0.8413
B) 0.9772
C) 0.9332
D) 0.0668
* C
Why: The cumulative standard normal at 1.5 is 0.9332. The value 0.0668 is the upper tail, and 0.8413 corresponds to \(z=1\).

Q7. If \(\ln X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then the median of \(X\) is
A) \(\mu\)
B) \(e^\mu\)
C) \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\)
D) \(\mu^2\)
* B
Why: The median is invariant under the monotone transformation, so it is \(e^{\mu}\). The mean is \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\), larger because the lognormal is right-skewed.

Q8. The mean of an exponential distribution with rate \(\lambda\) is
A) \(\sqrt{\lambda}\)
B) \(\lambda\)
C) \(1/\lambda\)
D) \(1/\lambda^2\)
* C
Why: The mean waiting time is the reciprocal of the rate. With two events per hour on average, the mean wait is half an hour.

Q9. The gamma distribution generalises the exponential by
A) allowing a shape parameter for the number of events
B) adding a location shift only
C) being discrete
D) removing the scale parameter
* A
Why: With shape \(k\), gamma models the time to the \(k\)-th event; \(k=1\) gives the exponential. The scale parameter sets the time units.

Q10. A linear combination of independent normal variables is
A) exponential
B) uniform
C) always skewed
D) normal
* D
Why: Normality is closed under linear combinations: \(\sum a_iX_i\) is normal with the corresponding mean and variance. This is what makes normal-based inference tractable.

Q11. For a standard normal \(Z\), \(P(Z<-1)\) equals
A) \(P(Z<1)\)
B) 0.5
C) \(1-P(Z<1)\)
D) \(P(Z>1)\)
* D
Why: Symmetry about zero makes the lower tail at \(-1\) equal the upper tail at 1, about 0.1587. Answer D equals \(P(Z>1)\) numerically, which is the same value but the symmetry statement is the reason.

Q12. A probability density must satisfy
A) \(f(x)\le1\)
B) \(f(x)\ge0\) and \(\int f(x)dx=1\)
C) \(f(x)\) is symmetric
D) \(f\) is continuous everywhere
* B
Why: Non-negativity and unit total area define a density; individual heights may exceed 1. Symmetry and continuity are optional properties of particular families.

Q13. The lognormal distribution is
A) bounded above
B) symmetric
C) right-skewed with a heavy right tail
D) left-skewed
* C
Why: Exponentiating a normal variable stretches the upper tail multiplicatively, producing positive skew. It is unbounded above, which is why it fits incomes and claim sizes.

Q14. A quantile–quantile plot is used to
A) compare the empirical distribution with a theoretical one such as the normal
B) estimate the mean
C) test independence
D) compute a p-value
* A
Why: Departures from the reference line reveal skew, heavy tails or truncation. It is a diagnostic, not a formal test.

Q15. For an exponential distribution, the standard deviation equals
A) the mean
B) half the mean
C) the mean squared
D) \(\sqrt{\lambda}\)
* A
Why: Both mean and variance are \(1/\lambda^2\), so the SD is \(1/\lambda\), equal to the mean. This is a quick way to spot an exponential fit.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Why can a density exceed 1 while a probability cannot?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A density is probability per unit, so its height depends on the scale: a uniform density on
\((0,0.5)\) has height 2. Probability is the area under the density, and the total area is 1, so
any interval probability is at most 1.
</details>

**S2.** Explain the 68–95–99.7 rule and one situation where it would mislead.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For normal data, about 68 % of values lie within one SD of the mean, 95 % within two and 99.7 %
within three. It misleads for skewed or heavy-tailed data — incomes or latencies — where the
proportions differ markedly, and it says nothing about the distribution of the mean.
</details>

**S3.** What does the memoryless property of the exponential imply about waiting for a bus whose
arrivals are exponential?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
That the time already waited is irrelevant: the expected remaining wait is always \(1/\lambda\),
regardless of how long you have waited. This is counter-intuitive but follows from the constant
hazard rate, and it rules out "the bus is due".
</details>

**S4.** Why is the normal distribution so widely used if real data are rarely normal?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because sums and averages of many small independent effects are approximately normal (CLT), and
because many estimators are asymptotically normal. The normal is often a model for the sampling
distribution of a statistic, not for the raw data.
</details>

**S5.** How does the lognormal arise, and why is it appropriate for claim sizes?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When a positive quantity is the product of many small multiplicative factors, its logarithm is a
sum and is approximately normal. Claim sizes behave this way: they are positive, right-skewed,
and scale multiplicatively, so the lognormal captures both the skew and the positivity.
</details>

**S6.** Why does the gamma distribution have a shape parameter as well as a scale?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The shape \(k\) controls how many exponential waiting times are summed (the time to the \(k\)-th
event), changing the skew from strongly right-skewed at \(k=1\) toward symmetry for large \(k\).
The scale sets the time units. One parameter cannot capture both.
</details>

**S7.** What does a heavy right tail imply for the sample mean and for risk management?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The sample mean is dominated by rare large values, converges slowly, and is itself highly
variable. Risk measures based on the mean and SD understate tail losses, so high quantiles,
expected shortfall or extreme-value models are needed.
</details>

**S8.** Explain how to compute \(P(a<X<b)\) for a normal variable in practice.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Standardise: \(P(a<X<b)=\Phi((b-\mu)/\sigma)-\Phi((a-\mu)/\sigma)\), where \(\Phi\) is the
standard normal CDF. Software provides \(\Phi\); tables require rounding to two decimals, which
introduces small errors.
</details>

**S9.** Why does the exponential distribution have a constant hazard rate, and what does that
mean?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Its hazard is \(h(t)=f(t)/(1-F(t))=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}/e^{-\lambda t}=\lambda\), independent
of \(t\). The instantaneous failure risk does not change with age — appropriate for random
failures, inappropriate for wear-out or infant mortality.
</details>

**S10.** A QQ plot of residuals curves upward at both ends. What does that suggest?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A symmetric S-shape with both tails above/below the line indicates heavier tails than normal
(leptokurtic residuals), often from outliers or a mixture. An asymmetric curve indicates skew.
Either way, normal-based intervals may be too narrow or mis-centred.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,10)\). Compute \(P(2<X<7)\) and \(P(X>8)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Density is 0.1 on \((0,10)\). \(P(2<X<7)=5\times0.1=0.5\); \(P(X>8)=2\times0.1=0.2\).
Uniform probabilities are lengths divided by the total width.
</details>

**N2.** Compute the mean and variance of \(\mathrm{Uniform}(0,10)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean \(=(0+10)/2=5\); variance \((10-0)^2/12=100/12=8.333\), SD 2.887. The variance
formula is \((b-a)^2/12\), not \((b-a)^2/2\).
</details>

**N3.** For \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), find \(P(Z<1.96)\) and \(P(-1<Z<1)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(Z<1.96)=0.975\). \(P(-1<Z<1)=0.8413-0.1587=0.6827\), the 68 % rule. The two-sided
95 % interval uses \(\pm1.96\), not \(\pm2\).
</details>

**N4.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(0.5)\). Compute \(P(X>4)\) and the mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(X>4)=e^{-0.5\times4}=e^{-2}=0.1353\); mean \(=1/0.5=2\) time units. The probability
of exceeding twice the mean is about 13.5 %, not 5 %.
</details>

**N5.** Using N4, compute \(P(X>6\mid X>2)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Memorylessness: \(P(X>6\mid X>2)=P(X>4)=e^{-2}=0.1353\). Direct calculation gives
\(e^{-3}/e^{-1}=e^{-2}\), confirming the property.
</details>

**N6.** Find the 90th percentile of an exponential distribution with rate 0.5.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Solve \(1-e^{-0.5x}=0.9\), so \(e^{-0.5x}=0.1\) and \(x=\ln10/0.5=2.3026/0.5=4.605\). The
median would be \(\ln2/0.5=1.386\).
</details>

**N7.** A test score is \(N(100,15^2)\). Find the 95th percentile.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(100+1.645\times15=100+24.67=124.67\). The 97.5th percentile would use 1.96 and give 129.4;
the choice of percentile must match the one-sided or two-sided question.
</details>

**N8.** If \(\ln X\sim N(3,0.25)\), find the median and the mean of \(X\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Median \(=e^3=20.09\). Mean \(=e^{3+0.25/2}=e^{3.125}=22.76\). The mean exceeds the
median, as expected for a right-skewed variable.
</details>

**N9.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\text{shape}=3,\text{scale}=2)\). Find the mean and variance.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean \(=k\theta=3\times2=6\); variance \(=k\theta^2=3\times4=12\), SD 3.464. With shape
1 the same formulas give the exponential mean \(\theta\) and variance \(\theta^2\).
</details>

**N10.** A normal variable has mean 50 and SD 10. What proportion lies above 65?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(z=(65-50)/10=1.5\); \(P(Z>1.5)=1-0.9332=0.0668\), about 6.7 %. In a sample of 200, expect
about 13 observations above 65 if the model holds.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** Server response times have median 80 ms and mean 240 ms. Which distribution family would
you try, and what would you check?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The mean far above the median indicates strong right skew, typical of a lognormal (or gamma)
model. Fit on the log scale, check a QQ plot of logs, and compare AIC with a gamma fit. Report
the median and 95th/99th percentiles rather than the mean, since the tail drives user
experience.
</details>

**P2.** A component's lifetime is exponential with mean 5 years. A warranty covers 2 years. What
fraction of components fail within warranty, and why is the exponential a questionable model?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(P(X<2)=1-e^{-2/5}=1-0.6703=0.3297\)**, about 33 %. The exponential assumes a constant hazard
— no wear-out and no infant mortality — which is unrealistic for mechanical components; a
Weibull would allow increasing hazard.
</details>

**P3.** A quality engineer finds that 2.5 % of shafts exceed the upper tolerance limit, and the
diameters are normal with mean 25.00 and SD 0.02. Where is the limit?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The 97.5th percentile is \(25.00+1.96\times0.02=25.0392\)** mm. Conversely, if the limit is
fixed at 25.03, the exceedance rate is \(P(Z>(25.03-25)/0.02)=P(Z>1.5)=6.68 %\) — the SD must
be reduced to meet a 2.5 % target.
</details>

**P4.** An insurer models claim size as lognormal with \(\mu=8\), \(\sigma=1\) (log-dollars).
Estimate the probability that a claim exceeds 10,000.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(\ln(10{,}000)=9.21\), so \(z=(9.21-8)/1=1.21\) and \(P=1-\Phi(1.21)=0.113\). The mean
claim is \(e^{8.5}\approx4{,}915\) but the tail drives capital requirements, which is why the
whole distribution matters.
</details>

**P5.** You must choose between modelling a positive, skewed variable with a normal or a
lognormal. What checks decide it, and what goes wrong with the wrong choice?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Check the sign of the data (normal allows negatives), skewness of raw versus log values, and QQ
plots of both. A normal fit to lognormal data predicts negative values, misstates the mean and
understates tail risk; a lognormal fit to symmetric data imposes artificial skew. Compare with
AIC and out-of-sample scoring.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive the mean and variance of the exponential distribution from its density and show
the memoryless property.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(E[X]=\int_0^\infty x\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=1/\lambda\) (integrate by parts).
\(E[X^2]=\int x^2\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=2/\lambda^2\), so
\(\mathrm{Var}=2/\lambda^2-1/\lambda^2=1/\lambda^2\). Memorylessness:
\(P(X>s+t\mid X>s)=e^{-\lambda(s+t)}/e^{-\lambda s}=e^{-\lambda t}=P(X>t)\).
</details>

**C2.** Show that if \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) then \(Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\) is standard normal, and
derive \(P(a<X<b)\) in terms of \(\Phi\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
The CDF of \(Z\) is \(P((X-\mu)/\sigma\le z)=P(X\le\mu+\sigma z)=\Phi((\mu+\sigma z-\mu)/\sigma)
=\Phi(z)\), so \(Z\) is standard normal. Then
\(P(a<X<b)=P((a-\mu)/\sigma<Z<(b-\mu)/\sigma)=\Phi((b-\mu)/\sigma)-\Phi((a-\mu)/\sigma)\).
</details>

**C3.** For independent \(X\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\) and \(Y\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\), derive the
distribution of \(aX+bY\) and explain why independence is needed for the variance formula used.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Means add linearly: \(E[aX+bY]=a\mu_1+b\mu_2\). Variances:
\(\mathrm{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\sigma_1^2+b^2\sigma_2^2+2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\); independence makes the
covariance zero. A linear combination of jointly normal variables is normal, so
\(aX+bY\sim N(a\mu_1+b\mu_2,\ a^2\sigma_1^2+b^2\sigma_2^2)\). Without independence the variance
term changes even though the mean does not.
</details>
