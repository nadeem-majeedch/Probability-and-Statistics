## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. A one-way ANOVA tests
A) independence of two categorical variables
B) normality of the residuals
C) equality of variances across groups
D) equality of means across groups
* D
Why: The null is \(H_0:\mu_1=\cdots=\mu_k\). Equal variances are an assumption, and independence of categorical variables is a chi-square test.

Q2. The ANOVA \(F\) statistic is the ratio of
A) within-group to between-group variance
B) between-group to within-group variance
C) total to within-group variance
D) between-group variance to the mean
* B
Why: A large ratio means group means differ by more than the internal noise explains. Reversing the ratio would make large values evidence *for* the null.

Q3. For \(k\) groups with total \(N\) observations, the degrees of freedom are
A) \(k-1\) and \(N-k\)
B) \(k\) and \(N\)
C) \(N-1\) and \(k\)
D) \(k-1\) and \(N-1\)
* A
Why: Between-group df is \(k-1\), within-group df is \(N-k\), and they sum to \(N-1\), the total df.

Q4. ANOVA assumes
A) independence, approximate normality and equal variances within groups
B) equal group sizes
C) normal predictors
D) a linear relationship between the outcome and group labels
* A
Why: These are the standard conditions for the \(F\) distribution. Unequal group sizes are allowed; unequal variances and dependence are not.

Q5. A significant one-way ANOVA tells you
A) the size of the largest difference
B) which groups differ
C) that at least one group mean differs
D) that all groups differ
* C
Why: The omnibus test rejects equality but does not localise the difference. Post-hoc comparisons with multiplicity control are needed to say which groups differ.

Q6. A chi-square test of independence assesses
A) whether means differ
B) whether variances are equal
C) the correlation between two continuous variables
D) whether two categorical variables are associated
* D
Why: It compares observed cell counts with those expected under independence. Means and variances belong to other tests.

Q7. The expected count in a contingency table cell is
A) (row total × column total)/grand total
B) row total/column total
C) observed count × 2
D) grand total/number of cells
* A
Why: Under independence, the cell probability is the product of the marginal probabilities, which yields this formula after multiplying by the grand total.

Q8. The chi-square approximation is unreliable when
A) expected counts are below about 5
B) the sample is large
C) there are two rows
D) the variables are categorical
* A
Why: Sparse cells make the chi-square distribution a poor approximation. Use Fisher's exact test or combine categories.

Q9. For a \(3\times4\) contingency table, the degrees of freedom are
A) 11
B) 12
C) 6
D) 5
* C
Why: \((r-1)(c-1)=2\times3=6\). Using the number of cells overstates the df and understates the p-value.

Q10. The Mann–Whitney test is preferred to a two-sample \(t\)-test when
A) variances are equal
B) the outcome is normal
C) the data are heavily skewed or ordinal
D) the sample is very large
* C
Why: It compares ranks and does not require normality. It tests a different hypothesis — stochastic dominance or a shift in distribution — not simply equality of means.

Q11. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test is used for
A) variance equality
B) paired or matched data
C) three or more independent groups
D) two categorical variables
* B
Why: It ranks the absolute differences within pairs. For independent groups the Mann–Whitney test applies.

Q12. The nonparametric alternative to one-way ANOVA is
A) Wilcoxon signed-rank
B) Levene's test
C) Kruskal–Wallis
D) chi-square goodness of fit
* C
Why: Kruskal–Wallis compares rank distributions across \(k\) groups. The signed-rank test handles paired data only.

Q13. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test compares
A) variances of two samples
B) observed frequencies with hypothesised probabilities
C) two group means
D) observed and expected counts under independence
* B
Why: It checks whether the observed category frequencies match a specified distribution. The independence test is a different use of the same statistic.

Q14. When the normality assumption holds, nonparametric tests are generally
A) more powerful
B) less powerful
C) equally powerful
D) invalid
* B
Why: Rank tests discard the magnitude information that the \(t\)-test exploits, so they lose some power under normality — the price of robustness.

Q15. Levene's test is used to check
A) equality of means
B) independence
C) normality
D) equality of variances across groups
* D
Why: It tests homogeneity of variance, which underpins pooled \(t\)-tests and ANOVA. Normality is checked with QQ plots or the Shapiro–Wilk test.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain how ANOVA partitions total variability.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
\(\mathrm{SST}=\mathrm{SSB}+\mathrm{SSW}\): total squared deviations from the grand mean split
into between-group variation (group means around the grand mean) and within-group variation
(observations around their group means). The \(F\) ratio compares their mean squares, so an effect
is detected when between-group variation exceeds what within-group noise would produce.
</details>

**S2.** Why is an omnibus test followed by post-hoc comparisons rather than many pairwise
\(t\)-tests?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Running all pairwise tests inflates the family-wise error rate — with 5 groups there are 10
comparisons, so the chance of a false positive is far above 5 %. ANOVA first tests a single
hypothesis; post-hoc procedures such as Tukey's HSD then control error across the comparisons.
</details>

**S3.** What does the chi-square statistic measure, and why does a large value indicate
association?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It sums \((O-E)^2/E\) over cells, measuring how far observed counts are from the independence
predictions relative to their size. Large values mean the data are unlikely under independence,
hence evidence of association.
</details>

**S4.** Why do nonparametric tests use ranks, and what do they lose?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Ranks are unaffected by the shape of the distribution and by extreme values, so the tests remain
valid without normality. They lose information about magnitudes, which reduces power when the
normal model is correct, and they answer questions about distributions or medians rather than
means.
</details>

**S5.** When would you prefer a Kruskal–Wallis test to a one-way ANOVA?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When the outcome is ordinal, strongly skewed, or contains outliers, or when group variances differ
markedly and transformations are unappealing. With small samples and doubtful normality, the rank
test is safer, though it tests a different null.
</details>

**S6.** What is the difference between a chi-square test of independence and a goodness-of-fit
test?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Independence compares two categorical variables in a contingency table, with expected counts from
the margins. Goodness of fit compares one variable's frequencies with externally specified
probabilities. Both use \(\sum(O-E)^2/E\), but the hypotheses and df differ.
</details>

**S7.** Why does a significant ANOVA not license the claim that "all groups differ from each
other"?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The omnibus alternative is that at least one mean differs. Some groups may be indistinguishable;
only pairwise comparisons with multiplicity control can identify which. Claiming all differ is a
multiple-comparisons error.
</details>

**S8.** What should you do if ANOVA's equal-variance assumption fails?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Use Welch's ANOVA, which does not assume equal variances, or a Kruskal–Wallis test; alternatively
transform the outcome. Check the sensitivity of conclusions and report which version was used.
</details>

**S9.** How would you report the results of an ANOVA to a non-specialist?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Give group means with intervals, the \(F\) statistic and p-value, and an effect size such as
\(\eta^2\) or \(\omega^2\) that states how much variation is associated with the grouping. Then
describe the post-hoc pattern in plain language, avoiding causal claims unless the design supports
them.
</details>

**S10.** Why can a chi-square test be significant with a tiny association in a large sample?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The statistic grows with \(N\), so even a negligible departure from independence becomes
detectable. Report the effect size (Cramér's V or the odds ratios) and the cell percentages to
show whether the association matters.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** Three groups of 10 each have \(\mathrm{SSB}=120\) and \(\mathrm{SSW}=270\). Compute the
\(F\) statistic.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{MSB}=120/(3-1)=60\); \(\mathrm{MSW}=270/(30-3)=10\); \(F=60/10=6.0\) with df
\((2,27)\). This exceeds the 5 % critical value of about 3.35, so reject equality of means.
</details>

**N2.** For N1, compute \(\eta^2\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\eta^2=\mathrm{SSB}/\mathrm{SST}=120/(120+270)=120/390=0.308\). About 31 % of the total
variation is between groups — a moderate effect.
</details>

**N3.** A \(2\times2\) table has observed counts 30, 20, 10, 40. Compute the expected count for the
first cell.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Row 1 total \(=50\), column 1 total \(=40\), grand total \(=100\). Expected
\(=50\times40/100=20\). The observed 30 exceeds expectation, suggesting association.
</details>

**N4.** Using N3, compute the chi-square statistic.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Expected: 20, 30, 20, 30. Contributions:
\((30-20)^2/20=5\); \((20-30)^2/30=3.33\); \((10-20)^2/20=5\); \((40-30)^2/30=3.33\). Total
\(\chi^2=16.67\) with 1 df, p < 0.001.
</details>

**N5.** For a \(4\times3\) table, give the degrees of freedom and the 5 % critical value
approximately.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
df \(=(4-1)(3-1)=6\); the 5 % critical value is about **12.59**. A statistic above this
indicates association at the 5 % level.
</details>

**N6.** Four groups of 8 give \(\mathrm{SSB}=90\), \(\mathrm{SSW}=280\). Compute \(F\) and the df.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{MSB}=90/3=30\); \(\mathrm{MSW}=280/28=10\); \(F=3.0\) with \((3,28)\) df. The 5 %
critical value is about 2.95, so the result is marginal.
</details>

**N7.** A Mann–Whitney test compares 6 observations against 7 with \(R_1=52\). Compute \(U_1\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(U_1=R_1-n_1(n_1+1)/2=52-6(7)/2=52-21=31\). With \(n_1n_2=42\), \(U_2=42-31=11\); the
smaller value enters the test.
</details>

**N8.** A goodness-of-fit test has categories with observed 40, 35, 25 and expected 33.33 each.
Compute \(\chi^2\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\((40-33.33)^2/33.33=1.335\); \((35-33.33)^2/33.33=0.084\); \((25-33.33)^2/33.33=2.083\). Total
\(\chi^2=3.50\) with 2 df; the 5 % critical value is 5.99, so no evidence against the
hypothesised distribution.
</details>

**N9.** Kruskal–Wallis with 3 groups of 5 gives \(H=7.4\). With df = 2, the 5 % critical value is
5.99. State the conclusion.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(H=7.4>5.99\), so reject the null of equal distributions at the 5 % level. This is an omnibus
result; post-hoc pairwise rank comparisons are needed to identify which groups differ.
</details>

**N10.** A one-way ANOVA on 3 groups yields \(F=2.2\) with df \((2,45)\). What is the decision at
5 %?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
The 5 % critical value is about 3.20, and \(2.2<3.20\), so fail to reject. Note that failure to
reject is not proof of equality; the confidence intervals for the group means may still show
meaningful differences.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** An experiment tests three website layouts with 500 users each. Click-through rates are
6.0 %, 6.4 % and 7.1 %. Outline the analysis and the decisions.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Use a chi-square test of independence (or a logistic regression with layout as a factor) on the
\(3\times2\) table. If the omnibus test is significant, compare layouts pairwise with multiplicity
control, reporting differences and intervals. Check guardrail metrics and whether randomisation
balanced exposure.
</details>

**P2.** Customer satisfaction is measured on a 1–5 ordinal scale across four regions, with skewed
responses. Which test and why?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Kruskal–Wallis, because the outcome is ordinal and skewed, and the test compares rank
distributions without a normality assumption. Report median scores and the distribution per
region; if the omnibus test is significant, use pairwise Mann–Whitney with a corrected
significance level.
</details>

**P3.** A chi-square test of independence in a \(5\times5\) table with 120 observations gives
\(p=0.02\). Should you trust it?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Check expected counts: 120/25 = 4.8 per cell on average, so several cells will be below 5, making
the approximation unreliable. Combine sparse categories, use an exact test or collect more data;
report Cramér's V to show whether any association matters.
</details>

**P4.** A researcher runs three separate \(t\)-tests between four groups and reports two as
significant. What is the problem and the fix?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Selective reporting of pairwise tests without multiplicity control inflates the false-positive
rate, and the choice of three comparisons out of six suggests cherry-picking. Use ANOVA with a
pre-specified contrast set or Tukey's HSD, and report all comparisons performed.
</details>

**P5.** An A/B/C test shows a significant ANOVA. The business wants to ship the best variant.
What else is needed?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Identify which variant differs (post-hoc with intervals), check that the difference is large
enough to matter and consistent across segments, verify guardrail metrics and novelty effects,
and confirm the test was adequately powered. A significant omnibus result alone does not identify
the best variant.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive the ANOVA decomposition \(\mathrm{SST}=\mathrm{SSB}+\mathrm{SSW}\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \(y_{ij}-\bar y=(\bar y_j-\bar y)+(y_{ij}-\bar y_j)\). Squaring and summing,
the cross term is \(2\sum_j(\bar y_j-\bar y)\sum_i(y_{ij}-\bar y_j)\), and the inner sum is zero
because deviations within a group sum to zero. Hence
\(\sum_{ij}(y_{ij}-\bar y)^2=\sum_j n_j(\bar y_j-\bar y)^2+\sum_{ij}(y_{ij}-\bar y_j)^2\).
</details>

**C2.** Show that under the null hypothesis of equal means with normal errors, the \(F\) ratio has
an \(F\) distribution, and explain why the ratio is formed with mean squares.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Under \(H_0\), \(\mathrm{SSW}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{N-k}\) and, independently,
\(\mathrm{SSB}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{k-1}\). Dividing each by its df and taking the ratio gives
\(F=\frac{\mathrm{SSB}/(k-1)}{\mathrm{SSW}/(N-k)}\sim F_{k-1,N-k}\). Mean squares are used
because the raw sums of squares have different df, so only the per-df versions are comparable.
</details>

**C3.** Show that the chi-square statistic can be written as a sum of squared standardised
residuals, and explain why large samples make small associations significant.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Each term is \(\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}=\left(\frac{O-E}{\sqrt E}\right)^2\), a squared standardised
residual. Under independence, the sum of these approximately follows \(\chi^2\) with
\((r-1)(c-1)\) df, which has mean equal to its df and grows with the number of cells. Because the
statistic is proportional to \(N\) for a fixed association, its expected value under an
alternative grows linearly in \(N\); hence trivial associations become significant in large
samples.
</details>
