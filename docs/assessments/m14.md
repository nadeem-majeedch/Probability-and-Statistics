## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. A 95 % confidence interval means
A) 95 % of the data lie in the interval
B) the estimate is correct 95 % of the time
C) there is a 95 % probability the parameter lies in this particular interval
D) in repeated sampling, 95 % of intervals constructed this way would contain the parameter
* D
Why: Coverage is a property of the procedure over repeated samples. Once computed, this interval either contains the parameter or does not; the probability statement refers to the method.

Q2. The margin of error of a confidence interval for a mean is
A) \(z\sigma n\)
B) \(\sigma/\sqrt n\)
C) \(z\sigma\)
D) \(z\sigma/\sqrt n\)
* D
Why: The half-width is the critical value times the standard error. Omitting \(\sqrt n\) describes the spread of individual observations, not the precision of the mean.

Q3. To halve the margin of error at fixed confidence, the sample size must be
A) increased by 50 %
B) doubled
C) quadrupled
D) halved
* C
Why: The margin scales as \(1/\sqrt n\), so a factor of 2 reduction needs \(2^2=4\) times the data. This is why precision is expensive.

Q4. An estimator is unbiased when
A) its variance is zero
B) its expected value equals the parameter
C) it is normally distributed
D) it is consistent
* B
Why: Unbiasedness is about the centre of the sampling distribution, not its spread. An unbiased estimator can still be highly variable.

Q5. The mean squared error of an estimator equals
A) \(\mathrm{Bias}^2-\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\)
B) \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\)
C) \(\mathrm{Bias}^2+\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\)
D) \(\mathrm{Bias}+\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\)
* C
Why: Squared error decomposes into the squared systematic offset plus random variability. A biased estimator with small variance can beat an unbiased one with large variance.

Q6. Which estimator of the population mean is unbiased?
A) The sample median
B) The sample mean
C) The midrange
D) The sample mode
* B
Why: \(E[\bar X]=\mu\) for any distribution with a finite mean. The median is unbiased only for symmetric distributions; the midrange is highly sensitive to outliers.

Q7. As \(n\to\infty\), a consistent estimator
A) converges in probability to the parameter
B) always has zero bias for finite \(n\)
C) has variance equal to the parameter
D) becomes biased
* A
Why: Consistency is an asymptotic property: the estimator concentrates on the true value. An estimator can be biased for every finite \(n\) yet consistent.

Q8. Which interval is wider, all else equal?
A) A 90 % confidence interval
B) A 99 % confidence interval
C) They are the same
D) It depends on the mean
* B
Why: Higher confidence requires a larger critical value and therefore a wider interval. There is a direct trade-off between confidence and precision.

Q9. A 95 % prediction interval for a new observation is wider than a 95 % confidence interval for
A) it must also cover the variability of the individual observation
B) the mean is unknown
C) prediction intervals use a smaller critical value
D) it is computed from a smaller sample
* A
Why: The prediction interval adds the residual variance \(\sigma^2\) to the variance of the estimated mean, so it reflects both estimation uncertainty and individual noise.

Q10. When the population standard deviation is unknown and the sample is small, the interval for
A) the normal critical value
B) the \(t\) critical value with \(n-1\) df
C) the chi-square critical value
D) no critical value
* B
Why: Estimating \(\sigma\) by \(s\) adds variability, and \(t\) accounts for it. For large \(n\), \(t\) and normal critical values nearly coincide.

Q11. The bootstrap is most useful when
A) the population is known
B) \(n\) is very small and the data are dependent
C) the statistic has no simple standard error formula
D) the sample is biased
* C
Why: Resampling approximates the sampling distribution of statistics such as medians, ratios or correlations. It cannot repair a biased sample and struggles with very small or dependent data.

Q12. A confidence interval for a proportion should not be used when
A) \(p\) is near 0.5
B) the sample is random
C) \(n\) is large
D) the number of successes or failures is very small
* D
Why: The normal approximation fails when counts are small, producing intervals that include impossible values or have poor coverage. Use exact or Wilson intervals.

Q13. Increasing the confidence level while keeping the sample fixed
A) widens the interval
B) leaves it unchanged
C) reduces bias
D) narrows the interval
* A
Why: A larger critical value multiplies the standard error, widening the interval. Precision and confidence cannot both be improved without more data.

Q14. A 95 % interval for a mean is (10.2, 13.8). The point estimate and margin of error are
A) 12.0 and 1.8
B) 12.0 and 3.6
C) 11.0 and 2.8
D) 13.8 and 10.2
* A
Why: The estimate is the midpoint, \((10.2+13.8)/2=12.0\); the margin is half the width, 1.8. The full width is 3.6.

Q15. For a fixed sample, which change makes a confidence interval for a mean narrower?
A) Increasing the confidence level
B) Increasing the sample standard deviation
C) Increasing the sample size
D) Using a smaller sample
* C
Why: The standard error falls as \(\sqrt n\) grows. A larger SD or a higher confidence level would widen the interval.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain carefully what "95 % confident" means, and what it does not mean.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It means the procedure produces intervals that contain the true parameter in 95 % of repeated
samples. It does not mean this interval has a 95 % chance of containing the parameter, nor that
95 % of the data are inside it. The parameter is fixed; the randomness is in the interval.
</details>

**S2.** Why is the sample mean unbiased for \(\mu\) but the sample standard deviation not unbiased
for \(\sigma\)?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Linearity gives \(E[\bar X]=\mu\) immediately. For the SD, Jensen's inequality applied to the
concave square root gives \(E[S]\le\sqrt{E[S^2]}=\sigma\), so it underestimates on average. The
bias is small for large \(n\) and can be corrected with a factor from the gamma function.
</details>

**S3.** Explain the trade-off captured by mean squared error, and when a biased estimator is
preferable.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
\(\mathrm{MSE}=\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}\). A small bias can be worth accepting if it
substantially reduces variance — ridge regression and shrinkage estimators do exactly this,
trading a little bias for much lower MSE, which improves prediction.
</details>

**S4.** Why does a confidence interval not become narrower as the population size grows, when
sampling without replacement?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It does, but only through the finite population correction
\(\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}\), and only when the sampling fraction is appreciable. The dominant term is
still \(1/\sqrt n\); a census has zero sampling error, but most surveys sample a tiny fraction of
a large population, so \(N\) barely matters.
</details>

**S5.** What is the difference between a confidence interval and a credible interval?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A confidence interval is justified by repeated-sampling coverage and makes no probability
statement about the parameter. A credible interval is a posterior probability statement: given
the model and prior, the parameter lies in it with the stated probability. They often coincide
numerically with a flat prior.
</details>

**S6.** Why is the \(t\) interval wider than the \(z\) interval for the same data?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because \(s\) is estimated, the ratio \((\bar x-\mu)/(s/\sqrt n)\) has heavier tails than the
normal; the \(t\) critical value exceeds \(z\) to maintain the stated coverage. The gap shrinks as
the degrees of freedom increase.
</details>

**S7.** What does it mean for an estimator to be efficient?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It attains the smallest possible variance among unbiased estimators — the Cramér–Rao lower
bound. Efficiency is a large-sample or asymptotic property for many estimators, and a more
efficient estimator gives shorter intervals for the same data.
</details>

**S8.** How would you decide the sample size for a survey with a target margin of error?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Use \(n=z^2p(1-p)/m^2\), with \(p=0.5\) if unknown, and inflate for the expected response rate
and design effect. For ±3 % at 95 % confidence this gives about 1,068 respondents before
adjusting for non-response.
</details>

**S9.** Why can a confidence interval be misleading when the estimator is biased?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The interval is centred on a biased estimate, so it is systematically displaced from the
parameter; coverage falls even as \(n\) grows. Bias from non-response, measurement error or a
misspecified model is not repaired by a standard error.
</details>

**S10.** What does the coverage probability of an interval procedure depend on?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
On the model assumptions — independence, distributional shape, correct standard error — and on
the sample size. Under misspecification, nominal 95 % intervals can cover far less; simulations
and bootstrap checks reveal the actual coverage.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** A sample of 25 gives mean 100 and \(s=15\). Compute the 95 % \(t\) interval
(\(t_{0.975,24}=2.064\)).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=15/5=3\); margin \(=2.064\times3=6.19\); interval \(100\pm6.19=(93.81,106.19)\).
With \(z=1.96\) the interval would be slightly narrower, understating uncertainty.
</details>

**N2.** \(\sigma=10\), \(n=64\). Compute the 95 % margin of error for the mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=10/8=1.25\); margin \(=1.96\times1.25=2.45\). The interval is
\(\bar x\pm2.45\).
</details>

**N3.** How large a sample gives a 95 % margin of error of 2 when \(\sigma=10\)?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(1.96\times10/\sqrt n\le2\) gives \(\sqrt n\ge9.8\), so \(n\ge96.04\); round up to \(97\).
Rounding down would miss the target.
</details>

**N4.** In a sample of 400, 160 report satisfaction. Compute the 95 % interval for the proportion.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\hat p=0.4\); \(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.24/400}=0.0245\); margin \(=1.96(0.0245)=0.048\). Interval
\(0.352\) to \(0.448\). Report as 35–45 %.
</details>

**N5.** An estimator has bias 2 and variance 9. Compute its MSE and RMSE.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{MSE}=4+9=13\); \(\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{13}=3.606\). The bias contributes less
than the variance here, but both matter.
</details>

**N6.** A 99 % interval for a mean uses \(z=2.576\). With \(\mathrm{SE}=1.5\), compute the margin.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Margin \(=2.576\times1.5=3.864\). Compare with 2.94 at 95 %: raising confidence by 4
percentage points widens the interval by about 31 %.
</details>

**N7.** A sample of 10 has \(s=4\). Give the \(t\) critical value for a 90 % interval and the
margin (\(t_{0.95,9}=1.833\)).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=4/\sqrt{10}=1.265\); margin \(=1.833\times1.265=2.32\). Fewer observations and
lower confidence both shrink the margin, but the small sample makes the interval fragile.
</details>

**N8.** A 95 % interval for a proportion is (0.48, 0.56). Can you conclude the proportion differs
from 0.5?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
No: 0.5 lies inside the interval, so the data are consistent with 0.5. The estimate is 0.52 with
a margin of 0.04, and the interval spans the null value.
</details>

**N9.** With \(\hat p=0.2\) and \(n=50\), the normal approximation is doubtful. Compute \(n\hat p\)
and \(n(1-\hat p)\) and comment.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(n\hat p=10\) and \(n(1-\hat p)=40\). The smaller expected count is exactly at the usual
threshold of 10, so the normal interval is borderline; an exact or Wilson interval is safer.
</details>

**N10.** A pilot study gives \(s=12\). How large a sample is needed for a 95 % margin of 3?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(n=(1.96\times12/3)^2=(7.84)^2=61.5\), so \(62\) observations. Using the pilot \(s\) adds
uncertainty; inflate slightly for safety.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A product manager reads "average revenue per user is 12.00 ± 1.50 (95 % CI, in local currency)"
and asks whether that means 95 % of users spend between 10.50 and 13.50. Correct the interpretation.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
No: the interval describes the uncertainty of the *mean*, not the spread of users. Individual
revenue is far more variable; a 95 % prediction or percentile range would be much wider. Because
revenue is skewed, even the mean's interval relies on the CLT and a large sample.
</details>

**P2.** A clinical trial reports a 95 % CI for a difference in recovery rates of (−2 %, +9 %).
How should the result be described, and what decision follows?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The data are compatible with effects from a 2-point harm to a 9-point benefit; the study does not
establish a benefit. Report the estimate and interval, note the width and the resulting
uncertainty, and avoid claiming "no effect" — the interval includes clinically meaningful
benefits, so the study is inconclusive rather than negative.
</details>

**P3.** You must estimate median household income with a 95 % interval. Why is a normal interval
on the mean a poor choice, and what would you do instead?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Income is right-skewed with outliers, so the mean's interval is unstable and describes a quantity
few households resemble. Use the median with a bootstrap percentile interval (or a
transformed/log approach), and report the quartiles alongside.
</details>

**P4.** A dashboard shows conversion 3.2 % with a 95 % interval of (0.4 %, 6.0 %) from 500
visitors. What is wrong, and how would you fix it?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
With 16 conversions the normal approximation is poor and the interval is too wide and possibly
mis-centred; it also cannot go below 0. Use an exact (Clopper–Pearson) or Wilson interval, or
collect more data. Report the count, not only the percentage.
</details>

**P5.** A model's accuracy is 0.81 on a test set of 200. Give an approximate 95 % interval and
explain why it may understate true uncertainty.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.81(0.19)/200}=0.0277\); interval \(0.81\pm0.054=(0.756,0.864)\). It
understates uncertainty if the test set is not independent of training (leakage), if the
population shifts, or if the 200 cases are clustered.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive the margin of error for a mean with known variance and show how the sample size is
chosen for a target margin.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\) approximately, so
\(P(|\bar X-\mu|\le z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n)=1-\alpha\). The margin is
\(m=z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n\), and solving for \(n\) gives
\(n=(z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/m)^2\), rounded up. The quadratic dependence on precision is why margins
are expensive to halve.
</details>

**C2.** Show that MSE decomposes into bias squared plus variance.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Let \(\bar\theta=E[\hat\theta]\). Then
\(E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]=E[((\hat\theta-\bar\theta)+(\bar\theta-\theta))^2]
=\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)+2(\bar\theta-\theta)E[\hat\theta-\bar\theta]+(\bar\theta-\theta)^2
=\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)+\mathrm{Bias}^2\), since the middle term vanishes.
</details>

**C3.** Explain why a \(100(1-\alpha)\)% \(t\) interval has exactly \(1-\alpha\) coverage when the
data are normal, and why coverage is only approximate otherwise.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
For normal data, \((\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\sim t_{n-1}\) exactly, so
\(P(-t\le(\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\le t)=1-\alpha\) by construction, independent of \(\mu\) and
\(\sigma\). Without normality the ratio is not exactly \(t\); the CLT makes coverage approach
\(1-\alpha\) as \(n\) grows, but small skewed samples can have coverage well below nominal.
</details>
