## A. Problem statement

A workshop machines shafts to a nominal diameter of 25.00 mm with tolerance limits of
24.85 mm and 25.15 mm. Three hundred shafts from two machines have been measured. The
production manager wants to know whether the process is capable of meeting the
specification, whether the two machines are equivalent, and how many shafts per thousand
are likely to be scrapped.

## B. Background and context

Quality control is applied statistics with money attached: every scrapped part is a
direct loss and every returned assembly is a warranty claim. The two questions that matter
are "is the process centred?" and "is it precise enough?" — and they are different
questions. A process can be perfectly centred and still fail because it is too variable,
or beautifully precise and fail because it is offset. Process capability indices (Cp, Cpk)
formalise both, and the normal distribution supplies the scrap-rate arithmetic. The
measurements themselves are the classic illustration of sampling variability: two
machines, same nominal target, different spreads.

## C. Dataset description

`shaft_diameters.csv` has 300 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `unit_id` | integer | Shaft identifier |
| `batch` | integer | Production batch, 1–30, ten shafts each |
| `machine` | text | `M1` (183 shafts) or `M2` (117) |
| `diameter_mm` | float | Measured diameter in millimetres |

**Generation method.** Machine M1 produces normal(25.00, 0.055) diameters; M2 produces
normal(25.05, 0.075), i.e. a 0.05 mm offset and a wider spread. Machine assignment is
Bernoulli with P(M2) = 0.4. Specification limits are 24.85 and 25.15 mm, giving a
tolerance width of 0.30 mm. The data are therefore not perfectly balanced between
machines — a realistic nuisance that students must handle.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What are the mean, standard deviation and range of the diameters?
2. Is the process centred on the nominal value? What proportion is out of specification?
3. Compute Cp and Cpk. Which machine is capable and which is not?
4. Is the difference in mean diameter between the machines statistically significant?
5. Is a normal model adequate for these measurements, and does it matter?
6. How many parts per million would each machine be expected to scrap?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Continuous distributions; normal density and the empirical rule.
- Standardisation: \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) and tail probabilities.
- Sampling distribution of the mean; standard error.
- Two-sample t-test and its assumptions.
- Process capability: Cp, Cpk, and their relation to specification limits.
- Normal probability plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test.
- Expected fraction nonconforming from a normal model.

## F. Mathematical formulation

Specification limits \(LSL\) and \(USL\), process mean \(\mu\), standard deviation \(\sigma\):

\[
C_p=\frac{USL-LSL}{6\sigma},\qquad
C_{pk}=\min\!\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3\sigma},\frac{\mu-LSL}{3\sigma}\right).
\]

\(C_p\) measures potential capability (spread only); \(C_{pk}\) penalises off-centre
processes. A commonly quoted minimum is 1.33; below 1.0 the process is not capable.

The expected fraction outside the limits under normality is

\[
p=\Phi\!\left(\frac{LSL-\mu}{\sigma}\right)+1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
\]

The two-sample Welch statistic for M1 versus M2 is

\[
t=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}.
\]

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Load the file; check count, ranges and missing values.
2. Summarise diameters overall and by machine.
3. Compute the out-of-specification count and proportion.
4. Compute Cp and Cpk for each machine; interpret against 1.33.
5. Predict the scrap rate from the normal model and compare with the observed count.
6. Test the difference in means with Welch's t-test; report the CI.
7. Check normality with a Q-Q plot and Shapiro-Wilk; discuss consequences.
8. Recommend an action: recentre, reduce variation, or both.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

LSL, USL, TARGET = 24.85, 25.15, 25.00
parts = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/shaft_diameters.csv")
print(parts.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("rows:", len(parts), "| machines:", parts.machine.value_counts().to_dict())

overall = parts["diameter_mm"]
print(f"\nmean = {overall.mean():.3f} mm, sd = {overall.std(ddof=1):.3f} mm, "
      f"range = {overall.min():.3f} to {overall.max():.3f}")

out = (overall < LSL) | (overall > USL)
print(f"out of specification: {int(out.sum())} of {len(parts)} "
      f"({out.mean() * 100:.2f}%, {out.mean() * 1e6:.0f} ppm)")


def capability(series, lsl=LSL, usl=USL):
    mu, sigma = series.mean(), series.std(ddof=1)
    cp = (usl - lsl) / (6 * sigma)
    cpk = min(usl - mu, mu - lsl) / (3 * sigma)
    p_out = stats.norm.cdf(lsl, mu, sigma) + 1 - stats.norm.cdf(usl, mu, sigma)
    return mu, sigma, cp, cpk, p_out


print("\ncapability by machine:")
for machine, group in parts.groupby("machine"):
    mu, sigma, cp, cpk, p_out = capability(group["diameter_mm"])
    print(f"  {machine}: n={len(group):3d} mean={mu:.3f} sd={sigma:.3f} "
          f"Cp={cp:.2f} Cpk={cpk:.2f} predicted scrap={p_out * 1e6:.0f} ppm")

m1 = parts.loc[parts.machine == "M1", "diameter_mm"]
m2 = parts.loc[parts.machine == "M2", "diameter_mm"]
welch = stats.ttest_ind(m1, m2, equal_var=False)
diff = m2.mean() - m1.mean()
se = np.sqrt(m1.var(ddof=1) / len(m1) + m2.var(ddof=1) / len(m2))
print(f"\nmean difference M2 - M1 = {diff:.4f} mm "
      f"(95% CI {diff - 1.96 * se:.4f} to {diff + 1.96 * se:.4f})")
print(f"Welch t = {welch.statistic:.2f}, p = {welch.pvalue:.2e}")

print("\nnormality (Shapiro-Wilk):")
for machine, group in parts.groupby("machine"):
    w = stats.shapiro(group["diameter_mm"])
    print(f"  {machine}: W={w.statistic:.4f}, p={w.pvalue:.3f}")
```

**How to read it.** M2's distribution is visibly shifted to the right and wider, so its
right tail crosses the upper limit while M1 stays inside both limits almost everywhere.
The Q-Q plot for M2 is close to a straight line, so normality is a reasonable model and
the normal-based capability arithmetic is trustworthy. Note that both histograms are
drawn with density scaling so that the different sample sizes do not distort the shapes.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Overall mean 25.017 mm, SD 0.071 mm; 8 of 300 shafts (2.67 %) lie outside 24.85–25.15.
- M1: mean 24.998, SD 0.060, Cp 0.83, Cpk 0.82 — centred but not precise enough.
- M2: mean 25.048, SD 0.076, Cp 0.66, Cpk 0.45 — off-centre **and** too variable.
- The mean difference is 0.050 mm (95 % CI roughly 0.034–0.066), t ≈ 6, p < 0.001: the
  machines are not equivalent.
- The normal model predicts about 1.3 % scrap for M1 and about 9.3 % for M2; the observed
  1 of 183 (0.55 %) and 7 of 117 (5.98 %) are of the same order and compatible within
  sampling error for such small counts.
- Capability below 1.0 on both machines means the specification cannot be met with the
  current process: M2 must be recentred first, then its variation reduced.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — summaries.** The pooled mean is 25.017 mm, only 0.017 mm above nominal, which
looks reassuring until the machines are separated: M1 averages 24.998 and M2 averages
25.048. The pooled standard deviation (0.071) is also a blend of 0.060 and 0.076, and
blending hides the problem machine.

**Step 2 — out of specification.** Eight shafts fail: 1 of 183 from M1 (0.55 %) and 7 of
117 from M2 (5.98 %). In parts-per-million terms that is roughly 5,500 ppm versus 60,000
ppm — a tenfold difference in scrap.

**Step 3 — Cp.** Cp = 0.30/(6 × 0.060) = 0.83 for M1 and 0.30/(6 × 0.076) = 0.66 for M2.
Neither reaches the conventional 1.33 threshold; even a perfectly centred M1 would fail,
because its spread alone consumes more than the tolerance.

**Step 4 — Cpk.** For M1 the binding limit is the upper one: (25.15 − 24.998)/(3 × 0.060)
= 0.84, and the lower side gives (24.998 − 24.85)/0.180 = 0.82, so Cpk = 0.82. For M2 the
upper limit binds badly: (25.15 − 25.048)/0.228 = 0.45. The gap between Cp and Cpk is the
off-centre penalty: M1 loses almost nothing (0.83 → 0.82), M2 loses a third (0.66 → 0.45).

**Step 5 — predicted scrap.** Using the normal CDF with each machine's mean and SD gives
about 1.3 % for M1 and 9.3 % for M2. The observed 0.55 % and 5.98 % are lower, which is
unsurprising with only 183 and 117 units: the expected counts are about 2.4 and 10.9, and
observing 1 and 7 is well within Poisson noise. The model is doing real work here — it lets
the manager predict the cost of production before measuring every part — but the observed
rates from one small sample should not be quoted as precise estimates.

**Step 6 — the test.** The mean difference is 0.050 mm with a 95 % interval of about
0.034–0.066. t ≈ 6, p < 0.001. Both statistical and practical significance hold: 0.05 mm
is a sixth of the total tolerance, far too large to ignore.

**Step 7 — normality.** Shapiro-Wilk gives p = 0.968 for M1 and p = 0.070 for M2; neither
rejects normality at 5 %. The Q-Q plot is straight. This matters because the capability
indices and the ppm arithmetic assume normality; with a clearly non-normal process the
indices would need a transformation or a non-parametric estimate.

**Recommendation.** M2 is the priority: adjust its set-point down by 0.05 mm, which alone
would raise Cpk from 0.45 to about 0.66 and cut its scrap rate by roughly two-thirds. Both
machines still need variation reduction — a target SD of 0.03 mm would give Cp ≈ 1.67 for
M1 — through tool wear monitoring, fixture maintenance or temperature control. Until then,
100 % inspection of M2 output is cheaper than the warranty cost of shipping 6 % bad parts.

**Why capability indices are not enough.** Cp and Cpk assume a stable, normal process. If
the process drifts between shifts, a single Cpk computed over a month is meaningless; the
control-chart analysis in case study A7 shows how to check stability before trusting
capability.
