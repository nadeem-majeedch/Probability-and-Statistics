## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The standard error of the sample mean is
A) \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\)
B) \(\sigma^2/n\)
C) \(s\)
D) \(\sigma\)
* A
Why: The mean's variability shrinks with the square root of the sample size. \(\sigma^2/n\) is the variance of the mean, not its standard deviation.

Q2. If the sample size is quadrupled, the standard error of the mean
A) is unchanged
B) halves
C) quarters
D) doubles
* B
Why: \(1/\sqrt{4}=1/2\). Precision improves slowly: four times the data buys twice the precision, which is why very large samples give diminishing returns.

Q3. The \(t\) distribution is used instead of the normal when
A) the mean is zero
B) the population standard deviation is unknown and estimated by \(s\)
C) the sample is large
D) the data are categorical
* B
Why: Replacing \(\sigma\) by \(s\) adds variability, which the heavier tails of \(t\) account for. As \(n\) grows, \(s\) approaches \(\sigma\) and \(t\) approaches the normal.

Q4. Compared with the standard normal, the \(t\) distribution has
A) bounded support
B) lighter tails
C) heavier tails
D) the same tails for all \(n\)
* C
Why: The extra uncertainty from estimating the variance produces more extreme values than the normal would allow, especially for small degrees of freedom.

Q5. For a one-sample \(t\) procedure with \(n=20\), the degrees of freedom are
A) 19
B) 21
C) 18
D) 20
* A
Why: One degree of freedom is used by the estimated mean, leaving \(n-1=19\). Using \(n\) would make the critical value too small.

Q6. The sampling distribution of a statistic is
A) the distribution of the data
B) the distribution of the statistic over repeated samples
C) always normal
D) the distribution of the population
* B
Why: It describes how the statistic would vary if the study were repeated, and its spread is what standard errors and p-values quantify.

Q7. The standard error of a sample proportion is
A) \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\)
B) \(p(1-p)\)
C) \(p/n\)
D) \(\sqrt{n/p}\)
* A
Why: The proportion is a mean of Bernoulli variables with variance \(p(1-p)\), so its standard error is the square root of the variance divided by \(n\).

Q8. The sample variance \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2\) follows
A) a \(t\) distribution
B) an \(F\) distribution
C) a normal distribution
D) a chi-square distribution with \(n-1\) df
* D
Why: Sums of squared standard normal deviations are chi-square; estimating the mean costs one degree of freedom. This is the basis of variance intervals and tests.

Q9. The \(F\) distribution arises as
A) the square of a \(t\)
B) a weighted mean
C) the ratio of two independent chi-square variables divided by their degrees of freedom
D) the sum of two normals
* C
Why: It compares two variance estimates, which is why ANOVA and variance-ratio tests use it. A \(t\) variable squared with \(d\) df is \(F(1,d)\), a special case.

Q10. The finite population correction factor is
A) \(n/N\)
B) \(\sqrt{n/N}\)
C) \(1-n\)
D) \(\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}\)
* D
Why: Sampling without replacement from a finite population reduces variability because large samples exhaust the population. The factor approaches 1 when \(n\) is small relative to \(N\).

Q11. The standard error differs from the standard deviation because it describes
A) the spread of individual observations
B) the spread of an estimator across samples
C) the bias of an estimator
D) the range of the data
* B
Why: The SD measures variability of the data; the SE measures uncertainty of a statistic. Reporting an SD as an SE understates uncertainty by a factor \(\sqrt{n}\).

Q12. Increasing the sample size reduces
A) both automatically
B) bias but not variance
C) variance but not bias
D) neither bias nor variance
* C
Why: Random error shrinks as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), but systematic bias from a bad sampling frame or a mismeasured instrument persists regardless of \(n\).

Q13. Compared with simple random sampling, stratified sampling usually
A) requires no sampling frame
B) increases the standard error
C) reduces the standard error when strata are internally homogeneous
D) removes all bias
* C
Why: Removing between-stratum variation from the within-stratum estimates reduces variance. It does not fix coverage or measurement bias.

Q14. A bootstrap standard error is estimated by
A) assuming normality
B) increasing the sample size
C) deleting outliers
D) resampling with replacement from the observed data and recomputing the statistic
* D
Why: Resampling approximates the sampling distribution without a formula, which is useful for medians, ratios and other statistics with no simple standard error.

Q15. The sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal for large \(n\) even when the
A) the variance is finite
B) the sample is biased
C) \(n<30\)
D) the data are symmetric
* A
Why: That is the central limit theorem, revisited in Module 13. Heavy-tailed distributions with infinite variance are the exception, and skew slows convergence.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain the difference between the standard deviation of the data and the standard error
of the mean.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The SD describes how spread out individual observations are and stays roughly constant as \(n\)
grows. The SE describes how much the sample mean would vary across repeated samples and shrinks
as \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). Reporting the SD when the SE is meant overstates precision.
</details>

**S2.** Why does the \(t\) distribution have heavier tails than the normal?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because \(s\) is itself estimated and varies between samples: sometimes it is too small, making
the ratio \((\bar x-\mu)/(s/\sqrt n)\) unusually large. Averaging over that extra randomness
produces a distribution with more probability in the tails, converging to normal as \(n\) grows.
</details>

**S3.** What does the finite population correction do, and when can it be ignored?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It multiplies the standard error by \(\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}\), reflecting that sampling without
replacement from a finite population gives more information than independent draws. It can be
ignored when the sampling fraction \(n/N\) is below about 5 %.
</details>

**S4.** Why is the sampling distribution central to inference?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because inference compares an observed statistic with what its sampling distribution would
produce under a hypothesis. Standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values and power all derive
from the spread and shape of that distribution.
</details>

**S5.** Explain why a biased sampling frame cannot be fixed by increasing the sample size.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Sampling error shrinks with \(n\), but a frame that omits part of the population produces the
same systematic error at every sample size. A larger sample gives a more precise estimate of the
wrong quantity — the classic failure of opt-in online polls.
</details>

**S6.** When is the normal approximation to the sampling distribution of a proportion adequate?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When both expected counts \(np\) and \(n(1-p)\) are at least about 10, so the distribution is
symmetric and bounded away from 0 and 1. For small counts the exact binomial or a Wilson
interval is preferable.
</details>

**S7.** What is a cluster sample, and what is its main statistical cost?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Whole groups (schools, clinics) are selected and all units within them observed. Units within a
cluster are usually similar, so the effective sample size is smaller than the number of
individuals; the design effect inflates standard errors, which must account for clustering.
</details>

**S8.** Why might a bootstrap fail for the maximum of a sample?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The bootstrap resamples observed values, so the resampled maximum never exceeds the observed
maximum; the bootstrap distribution is shifted and cannot capture the tail beyond the data. For
such non-regular statistics, parametric or extreme-value methods are needed.
</details>

**S9.** Explain the difference between an estimator's standard error and its bias.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Bias is the systematic difference between the estimator's average value and the true parameter;
standard error is the random variability around that average. An estimator can be unbiased with
a large SE, or biased with a small SE; MSE combines both.
</details>

**S10.** In a survey of 1,000 people, the proportion supporting a policy is 0.52 with a standard
error of 0.016. Why is "52 % support" not a precise statement?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because 0.52 is an estimate with uncertainty: an approximate 95 % interval is
\(0.52\pm1.96(0.016)=(0.489,0.551)\), which includes values below 50 %. The data are consistent
with a minority in favour, so the direction is not established.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** A population has \(\sigma=10\). For samples of size 25, compute the standard error of the
mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=10/\sqrt{25}=10/5=2\). About 95 % of sample means would fall within
\(\pm3.92\) of the population mean if the sampling distribution is normal.
</details>

**N2.** What sample size gives a standard error of 1 when \(\sigma=10\)?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(10/\sqrt n=1\) gives \(\sqrt n=10\), so \(n=100\). Precision improves as the square of the
sample size: halving the SE requires four times the data.
</details>

**N3.** A proportion is estimated from 400 observations and the true proportion is 0.4. Compute
the standard error.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.4\times0.6/400}=\sqrt{0.24/400}=\sqrt{0.0006}=0.0245\). A 95 %
interval would be roughly \(0.4\pm0.048\).
</details>

**N4.** A population of \(N=500\) is sampled without replacement with \(n=50\) and \(\sigma=12\).
Compute the standard error with the finite population correction.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Uncorrected: \(12/\sqrt{50}=1.697\). Correction:
\(\sqrt{(500-50)/(500-1)}=\sqrt{450/499}=0.9496\). Corrected SE
\(=1.697\times0.9496=1.612\). The 10 % sampling fraction reduces the SE by about 5 %.
</details>

**N5.** A sample of 20 has mean 50 and \(s=5\). Compute the 95 % \(t\)-based margin of error
(\(t_{0.975,19}=2.093\)).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=5/\sqrt{20}=1.118\); margin \(=2.093\times1.118=2.34\)**. The interval is
\((47.66,52.34)\). Using 1.96 instead would understate the margin because \(s\) is estimated.
</details>

**N6.** A sample of 25 gives mean 52, with \(\sigma=10\) and hypothesised mean 50. Compute the
\(z\)-statistic for the sample mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=10/5=2\), so \(z=(52-50)/2=1\)**. A value this large or larger occurs with
probability about 0.32 in a two-sided test — no evidence against the hypothesised mean.
</details>

**N7.** If \(s^2\) is the sample variance from \(n=10\) normal observations, what are the mean and
variance of \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2\)?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
The statistic is chi-square with \(9\) df, so its mean is \(9\) and variance \(2\times9=18\).
This is why variance estimates are skewed for small samples.
</details>

**N8.** In a sample of 100, 55 succeed. Test the proportion against 0.5 using a \(z\)-statistic.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Under \(H_0\), \(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.25/100}=0.05\), so \(z=(0.55-0.5)/0.05=1.0\). The
two-sided p-value is about 0.32; a 5-point difference in 100 observations is well within noise.
</details>

**N9.** Two independent samples: group 1 has \(\sigma_1=10\), \(n_1=25\); group 2 has
\(\sigma_2=8\), \(n_2=16\). Compute the standard error of the difference in means.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{100/25+64/16}=\sqrt{4+4}=\sqrt8=2.828\). Variances of independent
estimates add even when the estimates are subtracted.
</details>

**N10.** How large a sample is needed for a 95 % margin of error of 0.03 on a proportion, using
the conservative \(p=0.5\)?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(n=(1.96/0.03)^2\times0.25=4268.4\times0.25=1067.1\), so round up to \(1{,}068\). Polls
reporting ±3 % therefore need about 1,000 respondents.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** An A/B test shows conversion 5.0 % in control and 5.6 % in treatment with 10,000 users
per arm. Compute the standard error of the difference and judge the result.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.05(0.95)/10{,}000+0.056(0.944)/10{,}000}
=\sqrt{0.00000475+0.00000529}=0.00317\). The difference 0.006 is 1.9 SE, a two-sided p-value
near 0.06 — suggestive but not conclusive at 5 %. Report the interval, not just the point.
</details>

**P2.** A dashboard shows average session length from 300 sessions as 4.2 minutes with SD 6.0.
The product team asks how precise the number is. What do you report?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(\mathrm{SE}=6/\sqrt{300}=0.346\); a 95 % interval is about \(4.2\pm0.68\), i.e. (3.5, 4.9).
Because session length is heavily skewed, the interval is approximate; report the median as well
and consider a bootstrap or log-scale analysis.
</details>

**P3.** A survey uses a cluster sample of 20 classrooms with 30 students each. Explain why the
effective sample size is below 600.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Students within a classroom share a teacher and neighbourhood, so their answers are correlated.
The design effect \(1+(m-1)\rho\) with cluster size \(m=30\) and intraclass correlation \(\rho\)
inflates the variance; even \(\rho=0.05\) gives a design effect of 2.45, so the effective sample is
about 245. Standard errors must account for clustering.
</details>

**P4.** A model is trained on a 5 % sample of transactions and evaluated on the remaining 95 %.
Is the evaluation standard error smaller than the training one? Explain.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Yes, if the evaluation set is independent and much larger: the SE of an estimated accuracy scales
as \(1/\sqrt{n_{\text{eval}}}\), so the 95 % holdout gives about \(\sqrt{19}\approx4.4\) times
smaller SE than evaluating on a 5 % set. But both sets must be drawn from the same population; a
temporal split may change the target quantity entirely.
</details>

**P5.** You bootstrap a median 2,000 times and obtain a 95 % interval that is asymmetric. Is that
a problem?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
No — it is informative. The sampling distribution of a median is skewed for skewed data, and the
percentile interval reflects that asymmetry honestly. A symmetric normal interval would be
misleading. Check the number of resamples and the stability of the endpoints.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\) for independent observations and state where
independence is used.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i\), so
\(\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\mathrm{Var}(X_i)+\frac{2}{n^2}\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\).
Independence makes every covariance zero, leaving \(\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2=\sigma^2/n\).
With positive dependence the variance is larger, which is the cluster-sampling problem.
</details>

**C2.** Show that the sample proportion's variance is \(p(1-p)/n\) and use it to derive the sample
size for a given margin of error.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
With \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)\), \(\hat p=X/n\), so
\(\mathrm{Var}(\hat p)=np(1-p)/n^2=p(1-p)/n\). A margin \(m\) at confidence \(1-\alpha\) requires
\(z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\le m\), giving
\(n\ge z^2p(1-p)/m^2\). The conservative choice \(p=1/2\) maximises \(p(1-p)\) and hence \(n\).
</details>

**C3.** Explain why \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\), and why the degrees of freedom are
\(n-1\) rather than \(n\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \(\sum(X_i-\mu)^2/\sigma^2=\sum Z_i^2\sim\chi^2_n\) for standardised deviations. The
identity \(\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\sum(X_i-\mu)^2-n(\bar X-\mu)^2\) splits the total into the
within-sample sum plus one squared standard normal term. The two parts are independent, so the
first has \(\chi^2\) with \(n-1\) df: one dimension of the deviations is fixed by the constraint
\(\sum(X_i-\bar X)=0\).
</details>
