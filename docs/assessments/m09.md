## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. A binomial model requires
A) a Poisson arrival process
B) a continuous outcome
C) a fixed number of independent trials with constant success probability
D) draws without replacement
* C
Why: Fixed \(n\), independence, two outcomes and constant \(p\) define the binomial. Without replacement the trials are dependent and the hypergeometric applies.

Q2. For \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)\), the mean and variance are
A) \(n/p\) and \(n(1-p)/p^2\)
B) \(np\) and \(np(1-p)\)
C) \(p\) and \(p(1-p)\)
D) \(np\) and \(np\)
* B
Why: A binomial is a sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli variables, so means add to \(np\) and variances add to \(np(1-p)\). The other pairs belong to Bernoulli or Poisson.

Q3. For a Poisson variable, the mean and variance are
A) unrelated
B) equal
C) variance = mean squared
D) variance = \(\sqrt{\text{mean}}\)
* B
Why: Both equal \(\lambda\). This equidispersion property is the basis of a common model check: over-dispersion (variance > mean) means the Poisson is inappropriate.

Q4. The geometric distribution models
A) the number of events in a fixed interval
B) the number of failures before \(r\) successes
C) the number of successes in \(n\) trials
D) the number of trials until the first success
* D
Why: Each trial is independent with constant success probability, and the variable counts trials up to and including the first success. The failure count before \(r\) successes is the negative binomial.

Q5. The probability of at least one event for \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)\) is
A) \(1/\lambda\)
B) \(e^{-\lambda}\)
C) \(1-e^{-\lambda}\)
D) \(\lambda e^{-\lambda}\)
* C
Why: \(P(X\ge1)=1-P(X=0)=1-e^{-\lambda}\). For \(\lambda=1\) this is about 0.632, not 1.

Q6. If events arrive at 3 per hour, the count in 20 minutes follows
A) \(\mathrm{Poisson}(0.33)\)
B) \(\mathrm{Binomial}(20,3)\)
C) \(\mathrm{Poisson}(3)\)
D) \(\mathrm{Poisson}(1)\)
* D
Why: The Poisson mean scales with exposure: \(3\times(20/60)=1\). Using 3 in a 20-minute window overstates the rate threefold.

Q7. The Poisson approximates the binomial well when
A) \(p=0.5\) always
B) \(n=p\)
C) \(n\) is small and \(p\) is large
D) \(n\) is large and \(p\) is small, with \(np\) moderate
* D
Why: Rare events in many trials give an approximately Poisson count with \(\lambda=np\). The rule of thumb is \(n\ge20\), \(p\le0.05\), though the approximation depends on \(np\).

Q8. The negative binomial counts
A) successes without replacement
B) successes in \(n\) trials
C) failures before the \(r\)-th success
D) events per unit time
* C
Why: It generalises the geometric distribution to the \(r\)-th success. Its variance exceeds its mean, which is why it is also used for over-dispersed counts.

Q9. Sampling 5 items without replacement from a lot of 20 containing 3 defectives gives a
A) hypergeometric distribution
B) Poisson distribution
C) geometric distribution
D) binomial distribution
* A
Why: The success probability changes after each draw because the lot composition changes. The binomial would require independence, which replacement or a very large lot would provide.

Q10. \(P(X=k)\) for \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)\) is
A) \(n p^k\)
B) \(\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\)
C) \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\)
D) \(\binom{n}{k}p^{n-k}(1-p)^k\)
* B
Why: Choose which \(k\) trials succeed, then multiply the probability of those successes and the remaining failures. Omitting the binomial coefficient counts only one ordering.

Q11. For \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(4)\), \(P(X=0)\) is
A) 0.9817
B) 4
C) 0.0183
D) 0.25
* C
Why: \(e^{-4}=0.0183\). The complementary value 0.9817 is \(P(X\ge1)\), a common misreading.

Q12. A Poisson variable has variance 3. Its standard deviation is
A) 1.732
B) 9
C) 0.577
D) 3
* A
Why: Variance equals the mean, so the mean is 3 and the SD is \(\sqrt3=1.732\). The variance is not the SD.

Q13. The mean of a geometric distribution with success probability \(p\) is
A) \(1/p\)
B) \((1-p)/p\)
C) \(p/(1-p)\)
D) \(p\)
* A
Why: The expected number of trials to the first success is \(1/p\); the expected number of failures before it is \((1-p)/p\). Reading the question's definition of the variable is essential.

Q14. If \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(2)\) and \(Y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(5)\) are independent, then
A) \(\mathrm{Poisson}(7)\)
B) \(\mathrm{Poisson}(10)\)
C) binomial
D) normal
* A
Why: Independent Poisson variables add, with means adding: \(2+5=7\). This closure property is proved with probability generating functions.

Q15. A binomial count with \(n=100\) and \(p=0.02\) has mean and variance
A) 2 and 2
B) 2 and 1.96
C) 2 and 0.02
D) 20 and 1.96
* B
Why: Mean \(=np=2\); variance \(=np(1-p)=2\times0.98=1.96\). The Poisson approximation would set both to 2, close but not exact.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** State the four conditions for a binomial distribution and give an example where one
fails.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Fixed number of trials \(n\); two outcomes per trial; constant success probability \(p\);
independent trials. Sampling without replacement from a small population violates independence
and constant \(p\), so the hypergeometric applies instead.
</details>

**S2.** Explain why the Poisson mean equals its variance, and what over-dispersion signals.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For a Poisson process, events arrive independently at a constant rate, and the derivation gives
\(E[X]=\mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda\). If observed variance exceeds the mean, events are clustered or
the rate varies, so a Poisson model understates uncertainty and a negative binomial or
mixed model is needed.
</details>

**S3.** Why is the geometric distribution memoryless, and what does that imply practically?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
\(P(X>m+n\mid X>m)=P(X>n)\) because past failures do not change future independent trials. So
waiting time already spent does not make a success "due" — the gambler's fallacy. The same
property holds for the continuous exponential.
</details>

**S4.** When is the Poisson approximation to the binomial justified?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When \(n\) is large, \(p\) is small and \(np\) is moderate (roughly \(n\ge20\), \(p\le0.05\)).
Then \(\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\approx e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\) with \(\lambda=np\), because
\((1-p)^n\approx e^{-np}\). It fails when \(p\) is not small.
</details>

**S5.** Explain the difference between the geometric and negative binomial distributions.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The geometric counts trials (or failures) until the first success; the negative binomial counts
failures before the \(r\)-th success, and reduces to the geometric when \(r=1\). The negative
binomial has larger variance relative to its mean, which is why it models over-dispersed counts.
</details>

**S6.** Why does the hypergeometric variance include a finite-population correction?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Without replacement, draws are negatively dependent: a success reduces the chance of another, so
the count varies less than independent draws would. The factor \((N-n)/(N-1)\) shrinks the
binomial variance, disappearing as \(N\to\infty\) relative to \(n\).
</details>

**S7.** A call centre receives 2 calls per minute on average. Why might a Poisson model fail in
practice?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Calls cluster (lunchtime peaks, outages), so the rate is not constant and arrivals are not
independent; the variance then exceeds the mean. Also, simultaneous calls may be limited by
trunk capacity. A time-varying or over-dispersed model is more realistic.
</details>

**S8.** Explain how to simulate a binomial count from Bernoulli trials, and why that is useful.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Generate \(n\) independent Bernoulli outcomes with probability \(p\) and sum them; the sum is
binomial. Simulation checks analytic results, handles non-standard variants (varying \(p\), small
\(n\)), and makes the meaning of "independent trials" concrete.
</details>

**S9.** Why is the Poisson distribution a limit of the binomial, and which parameter is held
fixed?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Hold \(\lambda=np\) fixed while \(n\to\infty\) and \(p\to0\). Then the binomial probability tends
to \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\). The limit describes many trials each with a tiny chance of
success — the typical structure of rare-event counts.
</details>

**S10.** Two models fit count data: Poisson and negative binomial. How would you choose between
them?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Compare the sample mean and variance; if variance substantially exceeds the mean, the Poisson is
misspecified. Fit both, compare AIC or likelihood, and check residual dispersion and
predictive performance. Prefer the simpler Poisson when equidispersion holds.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(10,0.3)\). Compute \(P(X=3)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(X=3)=\binom{10}{3}(0.3)^3(0.7)^7=120\times0.027\times0.08235=0.2668\). The coefficient
120 counts the orderings; omitting it gives a probability about 120 times too small.
</details>

**N2.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(4)\). Compute \(P(X=0)\) and \(P(X\ge1)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(X=0)=e^{-4}=0.0183\); \(P(X\ge1)=1-0.0183=0.9817\). The complement is much easier
than summing the tail.
</details>

**N3.** A geometric variable has \(p=0.2\). Compute the mean and \(P(X=3)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean \(=1/0.2=5\) trials. \(P(X=3)=(0.8)^2(0.2)=0.64\times0.2=0.128\). The first two
trials must fail and the third succeed.
</details>

**N4.** Calls arrive at 2 per hour. Find the probability of no calls in 15 minutes.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Rate for 15 minutes: \(\lambda=2\times0.25=0.5\). \(P(X=0)=e^{-0.5}=0.6065\). Using 2
instead of 0.5 would wrongly give 0.1353.
</details>

**N5.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(20,0.05)\). Compute the mean, variance and SD.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean \(=20\times0.05=1\); variance \(=1\times0.95=0.95\); SD \(=0.975\). The Poisson
approximation would give mean 1 and variance 1, close but slightly over-dispersed.
</details>

**N6.** A lot of 20 items contains 3 defectives; 5 are drawn without replacement. Find
\(P(X=0)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(X=0)=\frac{\binom{3}{0}\binom{17}{5}}{\binom{20}{5}}=\frac{6{,}188}{15{,}504}=0.399\).
The binomial with \(p=0.15\) would give \(0.85^5=0.444\), overstating the chance of a clean
sample.
</details>

**N7.** A basketball player scores a free throw with probability 0.8, independently. What is the
probability she needs exactly 3 attempts for her first success?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Geometric: \(P(X=3)=(0.2)^2(0.8)=0.04\times0.8=0.032\). Two misses then a success; the
expected number of attempts is \(1/0.8=1.25\).
</details>

**N8.** \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(3)\). Compute \(P(X=2)\) and \(P(X\le2)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(X=2)=e^{-3}3^2/2=0.0498\times4.5=0.2240\). \(P(X\le2)=P(0)+P(1)+P(2)
=0.0498+0.1494+0.2240=0.4232\).
</details>

**N9.** A negative binomial counts failures before the 3rd success with \(p=0.5\). Compute
\(P(X=2)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
The 5th trial is the 3rd success: \(\binom{4}{2}(0.5)^5=6/32=0.1875\). The coefficient
chooses the positions of the two failures among the first four trials.
</details>

**N10.** Out of 1,000 emails, 2 % are spam. Approximate the probability that exactly 20 are spam
using the Poisson model.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\lambda=1{,}000\times0.02=20\), so \(P(X=20)=e^{-20}20^{20}/20!\approx0.0884\). The exact
binomial value is about 0.0897; the approximation is close because \(n\) is large and \(p\) is
small.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A support desk receives 5 tickets per hour. Staffing handles 8 per hour. Estimate the
probability that a given hour exceeds capacity, and discuss the modelling assumptions.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
With \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(5)\), \(P(X>8)=1-P(X\le8)\approx1-0.9319=0.068\), about 7 % of
hours. Assumes a constant rate and independent arrivals; real traffic has daily peaks, so
compute per-shift rates and consider over-dispersion, then staff to the 95th percentile of each
shift.
</details>

**P2.** A factory samples 10 items per hour and stops production if 2 or more are defective.
Defect rate is 5 %. Find the false-alarm probability.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(P(X\ge2)=1-P(0)-P(1)\) with \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(10,0.05)\):
\(1-0.5987-0.3151=0.0861\), about 8.6 % of hours stopped with no change in the process.
Alert fatigue follows; choose limits from the acceptable false-alarm rate and consider cumulative
sum charts.
</details>

**P3.** A website's signup conversions are 3 % per visitor, independent. How many visitors are
needed so that the chance of at least one signup exceeds 90 %?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(1-0.97^n>0.9\) gives \(0.97^n<0.1\), so \(n>\ln0.1/\ln0.97=2.3026/0.03046=75.6\), i.e. 76
visitors. Note this is about conversions, not statistical power — a different calculation.
</details>

**P4.** A model predicts 12 claims per day, but the observed variance across days is 40. What
does this tell you, and what would you fit instead?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Variance (40) far exceeds the mean (12): strong over-dispersion, so the Poisson understates
uncertainty. Fit a negative binomial or a Poisson mixed model with a random day effect; check
whether the extra variation comes from a few catastrophic days.
</details>

**P5.** A retailer sends 500 catalogues; the response rate is 0.5 %. Estimate the number of
orders and the probability of zero orders, and comment on the Poisson approximation.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(\lambda=2.5\); expected orders 2.5; \(P(0)=e^{-2.5}=0.0821\). The binomial with
\(n=500,p=0.005\) gives essentially the same value, so the Poisson approximation is adequate. If
responses cluster within households, independence fails and the variance would be larger.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive the mean and variance of the binomial distribution from the Bernoulli
decomposition.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \(X=\sum_{i=1}^n I_i\) with \(I_i\) independent Bernoulli(\(p\)). Then
\(E[X]=\sum E[I_i]=np\). Independence gives
\(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sum\mathrm{Var}(I_i)=np(1-p)\), since each Bernoulli variance is \(p(1-p)\).
Without independence the cross-covariances would have to be added.
</details>

**C2.** Show that the Poisson distribution arises as the limit of the binomial with
\(np=\lambda\) fixed as \(n\to\infty\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \(p=\lambda/n\). Then
\(\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}\frac{\lambda^k}{n^k}
\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k}\). The first factor tends to 1 (it is a product of \(k\)
terms each tending to 1 after dividing by \(n^k\)), \((1-\lambda/n)^n\to e^{-\lambda}\), and
\((1-\lambda/n)^{-k}\to1\). The limit is \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\).
</details>

**C3.** Prove that the geometric distribution is memoryless, and show that among discrete
distributions on \(\{1,2,\dots\}\) it is the only one with this property.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(P(X>m+n\mid X>m)=\frac{P(X>m+n)}{P(X>m)}=\frac{(1-p)^{m+n}}{(1-p)^m}=(1-p)^n=P(X>n)\).
Conversely, let \(g(n)=P(X>n)\). Memorylessness gives \(g(m+n)=g(m)g(n)\), whose only
non-increasing solution with \(g(0)=1\) is \(g(n)=q^n\), so \(P(X=n)=q^{n-1}(1-q)\) — geometric.
</details>
