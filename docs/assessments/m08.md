## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. For constants \(a\) and \(b\), \(E[aX+b]\) equals
A) \(aE[X]+b\)
B) \(aE[X]+ab\)
C) \(aE[X]\)
D) \(E[X]+b\)
* A
Why: Expectation is linear, so the constant \(b\) passes through unchanged and the scaling by \(a\) applies to the expectation. Forgetting to add \(b\) is the usual slip.

Q2. \(\mathrm{Var}(aX+b)\) equals
A) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\)
B) \(a\,\mathrm{Var}(X)+b\)
C) \(a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)\)
D) \(a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)+b^2\)
* C
Why: Shifts do not change spread, while scaling by \(a\) multiplies deviations by \(a\) and hence the variance by \(a^2\). The constant \(b\) contributes nothing.

Q3. A probability mass function must satisfy
A) \(p(x)\) increasing in \(x\)
B) \(p(x)\ge0\) and \(\sum_x p(x)=1\)
C) \(p(x)\le1\) only
D) \(\sum_x p(x)=0\)
* B
Why: Non-negativity and normalisation are the defining conditions; any assignment satisfying them describes a valid discrete distribution. Individual values above 1 are impossible for a pmf, but a density may exceed 1.

Q4. The expectation of a fair die roll is
A) 3.5 squared
B) 4
C) 3
D) 3.5
* D
Why: \((1+2+3+4+5+6)/6=21/6=3.5\). The mean need not be an attainable value of the variable.

Q5. For a Bernoulli variable with success probability \(p\), the variance is
A) \(np(1-p)\)
B) \(p^2\)
C) \(p\)
D) \(p(1-p)\)
* D
Why: \(E[X^2]=p\) because \(X^2=X\) for 0/1 values, so \(\mathrm{Var}=p-p^2=p(1-p)\). The \(np(1-p)\) form is the variance of a binomial count.

Q6. A cumulative distribution function \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\) is always
A) non-decreasing, right-continuous, with limits 0 and 1
B) symmetric
C) continuous
D) decreasing
* A
Why: Probabilities accumulate, so \(F\) cannot decrease, and it approaches 0 and 1 at the ends. It can jump (discrete variables), so continuity is not required.

Q7. For a continuous random variable, \(P(X=x)\) equals
A) \(f(x)\)
B) 0
C) \(F(x)\)
D) \(1\)
* B
Why: Probability is area under the density, and a single point has zero width. This is why continuous variables are described by interval probabilities.

Q8. \(E[X^2]\) is always
A) non-negative
B) at least \(E[X]\)
C) equal to \((E[X])^2\)
D) zero
* A
Why: A square is non-negative, so its expectation is too. It equals \((E[X])^2\) only when \(X\) is constant, and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\ge0\).

Q9. Linearity of expectation requires
A) identical distributions
B) no assumptions beyond finite expectations
C) normality
D) independence
* B
Why: \(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\) holds for any random variables with finite means. Independence is needed for variances to add, not for means.

Q10. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)\) equals
A) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)\)
B) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\)
C) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\mathrm{Var}(Y)\)
D) \([\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)]^2\)
* A
Why: Independence makes the covariance zero, so variances add. The general formula includes the covariance term, which can be negative.

Q11. The units of \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\) are
A) unit-free
B) undefined
C) the same as \(X\)
D) squared units of \(X\)
* D
Why: Squaring deviations squares the units. The standard deviation restores them, which is one reason it is easier to report.

Q12. \(E[1/X]\) compared with \(1/E[X]\) is generally
A) larger
B) smaller
C) undefined
D) equal
* A
Why: \(1/x\) is convex for \(x>0\), so Jensen's inequality gives \(E[1/X]\ge1/E[X]\). The two are equal only when \(X\) is constant.

Q13. For an indicator variable \(I_A\), \(E[I_A]\) equals
A) \(P(A)\)
B) \(1-P(A)\)
C) \(P(A)^2\)
D) 1
* A
Why: The indicator is 1 with probability \(P(A)\), so its expectation is \(1\cdot P(A)+0\cdot(1-P(A))\). This trick converts probability statements into expectations.

Q14. The moment generating function of \(X\) is defined as
A) \(E[X^t]\)
B) \(E[e^{tX}]\)
C) \(E[X]^t\)
D) \(E[e^{X}]\)
* B
Why: \(M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]\) generates moments by differentiation at \(t=0\): \(M^{(k)}(0)=E[X^k]\). It exists only when the expectation is finite near zero.

Q15. A median \(m\) of a continuous distribution satisfies
A) \(f(m)\) is maximal
B) \(P(X\le m)=0.5\)
C) \(E[X]=m\)
D) \(m\) is the most likely value
* B
Why: The median splits probability in half. The maximiser of the density is the mode, and the mean equals the median only for symmetric distributions.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain why the expectation of a random variable is not necessarily a value the
variable can take.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Expectation is a probability-weighted average over the support. A fair die has mean 3.5, which no
roll can produce; a Bernoulli variable with \(p=0.3\) has mean 0.3. The mean describes the
long-run average, not a possible outcome.
</details>

**S2.** State the two defining properties of a probability mass function and explain what each
excludes.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Non-negativity excludes negative probabilities; summing to 1 excludes unnormalised weights that
would make total probability other than 1. Together they guarantee the distribution describes a
proper random experiment.
</details>

**S3.** What does the cumulative distribution function add beyond the pmf or density?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It gives interval probabilities directly, \(P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)\), works uniformly for
discrete and continuous variables, defines quantiles by inversion, and is the natural object for
the probability integral transform and for comparing distributions.
</details>

**S4.** Explain the difference between \(E[X^2]\) and \((E[X])^2\), and why the difference
matters.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
\(E[X^2]\) averages the squared values; \((E[X])^2\) squares the average. Their difference is
the variance, so it measures dispersion. Using \((E[X])^2\) where \(E[X^2]\) is required
understates the mean square and corrupts the variance calculation.
</details>

**S5.** Why is variance defined with squared deviations rather than absolute deviations?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Squares are differentiable, decompose additively (between- plus within-group variance), and make
the variance of a sum depend on covariances in a simple way. Absolute deviations give a more
robust but less tractable measure, which is why both exist.
</details>

**S6.** What is the law of the unconscious statistician, and why is it useful?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It states that \(E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p(x)\) (or \(\int g(x)f(x)dx\)) — you can average the
transformed values directly without deriving the distribution of \(g(X)\). This saves work when
only the expectation of a function is needed.
</details>

**S7.** Why does \(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\) hold even when \(X\) and \(Y\) are dependent?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because expectation is an integral (or sum) and integration is linear: the joint distribution
factors out of each term regardless of dependence. Dependence affects the variance of the sum
through the covariance, not the mean.
</details>

**S8.** Give an example where a random variable has a finite mean but an infinite variance, and
explain why that matters in practice.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A Pareto distribution with tail index between 1 and 2, or a t distribution with 2 degrees of
freedom, has a finite mean but infinite variance. Sample variances then fluctuate wildly and
never settle, so standard errors based on them are unreliable — a warning against routinely
assuming finite variance.
</details>

**S9.** What does it mean for an estimator to be a random variable?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
An estimator is a function of the sample, and the sample is random, so the estimator takes a
different value in each possible sample. Its distribution — the sampling distribution — has a
mean (bias) and a variance, which together determine its accuracy.
</details>

**S10.** Why is the moment generating function useful when it exists?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It encodes all moments through derivatives at zero, identifies distributions uniquely, and turns
sums of independent variables into products: \(M_{X+Y}=M_XM_Y\). That is how the normal and
Poisson distributions are shown to be closed under addition.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** A fair die is rolled. Compute \(E[X]\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\) and \(E[X^2]\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(E[X]=3.5\). \(E[X^2]=(1+4+9+16+25+36)/6=91/6=15.1667\). Variance
\(=15.1667-12.25=2.9167\)** \(=35/12\). Check: \(E[X^2]=\mathrm{Var}+(E[X])^2\).
</details>

**N2.** \(X\) takes 0, 1, 2 with probabilities 0.2, 0.5, 0.3. Compute \(E[X]\), \(E[X^2]\) and
\(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(E[X]=0(0.2)+1(0.5)+2(0.3)=1.1\). \(E[X^2]=0+0.5+4(0.3)=1.7\). Variance
\(=1.7-1.21=0.49\), standard deviation 0.7.
</details>

**N3.** \(E[X]=4\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=9\). Compute \(E[3X+2]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(3X+2)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(E[3X+2]=3(4)+2=14\). \(\mathrm{Var}(3X+2)=9\times9=81\), so the standard deviation is
9. The added constant affects only the mean.
</details>

**N4.** \(E[X]=5\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=4\). Compute \(\mathrm{Var}(-2X+5)\) and
\(E[X^2]\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{Var}(-2X+5)=4\times4=16\) (the sign disappears when squared).
\(E[X^2]=\mathrm{Var}+(E[X])^2=4+25=29\).
</details>

**N5.** A Bernoulli trial has \(p=0.3\). Compute the mean and standard deviation.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean \(=p=0.3\); variance \(=0.3\times0.7=0.21\), so SD \(=\sqrt{0.21}=0.458\). The SD
exceeds the mean here because the variable is bounded at 0 and 1.
</details>

**N6.** \(X\) has \(E[X]=6\) and \(E[X^2]=45\). Compute \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\) and the SD.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{Var}=45-36=9\), SD \(=3\). Always check that \(E[X^2]\ge(E[X])^2\); a negative
variance signals an arithmetic error.
</details>

**N7.** A fair die is rolled. Compute \(E[|X-3|]\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(|X-3|\) is 2, 1, 0, 1, 2, 3 for outcomes 1–6, summing to 9, so the expectation is
\(9/6=1.5\). The absolute deviation from 3 is not the same as the SD about the mean.
</details>

**N8.** For \(X\) Bernoulli(0.5), compute \(E[2^X]\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(E[2^X]=2^0(0.5)+2^1(0.5)=0.5+1=1.5\). Note this differs from \(2^{E[X]}=2^{0.5}=1.414\),
an illustration of Jensen's inequality for the convex function \(2^x\).
</details>

**N9.** A random variable has \(P(X=1)=0.4\), \(P(X=3)=0.1\), \(P(X=6)=0.5\). Compute the
expectation and the median.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(E[X]=0.4+0.3+3=3.7\). Cumulative probabilities: 0.4 at \(x=1\), 0.5 at \(x=3\), 1.0 at
\(x=6\). The smallest \(x\) with \(F(x)\ge0.5\) is 3, so the median is **3** — a value the mean
does not equal.
</details>

**N10.** A game pays 10 with probability 0.2 and costs 3 to play. Compute the expected net gain
and say whether the game is favourable.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Expected payout \(=0.2\times10=2\); net gain \(=2-3=-1\). The game loses 1 per play on
average, so it is unfavourable despite a possible 7 gain — expected value, not the best case, is
the long-run criterion.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A call centre models the number of calls per minute as a random variable with mean 4 and
variance 9. It staffs for 7 calls per minute. Use a rough bound to judge the risk of being
overloaded.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
By Chebyshev, \(P(|X-4|\ge3)\le9/9=1\), which is useless; but for a Poisson-like count with mean
4 the variance would be 4, and \(P(X\ge7)\) is about 0.11. The lesson: a variance of 9 versus a
mean of 4 signals over-dispersion, so a Poisson model is inappropriate and the staffing decision
needs the actual distribution or simulation.
</details>

**P2.** An insurance portfolio has expected annual claim cost 1,200 per policy and SD 4,000. Why
is the SD larger than the mean, and what does that imply for pricing?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Most policies have no claim, while a few produce large ones, so the distribution is heavy-tailed
and over-dispersed. Pricing on the mean alone ignores the capital needed for the tail; the
insurer must hold reserves for high percentiles (or use a risk loading), estimated from the
distribution or by simulation, not from mean ± SD under a normal assumption.
</details>

**P3.** You must choose between two delivery partners. A has mean delay 2 days, SD 1; B has mean
2.5 days, SD 0.3. Which do you choose and on what criterion?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
If only the average matters, A is better. If lateness beyond a deadline is costly, B is
preferable: its 95th percentile is roughly 3.0 days versus about 3.6 for A (normal
approximation). The decision needs the loss function, not just the mean — this is the point of
modelling the whole distribution.
</details>

**P4.** A model's error is symmetric with mean 0 and SD 5. A colleague claims "errors are never
more than 10". Is that justified?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
No. A mean of 0 and SD of 5 say nothing about the maximum; if errors are normal, about 4.6 % of
them exceed 10 in absolute value daily. Only bounded distributions give hard guarantees, and a
bound must come from the data or the physics, not from the SD.
</details>

**P5.** A product's revenue is \(R=P\cdot Q\) with independent price \(P\) (mean 10, SD 1) and
quantity \(Q\) (mean 100, SD 20). Compute the mean revenue, and explain why its variance needs
more than \(\mathrm{Var}(P)+\mathrm{Var}(Q)\).

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(E[R]=10\times100=1{,}000\). For independent factors,
\(\mathrm{Var}(PQ)=\sigma_P^2\sigma_Q^2+\mu_P^2\sigma_Q^2+\mu_Q^2\sigma_P^2
=1\cdot400+100\cdot400+10{,}000\cdot1=50{,}400\)**, SD ≈ 224. The extra terms come from the
product: variability in price is amplified by the size of quantity and vice versa.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\) and use it to show
\(\mathrm{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2]-2\mu E[X]+\mu^2=E[X^2]-\mu^2\). Then
\(\mathrm{Var}(aX+b)=E[(aX+b-a\mu-b)^2]=E[a^2(X-\mu)^2]=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)\), because the shift
cancels inside the square.
</details>

**C2.** Prove that \(E[(X-c)^2]\) is minimised at \(c=E[X]\) and give the minimum value.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \((X-c)^2=(X-\mu)^2+2(\mu-c)(X-\mu)+(\mu-c)^2\) with \(\mu=E[X]\). Taking expectations,
the middle term vanishes because \(E[X-\mu]=0\), leaving
\(\mathrm{Var}(X)+(\mu-c)^2\), minimised at \(c=\mu\) with value \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\). This is the
mean-squared-error decomposition used throughout estimation.
</details>

**C3.** For independent Poisson variables \(X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_1)\) and
\(Y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_2)\), show that \(X+Y\) is Poisson with mean
\(\lambda_1+\lambda_2\), using probability generating functions.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
The pgf of a Poisson variable is \(G_X(s)=e^{\lambda_1(s-1)}\). Independence gives
\(G_{X+Y}(s)=G_X(s)G_Y(s)=e^{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)(s-1)}\), which is the pgf of a Poisson
variable with mean \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2\). Uniqueness of pgfs completes the proof.
</details>
