## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. To display the distribution of a single continuous variable, the best choice is
A) a stacked bar chart
B) a pie chart
C) a histogram
D) a line chart of the sorted values
* C
Why: A histogram bins the range and shows how mass is distributed across it, revealing shape, centre and spread. A pie chart needs categories, and a line chart of sorted values encodes rank rather than density.

Q2. A bar chart is preferred to a pie chart for comparing categories mainly because
A) bars do not need labels
B) bars use colour better
C) bar lengths are easier to compare accurately than pie angles and areas
D) pies cannot show percentages
* C
Why: People judge lengths on a common scale far more accurately than angles or arc areas. Pies also lose precision as the number of slices grows.

Q3. Income data are strongly right-skewed. Plotting them on a logarithmic axis is useful because it
A) removes the skew by deleting outliers
B) makes multiplicative differences appear as equal distances
C) converts the data to ranks
D) guarantees normality
* B
Why: A log axis compresses large values and expands small ones, so doubling always looks like the same distance. It does not alter the data or make the distribution normal.

Q4. A bar chart of quarterly revenue starts the vertical axis at 90 instead of 0. The main problem is that
A) small differences look much larger than they are
B) the bars must be horizontal
C) colours become ambiguous
D) the axis labels are wrong
* A
Why: Bars encode magnitude by length from the baseline, so truncating the baseline distorts the visual ratio. Line charts may legitimately truncate because they encode position, not length.

Q5. For a diverging scale on a map of temperature change, the correct palette is
A) diverging, with a neutral colour at zero and two hues for opposite signs
B) a rainbow palette
C) a single saturated colour
D) sequential, light to dark blue
* A
Why: Diverging data have a meaningful midpoint (no change), so two hues with a neutral centre communicate direction and magnitude. A rainbow has no perceptual order and is not colour-blind safe.

Q6. A scatter plot is the right choice when both variables are
A) ordered categories
B) counts in one variable only
C) categorical
D) numerical
* D
Why: Position on two quantitative axes shows the joint relationship and reveals clusters, outliers and non-linearity that a correlation alone would hide.

Q7. A box plot can fail to reveal
A) outliers
B) the median
C) the quartiles
D) a bimodal distribution
* D
Why: A box plot summarises five numbers, and two very different distributions can share them. A histogram or density plot is needed to see multiple peaks.

Q8. A line chart is appropriate when the horizontal axis is
A) unordered categories
B) time or another ordered continuum
C) a set of unrelated groups
D) a nominal variable with many levels
* B
Why: Connecting points asserts continuity and order between adjacent x-values, which only makes sense for a sequence such as time.

Q9. Small multiples are useful because they
A) increase the data-ink ratio by removing axes
B) let readers compare the same chart across groups using a shared scale
C) replace the need for a legend
D) allow more than five colours
* B
Why: Repeating a simple chart per group keeps the encoding consistent, so patterns and differences are visible without overplotting everything in one panel.

Q10. About 8 % of men have red–green colour vision deficiency. A chart should therefore avoid
A) relying on red versus green alone to encode categories
B) labelling axes
C) using a log scale
D) using position and length
* A
Why: Position, length and lightness differences remain readable for almost everyone; a red/green contrast can be indistinguishable. Add shape or direct labels as a second cue.

Q11. The "data-ink ratio" principle argues for
A) adding gridlines and 3-D effects to look professional
B) removing decoration that does not encode data
C) using as many colours as possible
D) plotting every variable in the dataset
* B
Why: Ink should carry information; heavy gridlines, shadows and 3-D effects add noise and can distort perception. Minimal, high-contrast marks make the data easier to read.

Q12. Changing histogram bin width typically changes
A) the apparent shape of the distribution
B) the sample size
C) the units
D) the mean of the data
* A
Why: Bins are a presentation choice: narrow bins show noise, wide bins hide features such as a second peak. The underlying data are unchanged.

Q13. In a chart of part-to-whole composition across five time periods, a stacked bar chart mainly suffers from
A) being unprintable
B) requiring a log axis
C) too few categories
D) making the non-baseline segments hard to compare across bars
* D
Why: Only the bottom segment has a common baseline; the others float, so readers compare lengths that start at different heights. A line chart of shares or small multiples usually works better.

Q14. Encoding a quantity by the area of a circle is risky because
A) circles cannot be labelled
B) perceived area grows more slowly than actual area, so differences are underestimated
C) area has units
D) circles cannot show zero
* B
Why: Visual area estimation is biased and imprecise; doubling the value quadruples the area, which readers systematically misjudge. Length or position is more accurate.

Q15. The most important element of a chart for a non-specialist audience is
A) a 3-D effect
B) a clear title stating the takeaway and labelled axes with units
C) as many series as possible
D) an unlabelled colour legend
* B
Why: Readers need to know what is being shown, in what units, and what conclusion is intended. A takeaway title and labelled axes do more for comprehension than any styling choice.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain the difference between a histogram and a bar chart, including the type of
variable each displays.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A histogram displays a continuous variable by binning its range; the bars touch because the bins
are contiguous intervals, and the area represents frequency or density. A bar chart displays
categories, with a gap between bars, because the categories are separate and unordered (or
ordered but not continuous).
</details>

**S2.** Why is a log scale helpful for latency or income data, and what must the reader be told?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
These variables span orders of magnitude and are right-skewed; a log axis makes equal
multiplicative changes equally spaced, so structure at both ends stays visible. The reader must
be told that the axis is logarithmic, otherwise distances are misread as additive.
</details>

**S3.** What is chartjunk, and why does it matter for statistical communication?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Chartjunk is visual decoration that does not encode data — 3-D effects, shadows, heavy
gradients, unnecessary gridlines. It raises cognitive load, can distort perceived magnitudes,
and competes with the signal; removing it makes comparisons faster and more accurate.
</details>

**S4.** When is a pie chart acceptable?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When there are very few slices (roughly two to four), the values are simple fractions of a
whole, and the message is a single dominant proportion. Even then, a bar chart or a labelled
stacked bar is usually more precise.
</details>

**S5.** Explain overplotting and two ways to reduce it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Overplotting occurs when many points occupy the same region, hiding density. Reduce it with
transparency (alpha), smaller marks, jitter, binning into hexbins or 2-D histograms, or by
plotting a sample and summarising the rest.
</details>

**S6.** Why should the y-axis of a bar chart start at zero, while a line chart need not?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Bars encode magnitude by length from the baseline, so a truncated axis changes the visual ratio
between bars. A line chart encodes position, and its slope is meaningful on any linear scale;
truncation there exaggerates change but does not falsify the encoding of level.
</details>

**S7.** What does a box plot show, and what does it hide?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It shows the median, quartiles, whiskers (typically 1.5 IQR) and flagged outliers. It hides the
shape: bimodality, gaps, and the number of observations in each region — a histogram or strip
plot is needed for those.
</details>

**S8.** Why is colour alone a poor encoding for more than about five categories, and what
should be added?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Human colour discrimination is limited and varies between viewers, and colour-blind users may
not separate certain pairs. Add position, direct labels, shape or ordering, and keep the number
of colours small.
</details>

**S9.** Explain the difference between a sequential and a categorical palette, with one correct
use of each.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A sequential palette varies lightness (and sometimes hue) in one direction to encode ordered
magnitude, such as population density. A categorical palette uses distinct hues of similar
lightness for unordered groups, such as product categories.
</details>

**S10.** A chart shows a strong upward trend, but the x-axis is not time and the points are
sorted by y. Why is the trend misleading?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Sorting by the plotted value guarantees a monotone curve by construction; the line implies a
sequence that does not exist in the data. Without an ordered x variable, a scatter plot is the
honest display.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** A variable ranges from 12 to 92. You want 8 equal-width histogram bins. Compute the bin
width and the bin edges.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Range = 80; width = 80/8 = **10**. Edges: 12, 22, 32, 42, 52, 62, 72, 82, 92. Check that the
upper edge equals 92 and that each observation falls in exactly one bin.
</details>

**N2.** A bar chart compares 100 and 98 with the axis starting at 95. Compute the visual ratio of
bar lengths and compare it with the true ratio.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Displayed lengths are 100 − 95 = 5 and 98 − 95 = 3, giving a visual ratio of **1.67**. The true
ratio is 100/98 = **1.02**. The chart exaggerates a 2 % difference into a 67 % one — the
truncated-baseline distortion.
</details>

**N3.** A histogram has counts 20 in 0–10, 50 in 10–20 and 30 in 20–30. What proportion of
observations exceeds 20, and what is the modal class?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Total = 100; above 20 is 30, so **30 %**. The modal class is 10–20 with 50 observations (50 %),
which is also where the peak of the histogram lies.
</details>

**N4.** A five-number summary is 4, 9, 12, 18, 40. Compute the IQR and the upper whisker under
the 1.5 IQR rule, and say whether 40 is flagged.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
IQR = 18 − 9 = **9**; upper fence = 18 + 1.5×9 = **31.5**. The value 40 exceeds 31.5, so it is
flagged as a potential outlier and would be plotted as an individual point beyond the whisker
(which ends at the largest observation within the fence).
</details>

**N5.** Values 10, 100, 1000 are plotted on a log10 axis. Give their positions relative to each
other.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\log_{10}\) values are 1, 2, 3, so the points are **equally spaced**, each 1 unit apart. On a
linear axis the same points would be at 10, 100, 1000, and the first two would be visually
indistinguishable.
</details>

**N6.** You have 64 observations and want roughly \(\sqrt{n}\) bins. How many bins, and what
is the width if the range is 0–48?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\sqrt{64}=8\) bins. Width = 48/8 = **6**, so bins are 0–6, 6–12, …, 42–48. Always check
the resulting histogram at a couple of widths before committing.
</details>

**N7.** A pie chart shows a category with 45 % of the total. Compute the sector angle.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Angle = 0.45 × 360° = **162°**. Half the circle (50 %) would be 180°, so the slice is slightly
less than half.
</details>

**N8.** A scatter plot of 5,000 points is drawn in a panel of 2,500 pixels. What is the average
number of points per pixel, and what does that imply?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
5,000/2,500 = **2 points per pixel** on average, with much higher local density where points
cluster. Individual points overlap, so the chart hides where the mass is; use transparency,
hexbinning or a 2-D density instead.
</details>

**N9.** A 3-D bar of height 2 is compared with one of height 4, drawn with proportional depth.
Compare the true ratio with the volume ratio readers perceive.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
True ratio = 4/2 = **2**. If depth and width also double, volume scales as \(2^3=8\), so the
larger bar looks eight times bigger. This is why 3-D bars mislead and should be avoided.
</details>

**N10.** A survey of 250 respondents has 100 "satisfied", 90 "neutral" and 60 "dissatisfied".
Give the percentages and the angle for the satisfied sector.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Satisfied 100/250 = **40 %**, neutral **36 %**, dissatisfied **24 %**. The satisfied angle is
0.40 × 360 = **144°**. Check that the percentages sum to 100 and the angles to 360.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** You must show a hospital board that the 90th-percentile waiting time rose from 38 to 52
minutes while the median stayed near 20. Choose a chart and justify it.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
A line chart of both the median and the 90th percentile over time, with a clear annotation of
the change, or a small-multiple of the waiting-time distribution by month. The median's stability
alongside the tail's growth is the story; a single mean would hide it, and two bars for two
periods would omit the trend.
</details>

**P2.** A marketing team wants a pie chart with eleven slices of campaign spending. What do you
recommend and why?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Replace it with a sorted horizontal bar chart: eleven slices cannot be compared by eye, and
several will be tiny. If part-to-whole matters, add a cumulative percentage or a single
"top five plus other" grouping, and label values directly.
</details>

**P3.** A model's residuals are plotted against fitted values and show a funnel shape. What does
this indicate and what would you do next?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Non-constant variance (heteroscedasticity): the spread of residuals grows with the fitted value.
Consider a log or square-root transformation of the response, weighted least squares, or a model
that allows the variance to depend on the mean; then re-check the plot.
</details>

**P4.** You are asked to visualise the relationship between three variables: age group
(categorical), income (skewed), and churn (binary). Propose a display.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
A small multiple of income distributions by age group (histograms or box plots on a log scale),
with churn rate shown as a second panel or as a colour-coded proportion per group. Avoid
encoding income on a linear axis with three overlapping densities; avoid a 3-D scatter.
</details>

**P5.** A chart's y-axis label reads "value" and there are four unlabelled coloured lines. List
the four fixes that most improve it.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
(1) Put units and a descriptive label on the y-axis. (2) Label each line directly or provide a
clear legend ordered by final value. (3) Start the y-axis at a defensible value and state
whether it is truncated. (4) Add a title that states the takeaway, and reduce the palette to
distinguishable colours.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Explain, using the geometry of perception, why length encodings are more accurate than
area encodings, and derive the distortion when a value is doubled in each case.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Position along a common scale is judged with the smallest error, then length, then angle, then
area and volume (Cleveland and McGill's ordering). For length, doubling the value doubles the
mark, a ratio of 2 that matches the data. For a circle, area \(\propto r^2\), so doubling the
value requires \(r\to r\sqrt2\); readers compare radii rather than areas and perceive a ratio
near \(\sqrt2\approx1.41\), underestimating the true factor of 2.
</details>

**C2.** A histogram of \(n\) observations uses bins of width \(h\). Show that the total area of
the histogram equals \(n h\) when bar heights are counts, and 1 when heights are densities.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
With count heights, area \(=\sum_j (\text{count}_j)\cdot h=n h\). With density heights
\(\text{count}_j/(nh)\), area \(=\sum_j \frac{\text{count}_j}{nh}h=\frac{n}{n}=1\). This is why
density histograms are comparable across samples of different sizes and can be overlaid with a
fitted density.
</details>

**C3.** Show that a log transformation turns equal multiplicative changes into equal additive
changes, and explain the consequence for reading a log-scale chart.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Let \(y_2=ky_1\). Then \(\log y_2-\log y_1=\log k\), independent of \(y_1\). Hence a fixed
vertical distance on a log axis corresponds to a fixed percentage change, not a fixed absolute
change: a distance of \(\log 2\) is always a doubling. Readers must therefore interpret slopes
as growth rates rather than absolute increments.
</details>
