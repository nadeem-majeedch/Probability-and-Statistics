## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The number of ways to arrange 5 distinct books in a row is
A) 25
B) 120
C) 3125
D) 5
* B
Why: \(5!=5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1=120\). \(5^5\) would allow repetition, and 25 confuses permutations with squares.

Q2. The number of ways to choose 3 students from 10 for a committee is
A) 45
B) 90
C) 120
D) 720
* C
Why: \(\binom{10}{3}=120\). The committee is unordered, so \(10\cdot9\cdot8=720\) permutations must be divided by \(3!=6\). 45 is \(\binom{10}{2}\), a miscount of the group size.

Q3. The number of distinguishable arrangements of the letters of STAT is
A) 24
B) 12
C) 6
D) 4
* B
Why: Four letters with T repeated twice give \(4!/2!=12\). Dividing by the repeated letter's permutations removes the arrangements that look identical.

Q4. A password is 4 characters from 26 lowercase letters, repetition allowed. The count is
A) \(\binom{26}{4}\)
B) \(4^{26}\)
C) \(26\cdot25\cdot24\cdot23\)
D) \(26^4\)
* D
Why: Each of the four positions has 26 independent choices, so the multiplication principle gives \(26^4=456{,}976\). Without repetition it would be the falling factorial.

Q5. The number of permutations of \(n\) distinct objects around a circle is
A) \((n-1)!\)
B) \(n!/2\)
C) \(n^{n-1}\)
D) \(n!\)
* A
Why: Fix one object to break the rotational symmetry; the remaining \(n-1\) objects can be arranged in \((n-1)!\) ways. Rotations of a circular arrangement are the same arrangement.

Q6. If a task can be done in \(m\) ways and a second independent task in \(n\) ways, the number
A) \(\binom{m+n}{2}\)
B) \(m+n\)
C) \(mn\)
D) \(m^n\)
* C
Why: The multiplication principle: every choice for the first task can pair with every choice for the second. Addition applies only when the alternatives are mutually exclusive ways of doing one task.

Q7. The coefficient of \(x^3y^2\) in \((x+y)^5\) is
A) 32
B) 10
C) 5
D) 20
* B
Why: The binomial coefficient \(\binom{5}{3}=10\) counts which three of the five factors contribute \(x\). The sum of the coefficients is \(2^5=32\).

Q8. You want the number of 5-card hands from 52 cards. The correct expression is
A) \(\binom{52}{5}\)
B) \(5!\)
C) \(52^5\)
D) \(52!/47!\)
* A
Why: A hand is unordered and without replacement, so it is a combination. The falling factorial would count each hand \(5!\) times, ordering the cards.

Q9. "At least one" probabilities are usually computed as
A) the product of the individual probabilities
B) \(P(\text{one})\times n\)
C) the sum of all terms from one upward
D) \(1-P(\text{none})\)
* D
Why: The complement of "at least one" is "none", which is usually a single product. Summing every term is correct but far more work.

Q10. The number of ways to distribute 8 distinct tasks among 3 distinct people with no
A) \(\binom{8}{3}\)
B) \(3^8\)
C) \(8^3\)
D) \(8!/3!\)
* B
Why: Each of the 8 tasks independently chooses one of 3 people, giving \(3^8=6{,}561\). \(8^3\) would be the count if each of 3 people chose one of 8 tasks.

Q11. The number of distinguishable arrangements of MISSISSIPPI is
A) \(11!/(4!)\)
B) \(11!\)
C) \(11!/(4!4!2!)\)
D) \(\binom{11}{4}\)
* C
Why: Four S, four I and two P are repeated, so divide \(11!\) by each repeated letter's factorial. Forgetting one denominator is the standard error.

Q12. How many subsets does a set of 6 elements have?
A) 6
B) 36
C) 64
D) 720
* C
Why: Each element is either in or out, so \(2^6=64\), including the empty set and the full set. Summing \(\binom{6}{k}\) over all \(k\) gives the same total.

Q13. The number of ways to seat 5 people in a row with two particular people adjacent is
A) 48
B) 24
C) 60
D) 120
* A
Why: Glue the pair together: 4 objects give \(4!=24\) arrangements, and the pair can be ordered in 2 ways, so \(24\times2=48\).

Q14. Sampling without replacement from a small population gives a distribution that is
A) hypergeometric
B) always Poisson
C) uniform
D) always binomial
* A
Why: The hypergeometric distribution counts successes in draws without replacement, where the success probability changes after each draw. The binomial assumes independence, which requires replacement or a very large population.

Q15. If order matters and repetition is allowed, the number of length-\(k\) sequences from
A) \(k^n\)
B) \(\binom{n}{k}\)
C) \(n!/(n-k)!\)
D) \(n^k\)
* D
Why: Each of the \(k\) positions independently takes any of \(n\) symbols. The falling factorial applies when repetition is forbidden.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain the difference between a permutation and a combination, and how the two are
related.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A permutation counts ordered arrangements; a combination counts unordered selections. Every
selection of \(k\) objects can be ordered in \(k!\) ways, so
\(P(n,k)=k!\binom{n}{k}\) and \(\binom{n}{k}=P(n,k)/k!\). The factor \(k!\) is the only
difference.
</details>

**S2.** Why is the multiplication principle not the addition principle?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Multiplication applies when a procedure has successive stages and every combination of choices
occurs — you multiply the counts. Addition applies when a set of outcomes is split into
mutually exclusive cases — you add the counts of the cases. Confusing them is the most common
counting error.
</details>

**S3.** Why must distinguishable permutations divide by factorials of repeated items?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Treating identical items as distinct overcounts each arrangement by the number of ways those
items can be permuted among themselves. Dividing by \(n_1!n_2!\cdots\) removes exactly that
overcount, giving the number of visually different arrangements.
</details>

**S4.** Explain why "at least one" is easier through the complement.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
"At least one" is a union of many disjoint events (exactly one, exactly two, …), each needing
its own count. Its complement, "none", is a single event, usually one product or one
combination, so \(P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})\) is one subtraction.
</details>

**S5.** In the birthday problem, why is the complement approach natural?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The event of interest is "some pair shares a birthday", a union over many pairs with complicated
overlaps. Its complement, "all birthdays differ", is a single falling-factorial count:
\(365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)/365^n\). Subtracting from 1 avoids inclusion–exclusion.
</details>

**S6.** Why does a circular arrangement divide by \(n\) but a necklace (reflection allowed)
divide by \(2n\)?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Rotating a circular arrangement by one place gives the same seating, and there are \(n\)
rotations, so divide by \(n\): \((n-1)!\). If flipping the necklace over also gives the same
object, each arrangement is counted twice more, so divide by \(2n\) (for \(n\ge3\)).
</details>

**S7.** When is the binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{k}\) zero, and why does that make sense
combinatorially?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For \(k<0\) or \(k>n\), since you cannot choose more objects than exist or a negative number of
objects. The factorial formula would be undefined there, so the convention
\(\binom{n}{k}=0\) keeps sums over \(k\) well behaved.
</details>

**S8.** How does sampling with replacement change the probability model compared with sampling
without replacement?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
With replacement, draws are independent and the success probability is constant, giving the
binomial model. Without replacement, the composition of the population changes after each draw,
draws are negatively dependent, and the count follows the hypergeometric distribution, whose
variance is smaller by the finite-population correction.
</details>

**S9.** Explain the pigeonhole principle and give a statistical use.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
If \(n\) items are placed into \(m<n\) boxes, some box holds at least two items. In hashing, it
guarantees collisions once more than \(m\) keys are stored; in data cleaning it explains why
duplicate identifiers must appear once the number of records exceeds the number of possible
identifiers.
</details>

**S10.** Why does counting lie at the foundation of probability?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes,
\(P(A)=|A|/|\Omega|\). Probability therefore reduces to counting favourable and total outcomes,
which is why combinatorial identities are used to derive the binomial, hypergeometric and
multinomial distributions.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** Compute \(\binom{12}{4}\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\binom{12}{4}=\frac{12\cdot11\cdot10\cdot9}{4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1}=\frac{11{,}880}{24}=495\).
Equivalently \(12!/(4!8!)\).
</details>

**N2.** Compute \(P(10,3)\), the number of ordered selections of 3 from 10.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(10,3)=10\cdot9\cdot8=720\). This is \(\binom{10}{3}\times3!=120\times6=720\), confirming
the relation between permutations and combinations.
</details>

**N3.** How many distinguishable arrangements does the word BOOK have?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Four letters with O repeated twice: \(4!/2!=24/2=12\). Listing them confirms that each
arrangement appears twice if the two O's are treated as distinct.
</details>

**N4.** In how many ways can 5 people stand in a queue?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(5!=120\). The first position has 5 choices, the second 4, and so on, giving
\(5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1\).
</details>

**N5.** In how many ways can 5 people sit around a circular table?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\((5-1)!=4!=24\). Fixing one person removes the 5 rotations that produce identical seating
arrangements.
</details>

**N6.** A committee of 3 is chosen from 6 people, and it must include two particular people.
How many committees are possible?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
The two required people are fixed, leaving 1 place to fill from the other 4:
\(\binom{4}{1}=4\). Multiplying instead of choosing here is a common slip.
</details>

**N7.** A 4-character password uses lowercase letters with repetition allowed. How many
passwords exist, and how many if repetition is forbidden?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
With repetition: \(26^4=456{,}976\). Without: \(26\cdot25\cdot24\cdot23=358{,}800\). The
difference is the passwords containing a repeated letter.
</details>

**N8.** In how many ways can 8 distinct tasks be split into groups of 3, 3 and 2?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Multinomial count: \(\frac{8!}{3!\,3!\,2!}=\frac{40{,}320}{6\cdot6\cdot2}=\frac{40{,}320}{72}=560\).
If the two groups of 3 were indistinguishable labels, an extra \(2!\) would be needed; here the
groups are distinct.
</details>

**N9.** Twenty people each shake hands once with every other person. How many handshakes occur?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Each handshake is an unordered pair: \(\binom{20}{2}=190\). Alternatively \(20\times19/2=190\),
dividing by 2 because each handshake is counted from both ends.
</details>

**N10.** A fair die is rolled 4 times. Find the probability of at least one six.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(\text{no six})=(5/6)^4=625/1296=0.4823\), so
\(P(\text{at least one})=1-0.4823=0.5177\). The complement is far simpler than summing the
probabilities of one, two, three and four sixes.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A team must choose 4 analysts from 9 for a project, and then assign them to 4 distinct
roles. How many assignments are possible, and why are two different counts involved?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Choose the team: \(\binom{9}{4}=126\); assign roles: \(4!=24\). Total \(126\times24=3{,}024\).
The selection is unordered, the assignment is ordered, so the multiplication principle combines
one combination with one permutation. Equivalently \(P(9,4)=3{,}024\).
</details>

**P2.** A website tests 3 versions of a headline across 4 audience segments. Each visitor sees
exactly one combination. How many cells does the experiment have, and what is the risk if some
cells receive very few visitors?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(3\times4=12\) cells. With a fixed total sample, splitting into 12 cells shrinks each cell's
sample, widening intervals and reducing power; sparse cells also invite unstable rates. Either
increase the sample or reduce the design to fewer combinations.
</details>

**P3.** A quality inspector draws 5 items without replacement from a lot of 20 containing 3
defectives. Explain why the binomial is inappropriate and name the correct model.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Draws are not independent: after a defective is removed, the remaining proportion changes, so
the binomial's constant \(p\) is violated. The count of defectives follows the hypergeometric
distribution with parameters \(N=20\), \(K=3\), \(n=5\). The binomial becomes acceptable only
if the lot is very large relative to the sample.
</details>

**P4.** A data scientist generates 8-character identifiers from 36 symbols. How many identifiers
are available, and at what point does the birthday effect make collisions likely?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(36^8\approx2.82\times10^{12}\) identifiers. Collisions become likely once the number issued is
on the order of \(\sqrt{2\cdot36^8\ln2}\approx2\times10^6\) — the birthday bound, far below the
total. Use a longer identifier or check for duplicates on insert.
</details>

**P5.** An exam has 10 true/false questions. A student guesses all of them. How many answer
patterns are possible, and what is the probability of scoring exactly 5?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(2^{10}=1{,}024\) patterns. Exactly 5 correct:
\(\binom{10}{5}/2^{10}=252/1{,}024=0.246\). About one guesser in four scores exactly half,
which is why a pass mark well above 50 % is needed to distinguish knowledge from luck.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Prove the identity \(\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}\) combinatorially and
algebraically.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Combinatorial: fix one element. A \(k\)-subset either contains it — then choose the other
\(k-1\) from \(n-1\), giving \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\) — or does not, giving \(\binom{n-1}{k}\).
These cases are disjoint and exhaustive. Algebraic:
\(\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}+\frac{(n-1)!}{k!(n-k-1)!}
=\frac{(n-1)!(k+n-k)}{k!(n-k)!}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\).
</details>

**C2.** Derive the probability that at least two of \(n\) people share a birthday, and evaluate
it for \(n=23\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Assuming independence and 365 equally likely days,
\(P(\text{all distinct})=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{365-i}{365}\). Hence
\(P(\text{share})=1-\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{365}\right)\). For \(n=23\) the product is
about 0.4927, so the probability is about \(0.5073\) — just over half, despite 23 being far
below 365.
</details>

**C3.** Show that the number of ways to arrange \(n\) objects of which \(n_1,\dots,n_r\) are
identical is \(n!/(n_1!\cdots n_r!)\), and use it to count the arrangements of the letters of
BANANA.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Start with \(n!\) arrangements of distinct objects. For each pattern, the \(n_i\) identical
copies can be permuted among themselves in \(n_i!\) ways without changing the pattern, so each
pattern is counted \(\prod n_i!\) times. Dividing gives the formula. BANANA has 6 letters with
A×3, N×2, B×1: \(6!/(3!2!1!)=720/12=60\).
</details>
