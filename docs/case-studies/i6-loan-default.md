## A. Problem statement

A retail bank has 800 historical loan applications with a binary default outcome. The
credit risk team must build a simple scorecard: which applicant characteristics predict
default, how large the effects are, how well the model separates defaulters from
non-defaulters, and what approval threshold balances missed defaults against rejected good
customers.

## B. Background and context

Credit scoring is the canonical applied classification problem: an imbalanced binary
outcome, a handful of interpretable predictors, and a decision that costs money in both
directions. Regulators require explanations, so logistic regression — not a black box —
remains the industry standard. This case covers the full workflow: exploratory comparison
of defaulters and non-defaulters, the logit model with odds ratios, discrimination measured
by AUC, and the threshold trade-off expressed as a confusion matrix.

## C. Dataset description

`loans.csv` has 800 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `application_id` | integer | 1–800 |
| `credit_score` | integer | 300–850 |
| `annual_income` | integer | Annual income |
| `loan_amount` | integer | Requested amount |
| `employment_years` | integer | 0–24 |
| `default` | integer | 1 = defaulted, 0 = repaid |

**Generation method.** Credit score is normal(640, 75) clipped to 300–850; income is
lognormal with median 55,000; loan amount is lognormal with median 180,000; employment
years are uniform 0–24. The default log-odds are
\(-1.2-0.006(\text{score}-640)+0.9\ln(\text{amount}/\text{income})-0.04\cdot\text{employment}\),
so default falls with score and employment and rises with the loan-to-income ratio. The
realised default rate is 36.6 % (293 of 800) — deliberately high so that the case has
enough defaults to analyse.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What is the default rate, and how do defaulters differ from repayers?
2. Is credit score associated with default? How strong is the effect?
3. Does the loan-to-income ratio add information beyond credit score?
4. What are the odds ratios for a 10-point score increase and a 0.1 increase in LTI?
5. How well does the model discriminate (AUC)?
6. What happens to sensitivity and specificity as the approval threshold moves?
7. Which threshold would you recommend, and why?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Binary outcomes, odds and probabilities.
- Logistic regression, log-odds, odds ratios.
- Confidence intervals for coefficients; Wald and likelihood-ratio tests.
- Confounding and incremental validity.
- Discrimination (AUC, ROC) versus calibration.
- Confusion matrices, sensitivity, specificity, precision.
- Decision thresholds and expected cost.

## F. Mathematical formulation

\[
\mathrm{logit}\,P(\text{default})=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\text{score}
+\beta_2\,\text{LTI}+\beta_3\,\text{employment},
\]

where \(\mathrm{LTI}=\text{loan amount}/\text{annual income}\). The odds ratio for a
\(k\)-unit change is \(e^{k\beta}\), so a 10-point score increase multiplies the odds by
\(e^{10\beta_1}\).

The ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity as the threshold varies, and the
area under it is

\[
\mathrm{AUC}=P(\hat p_{\text{default}}>\hat p_{\text{repaid}})
\]

for a randomly chosen pair — the probability that the model ranks a defaulter above a
repayer. AUC = 0.5 is no discrimination, 1.0 is perfect.

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Compute the default rate; compare predictor means by outcome.
2. Check the distribution of LTI and consider a log transform of income and amount.
3. Fit a score-only model, then add LTI and employment.
4. Report odds ratios with 95 % intervals; test incremental contribution.
5. Compute predicted probabilities and AUC.
6. Build confusion matrices at thresholds 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5.
7. Express the trade-off in business terms (defaults caught versus good loans rejected).
8. Recommend a threshold with an explicit cost assumption.

## H. Student tasks

1. Report the default rate and the mean credit score of defaulters and repayers.
2. Fit default ~ credit_score. Give the odds ratio for 10 points and its interval.
3. Add LTI and employment. Which coefficient is largest in odds terms?
4. Compute AUC for the full model. Interpret it in one sentence.
5. At threshold 0.3, fill in the confusion matrix and compute sensitivity and specificity.
6. If a default costs 10 times a rejected good loan, which threshold is best?
7. Write the credit policy in three sentences.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

loans = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/loans.csv")
loans["lti"] = loans.loan_amount / loans.annual_income
print(loans.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print(f"applications: {len(loans)}, defaults: {int(loans.default.sum())} "
      f"({loans.default.mean():.1%})")

print("\nmeans by outcome:")
print(loans.groupby("default")[["credit_score", "annual_income", "loan_amount",
                                "employment_years", "lti"]].mean().round(2).to_string())

score_only = smf.logit("default ~ credit_score", data=loans).fit(disp=0)
print(f"\nscore only: beta = {score_only.params.credit_score:.5f}, "
      f"OR per 10 points = {np.exp(score_only.params.credit_score * 10):.3f}")

full = smf.logit("default ~ credit_score + lti + employment_years", data=loans).fit(disp=0)
summary = pd.DataFrame({
    "coef": full.params,
    "OR": np.exp(full.params),
    "OR_low": np.exp(full.conf_int()[0]),
    "OR_high": np.exp(full.conf_int()[1]),
    "p": full.pvalues,
}).round(4)
print("\nfull model:")
print(summary.to_string())
print(f"pseudo R2 = {full.prsquared:.3f}, AIC = {full.aic:.1f}")

pred = full.predict(loans)
print(f"\nAUC = {roc_auc_score(loans.default, pred):.3f}")

for threshold in (0.2, 0.3, 0.5):
    flagged = pred > threshold
    tp = int((flagged & (loans.default == 1)).sum())
    fp = int((flagged & (loans.default == 0)).sum())
    fn = int((~flagged & (loans.default == 1)).sum())
    tn = int((~flagged & (loans.default == 0)).sum())
    print(f"\nthreshold {threshold:.1f}: approved {int((~flagged).sum())}, "
          f"declined {int(flagged.sum())}")
    print(f"  TP={tp} FP={fp} FN={fn} TN={tn}")
    print(f"  sensitivity={tp / (tp + fn):.3f}  specificity={tn / (tn + fp):.3f}  "
          f"precision={tp / (tp + fp):.3f}")
```

**How to read it.** The two predicted-probability distributions overlap a lot: the model
separates the middle of each distribution but cannot cleanly split low-risk from high-risk
applicants. The ROC curve rises above the diagonal but stays well below the top-left
corner, which is the visual meaning of AUC ≈ 0.70. The dashed threshold at 0.3 sits in the
region where many repaid applicants are also flagged.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Default rate 36.6 % (293 of 800).
- Defaulters have lower credit scores (620 vs 649), lower income (57,061 vs 65,117),
  larger loans (256,609 vs 192,668) and slightly shorter employment (11 vs 13 years).
- Credit score alone: coefficient −0.0062, so a 10-point increase multiplies the odds of
  default by about 0.94 — a 6 % reduction per 10 points, or 46 % per 100 points.
- In the full model, LTI has OR 1.166 per unit, employment OR 0.942 per year, and score OR
  0.994 per point (all p < 0.001).
- AUC = 0.702: the model ranks a random defaulter above a random repayer about 70 % of the
  time — useful, not decisive.
- Threshold 0.3 catches 77.5 % of defaults while declining 52 % of good applicants;
  threshold 0.5 catches 34.5 % and declines 12 %.
- With defaults costing ten times a rejected good loan, the optimal threshold is
  low — around 0.2–0.25 — where 93 % of defaults are caught.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — group comparison.** Defaulters and repayers differ in every predictor, in the
directions the generator built in. The largest relative gap is in the loan amount
(256,609 vs 192,668, a third larger), which is why the loan-to-income ratio is the strongest
single additional predictor.

**Step 2 — score alone.** The coefficient on credit score is −0.0062, so each 10 points
lowers the odds of default by a factor of \(e^{-0.062} = 0.940\) — a 6 % reduction. Over the
realistic range of scores (500 to 750) the odds change by a factor of
\(e^{-0.0062 \times 250} = 0.21\), i.e. an applicant at 750 has about a fifth the odds of one
at 500. This is a strong effect in odds terms and still leaves plenty of overlap.

**Step 3 — the full model.** LTI is the strongest driver per unit: OR 1.166, so a rise from
2 to 3 in loan-to-income (a realistic move for a large loan on a modest income) multiplies
the odds by 1.17. Employment adds a modest protective effect (OR 0.942 per year, so ten
years ≈ 0.55). Score remains significant but its OR is close to 1 *per point*, which is
why quoting it per 10 or 100 points is clearer.

**Step 4 — discrimination.** AUC = 0.702 with 95 % bootstrap interval roughly 0.66–0.75.
That is respectable for a three-variable model on noisy data and far from the 0.85+ that a
production scorecard with 30 variables might reach. AUC is threshold-free: it says the
ranking is informative, not that any particular cut-off is right.

**Step 5 — thresholds.** At 0.2: sensitivity 0.928, specificity 0.223, precision 0.408 —
the bank declines 666 applicants (83 % of the book) to catch 272 of 293 defaults. At 0.3:
sensitivity 0.775, specificity 0.483, precision 0.464. At 0.5: sensitivity 0.345,
specificity 0.884, precision 0.631. The trade-off is stark: catching more defaults means
rejecting many good customers, because the model's overlap is large.

**Step 6 — cost-based choice.** If a default costs 10,000 and a rejected good loan costs
1,000 in lost margin, the expected cost of a threshold is
\(10{,}000\cdot FN+1{,}000\cdot FP\). At 0.2: 21 × 10,000 + 394 × 1,000 = 604,000. At 0.3:
66 × 10,000 + 262 × 1,000 = 922,000. At 0.5: 192 × 10,000 + 59 × 1,000 = 1,979,000. The low
threshold wins decisively, which is the general rule when the cost ratio is 10:1. This
calculation — not the AUC — is what a credit committee should see.

**Step 7 — calibration and fairness.** A model can discriminate well and still be
miscalibrated: predicted 20 % might mean 30 % in reality. Before using probabilities to
price loans, check a reliability plot (case study A5 shows how). Fairness matters too:
if credit score correlates with protected characteristics, the model may produce disparate
impact even without using those characteristics directly; regulators require disparate-impact
testing, which is outside the scope of this case but must be named.

**Limitations.** The data are cross-sectional and synthetic; there is no time dimension, so
the model cannot capture changes in the economy. The sample is small for a scorecard (800
applications, 293 defaults), and the AUC interval reflects that. Reject inference — the
fact that only approved applicants have observed outcomes — is a real problem in credit
scoring and is ignored here.
