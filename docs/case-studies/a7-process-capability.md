## A. Problem statement

A precision workshop machines shafts to 25.00 ± 0.15 mm across 30 batches of ten units,
producing on two machines. Quality engineering must decide whether the process is stable,
whether it is capable, how large a subgroup is needed to detect a 0.05 mm shift, and whether
the current inspection regime gives any real protection. The manager is reassured by a
control chart that shows no violations; the engineer suspects the chart is simply blind.

## B. Background and context

Statistical process control separates two questions that are often confused: *is the process
stable?* (control charts) and *is it good enough?* (capability indices). A stable process can
be hopelessly incapable, and a capable process can be unstable. This case computes both,
shows that an \(\bar X\) chart with ten units per subgroup cannot see a 0.05 mm shift, and
derives the subgroup size that would. It is the natural sequel to the easy case study E5,
which stopped at Cp and Cpk.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. Is the process in statistical control? Build an \(\bar X\) chart from the first 20 batches.
2. Do any later batches exceed the control limits? What does that tell you?
3. What subgroup size would detect a 0.05 mm shift with reasonable probability?
4. Compute Cp and Cpk per machine. Which is capable?
5. How many units per thousand are out of specification on each machine?
6. Is the normality assumption behind these indices satisfied?
7. What should the workshop do, in order?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Rational subgroups and the sampling distribution of the mean.
- \(\bar X\) control charts: centre line, 3-sigma limits, run rules.
- Average run length and the probability of detecting a shift.
- Process capability indices Cp, Cpk, and their normality assumption.
- Expected nonconforming fraction from a normal model.
- Power and subgroup size for detecting a mean shift.
- Distinguishing stability from capability.

## F. Mathematical formulation

For subgroups of size \(n\) with process standard deviation \(\sigma\):

\[
UCL_{\bar X}=\mu+3\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\qquad
LCL_{\bar X}=\mu-3\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.
\]

The probability that a single subgroup mean falls outside the limits when the true mean has
shifted by \(\delta\) is

\[
\beta_{\text{detect}}=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{UCL-\mu-\delta}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\right)
+\Phi\!\left(\frac{LCL-\mu-\delta}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\right).
\]

Capability:

\[
C_p=\frac{USL-LSL}{6\sigma},\qquad
C_{pk}=\min\!\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3\sigma},\frac{\mu-LSL}{3\sigma}\right).
\]

The required subgroup size to detect shift \(\delta\) with 50 % probability at the first
subgroup is approximately \(n\approx(3\sigma/\delta)^2\).

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Compute batch means and the within-batch standard deviation.
2. Build the \(\bar X\) chart using batches 1–20 as the baseline.
3. Check later batches against the limits and against run rules.
4. Compute the detection probability for a 0.05 mm shift at \(n=10\).
5. Derive the \(n\) needed for a 50 % and 90 % detection probability.
6. Compute Cp and Cpk per machine; verify normality with Shapiro-Wilk and a Q-Q plot.
7. Estimate the out-of-specification rate per machine.
8. Recommend actions in priority order.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

LSL, USL = 24.85, 25.15
parts = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/shaft_diameters.csv")
batch_means = parts.groupby("batch").diameter_mm.mean()
print(parts.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print(f"batches: {batch_means.size}, units per batch: {parts.groupby('batch').size().unique()}")

baseline = parts.loc[parts.batch <= 20, "diameter_mm"]
mu, sigma = baseline.mean(), baseline.std(ddof=1)
n = 10
ucl, lcl = mu + 3 * sigma / np.sqrt(n), mu - 3 * sigma / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"\nbaseline: mean={mu:.3f}, sd={sigma:.3f}")
print(f"Xbar limits: {lcl:.3f} to {ucl:.3f}")
outside = [int(b) for b, value in batch_means.items() if value > ucl or value < lcl]
print("batches outside the limits:", outside or "none")
print(f"batch means range {batch_means.min():.3f} to {batch_means.max():.3f}, "
      f"SD of batch means {batch_means.std(ddof=1):.4f} "
      f"(theory: {sigma / np.sqrt(n):.4f})")

delta = 0.05
z = delta / (sigma / np.sqrt(n))
power_one = 1 - stats.norm.cdf(3 - z) + stats.norm.cdf(-3 - z)
print(f"\nprobability a single subgroup signals a {delta} mm shift at n=10: {power_one:.3f}")
print(f"  n for 50% detection: {((3 + 0) * sigma / delta) ** 2:.0f} per subgroup")
for target in (0.8, 0.9):
    n_req = ((stats.norm.ppf(target) + 3) * sigma / delta) ** 2
    print(f"  n for {target:.0%} detection: {n_req:.0f} per subgroup")

print("\ncapability by machine:")
for machine, group in parts.groupby("machine"):
    values = group.diameter_mm
    m, s = values.mean(), values.std(ddof=1)
    cp, cpk = (USL - LSL) / (6 * s), min(USL - m, m - LSL) / (3 * s)
    out_rate = stats.norm.cdf(LSL, m, s) + 1 - stats.norm.cdf(USL, m, s)
    observed = ((values < LSL) | (values > USL)).mean()
    w = stats.shapiro(values)
    print(f"  {machine}: mean={m:.3f} sd={s:.3f} Cp={cp:.2f} Cpk={cpk:.2f} "
          f"expected={out_rate * 1e6:.0f} ppm, observed={observed * 1e6:.0f} ppm, "
          f"Shapiro p={w.pvalue:.3f}")
```

**How to read it.** Every batch mean lies inside 24.952–25.083, so the chart declares the
process stable. But the limits are ±0.066 mm wide — wider than the 0.05 mm offset between
machines — so the chart could not possibly detect the difference that is actually there. The

visible mass beyond 25.15.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Batch means range 24.971–25.066, with within-batch SD 0.069; theory predicts a batch-mean
  SD of 0.022, and the observed value matches.
- Control limits from batches 1–20 are 24.952 and 25.083; no batch breaches them, and the
  pre/post-batch-20 means are identical (25.017). The process is stable.
- The chart cannot detect a 0.05 mm shift: with \(n=10\) the probability is 0.24 for a single
  subgroup, because 0.05 mm is only 2.3 standard errors of a batch mean.
- Detecting that shift 90 % of the time requires about 35 units per subgroup; 50 % requires
  about 17.
- M1 is centred (24.998) with Cp 0.83, Cpk 0.82; M2 is offset (25.048) with Cp 0.66 and
  Cpk 0.45.
- Expected nonconforming: about 13,200 ppm for M1 and 92,500 ppm for M2; the observed 0.55 %
  (1 of 183) and 5.98 % (7 of 117) are consistent within sampling error for these counts.
- Shapiro-Wilk gives p = 0.968 (M1) and p = 0.070 (M2), so normality is acceptable and the
  capability arithmetic is credible.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — stability.** The \(\bar X\) chart built from batches 1–20 has centre 25.017 and
limits 24.952–25.083. No later batch violates them, and the mean of batches 21–30 equals the
baseline mean to three decimals. By the standard 3-sigma rule the process is in control. This
is a genuine finding, not a failure: the process is not drifting within the resolution the
chart can see.

**Step 2 — the blindness of the chart.** The standard error of a batch mean is
\(0.069/\sqrt{10}=0.0218\), so the 3-sigma limits span ±0.066. A 0.05 mm offset is 2.3 standard
errors, giving a first-subgroup detection probability of 0.24. Across ten consecutive
subgroups the chance of at least one signal rises to \(1-0.76^{10}\approx0.94\) — which is why
run rules matter — but a single chart with 30 points is a weak instrument for this shift.

**Step 3 — the subgroup size.** To detect 0.05 mm with 90 % probability at the first subgroup,
\(n\approx((1.28+3)\times0.069/0.05)^2\approx35\) units. For 50 %, \(n\approx(3\times0.069/
0.05)^2\approx17\). The workshop inspects 10 per batch, so it must aggregate at least two
batches (20 units) to have an even chance of seeing the machine difference, and four batches
to see it reliably.

**Step 4 — capability.** M1: Cp = 0.30/(6×0.060) = 0.83, Cpk = 0.82 — centred but not
precise. M2: Cp = 0.66, Cpk = 0.45 — both offset and imprecise. Neither meets the
conventional 1.33 threshold. Note that Cpk = 0.45 for M2 means the nearer specification limit
is only 1.35 standard deviations away, so a substantial fraction of output is out of
tolerance.

**Step 5 — ppm arithmetic.** For M2, \(P(X>25.15)=\Phi((25.048-25.15)/0.076)=\Phi(-1.34)
=0.090\), plus about 0.005 below the lower limit, giving roughly 9.5 %; the observed 5.98 %
(7 of 117) is lower, which is within the range of counts expected from 117 units. For M1 the
model predicts about 1.3 %, and one unit in 183 (0.55 %) was observed — again compatible with
a handful of events. With samples this small the ppm figures should be read as orders of
magnitude, not as precise rates.

**Step 6 — normality.** Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.968 for M1 and 0.070 for M2: no rejection at 5 %.
The Q-Q plot is close to linear. If the process were strongly skewed, Cp and Cpk would
misstate the ppm rate and a transformation or a non-normal capability method would be needed.

**Step 7 — the plan, in order.** (i) Recentre M2: a −0.048 mm set-point adjustment raises
Cpk from 0.45 to about 0.66 immediately at essentially no cost. (ii) Reduce variation: a
target SD of 0.030 mm would give Cp ≈ 1.67 on M1, achieved through tool-wear monitoring and
temperature control. (iii) Change the monitoring: aggregate two batches per subgroup (n = 20)
and add a run rule of seven points on one side of the centre line, which detects a 0.02 mm
shift within a few subgroups. (iv) Until (i)–(ii) are done, 100 % gauge inspection of M2
output, because 6 % escapes are more expensive than inspection.

**Stability versus capability, in one sentence.** The process is stable and incapable: it
reliably produces the wrong distribution, and no amount of chart-watching will fix that —
only a change to the process will.
