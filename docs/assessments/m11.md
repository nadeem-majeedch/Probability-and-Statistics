## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The marginal distribution of \(X\) is obtained from the joint distribution by
A) multiplying by \(Y\)
B) differentiating with respect to \(X\)
C) summing or integrating over \(Y\)
D) conditioning on \(Y\)
* C
Why: The marginal averages over all values of the other variable, which is summing a joint pmf or integrating a joint density. Conditioning would divide by the marginal instead.

Q2. \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if and only if
A) \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0\)
B) \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) for all \(x,y\)
C) \(E[XY]=0\)
D) they have zero correlation
* B
Why: Independence requires the joint to factorise everywhere. Zero covariance is implied by independence but does not imply it, except in the jointly normal case.

Q3. The conditional density \(f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\) equals
A) \(f_X(x)/f_{X,Y}(x,y)\)
B) \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)/f_X(x)\)
C) \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)/f_Y(y)\)
D) \(f_X(x)f_Y(y)\)
* B
Why: Conditioning fixes \(x\) and rescales the slice of the joint density by the marginal of \(X\) so it integrates to 1. Dividing by the wrong marginal is the standard error.

Q4. \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0\) implies independence when
A) both variables are uniform
B) always
C) never
D) \((X,Y)\) are jointly normal
* D
Why: For the bivariate normal, zero covariance makes the joint density factorise, so it implies independence. In general, zero covariance only rules out linear association.

Q5. The correlation coefficient always lies in
A) \([0,\infty)\)
B) \([0,1]\)
C) \([-1,1]\)
D) \((-\infty,\infty)\)
* C
Why: Correlation is a normalised covariance, bounded by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Values near zero indicate no linear association, not no association.

Q6. \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)\) equals
A) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)\)
B) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\)
C) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\mathrm{Var}(Y)\)
D) \(\mathrm{Var}(X)-\mathrm{Var}(Y)\)
* B
Why: Expanding \((X+Y)\) around its mean produces a cross term that averages to twice the covariance. The simple sum holds only when the covariance is zero.

Q7. The law of total expectation states
A) \(E[X]=E[E[X\mid Y]]\)
B) \(E[X\mid Y]=E[X]\)
C) \(E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]\)
D) \(E[X]=E[Y]\)
* A
Why: Averaging the conditional means over the distribution of \(Y\) returns the unconditional mean. It is the basis of simulation and of variance decomposition.

Q8. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(E[XY]\) equals
A) \(E[X]E[Y]\)
B) \(E[X]+E[Y]\)
C) 0
D) \(E[X]^2\)
* A
Why: Independence factorises the expectation of a product. The converse is false: \(E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]\) only implies zero covariance.

Q9. Correlation is unchanged by
A) any non-linear transformation
B) squaring one variable
C) conditioning on a third variable
D) adding a constant to \(X\) and multiplying \(Y\) by a positive constant
* D
Why: Linear transformations with positive scale factors cancel in the numerator and denominator; non-linear transformations generally change the correlation, sometimes to zero.

Q10. Covariance has units of
A) no units
B) squared units of \(X\)
C) \(X\) only
D) the product of the units of \(X\) and \(Y\)
* D
Why: It averages products of deviations, so the units multiply. Correlation divides by both SDs and is therefore unit-free.

Q11. Simpson's paradox illustrates that
A) marginal and conditional associations can have opposite signs
B) correlations are always positive
C) independence is transitive
D) covariance equals causation
* A
Why: Aggregating over a grouping variable can reverse an association present within every group. It is a reason to examine conditional relationships before drawing conclusions.

Q12. In a bivariate normal distribution, the conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X=x\) is
A) normal with mean \(\mu_Y+\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X)\)
B) uniform
C) exponential
D) independent of \(x\)
* A
Why: The conditional mean is linear in \(x\) with slope \(\rho\sigma_Y/\sigma_X\), and the conditional variance \(\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)\) does not depend on \(x\). This is the basis of regression.

Q13. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent with variances 4 and 9, \(\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)\) is
A) −5
B) 5
C) 13
D) 1
* C
Why: \(\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)-2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=4+9=13\) when independent. Variances add even when the variables are subtracted.

Q14. The joint pmf of two variables must satisfy
A) \(p(x,y)\ge0\) and \(\sum_x\sum_y p(x,y)=1\)
B) \(p(x,y)\le1\) only
C) \(\sum_x p(x,y)=1\) for every \(y\)
D) \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\)
* A
Why: Joint probabilities are non-negative and total 1 over the grid. Row or column sums give marginals and generally do not equal 1 individually.

Q15. A correlation of 0.8 between study time and marks means
A) 80 % of the variation in marks is explained
B) a strong positive linear association
C) studying causes higher marks
D) marks increase by 0.8 for each extra hour
* B
Why: Correlation measures the strength of linear association; \(r^2=0.64\) is the proportion of variance explained, causation is not implied, and the slope depends on the scales.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain the difference between a joint, a marginal and a conditional distribution.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The joint describes both variables together, \(P(X=x,Y=y)\) or \(f(x,y)\). A marginal describes
one variable alone, obtained by averaging over the other. A conditional describes one variable
when the other is fixed, obtained by renormalising the joint slice. Each answers a different
question.
</details>

**S2.** Why does zero correlation not imply independence? Give an example.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Correlation measures only linear association. Let \(X\sim U(-1,1)\) and \(Y=X^2\): they are
strongly dependent, yet \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[X^3]-E[X]E[X^2]=0\). A scatter plot reveals the
dependence that the correlation misses.
</details>

**S3.** What does the conditional expectation \(E[Y\mid X]\) represent, and how is it used in
regression?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
It is the mean of \(Y\) among units with a given \(X\), a function of \(X\). Regression estimates
this function; for jointly normal variables it is linear,
\(\mu_Y+\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X)\), and the residual variance is
\(\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)\).
</details>

**S4.** Explain the law of total variance in words.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
\(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=E[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)]+\mathrm{Var}(E[Y\mid X])\): total variability is the
average within-group variability plus the variability of group means. It explains why grouping
can either increase or decrease explained variance and underlies ANOVA.
</details>

**S5.** Why is the covariance matrix required to be positive semi-definite?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For any vector \(a\), \(\mathrm{Var}(a^\top X)=a^\top\Sigma a\ge0\). Negative values would imply
a negative variance, so \(\Sigma\) must be positive semi-definite; this constraint also guarantees
a valid multivariate normal density.
</details>

**S6.** What does it mean for two variables to be independent conditional on a third, and why
does it matter?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Within each level of the third variable, the two are independent. It matters because marginal
dependence can be induced by a common cause (or by conditioning on a common effect), so
conditional independence is the assumption behind many graphical models and causal claims.
</details>

**S7.** How would you estimate a joint distribution from data with 10,000 observations and two
continuous variables?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Parametrically, fit a bivariate normal or copula and check; nonparametrically, use a 2-D
histogram, kernel density estimate, or hexbin plot. Report marginals and correlation as
summaries, and inspect for non-linearity, clusters and outliers before choosing a model.
</details>

**S8.** Why can conditioning on a variable create a spurious association?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Collider bias: if two independent causes both influence a variable that you condition on,
within levels of it the causes become negatively associated. For example, conditioning on
admission to a selective programme can make ability and effort look negatively related.
</details>

**S9.** Give a Data Science example where joint modelling beats separate univariate models.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Modelling page views and purchases jointly: purchase probability depends on traffic, and ignoring
the dependence misstates uncertainty about revenue. A bivariate or hierarchical model captures
the shared variation and produces coherent predictions for derived quantities.
</details>

**S10.** Why is correlation invariant to changes of units but not to changes of the
experimental range?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Units enter as positive scale factors that cancel in the normalised covariance. Restricting the
range of \(X\) attenuates the correlation because the remaining variation is smaller relative to
noise — a selection effect, not a change of units.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** The joint pmf is \(P(0,0)=0.1\), \(P(0,1)=0.2\), \(P(1,0)=0.3\), \(P(1,1)=0.4\).
Find the marginals of \(X\) and \(Y\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(X=0)=0.1+0.2=0.3\); \(P(X=1)=0.3+0.4=0.7\). \(P(Y=0)=0.1+0.3=0.4\);
\(P(Y=1)=0.2+0.4=0.6\). Each set sums to 1.
</details>

**N2.** Using N1, compute \(E[X]\), \(E[Y]\) and \(E[XY]\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(E[X]=0(0.3)+1(0.7)=0.7\); \(E[Y]=0(0.4)+1(0.6)=0.6\). \(XY\) is 1 only when both are
1, so \(E[XY]=0.4\). Then \(\mathrm{Cov}=0.4-0.42=-0.02\): slight negative dependence.
</details>

**N3.** Using N1, compute \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(E[X^2]=0.7\), so \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=0.7-0.49=0.21\). \(E[Y^2]=0.6\), so
\(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=0.6-0.36=0.24\).
</details>

**N4.** Using N2–N3, compute the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\rho=-0.02/\sqrt{0.21\times0.24}=-0.02/\sqrt{0.0504}=-0.02/0.2245=-0.089\). A weak
negative linear association; the variables are not independent, since the joint is not the
product of the marginals.
</details>

**N5.** \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=4\), \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=9\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=3\). Compute
\(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)\) and the correlation.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=4+9+2(3)=19\). \(\rho=3/(2\times3)=0.5\). The positive covariance
inflates the variance of the sum beyond 13.
</details>

**N6.** Using N5, compute \(\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)=4+9-2(3)=7\). Positive dependence makes the difference less variable than
either variable, which is why pairing reduces variance.
</details>

**N7.** \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent with \(E[X]=2\), \(E[Y]=5\). Compute \(E[3XY-2X]\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Independence gives \(E[XY]=10\) and linearity gives \(3(10)-2(2)=30-4=26\). Note that
\(E[3XY-2X]\ne3E[X]E[Y]-2\) without distributing correctly.
</details>

**N8.** For the bivariate normal with \(\mu_X=10\), \(\mu_Y=20\), \(\sigma_X=2\), \(\sigma_Y=5\),
\(\rho=0.6\), compute \(E[Y\mid X=12]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Conditional mean \(=20+0.6(5/2)(12-10)=20+3=23\). Conditional variance
\(=25(1-0.36)=16\), conditional SD 4. Note the conditional variance does not depend on \(x\).
</details>

**N9.** A joint density on \(0<x<1\), \(0<y<1\) is \(f(x,y)=c(x+y)\). Find \(c\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\int_0^1\int_0^1 c(x+y)dy\,dx=c\int_0^1(x+1/2)dx=c(1/2+1/2)=c\), so \(c=1\). Check with
the density: \(\int_0^1\int_0^1(x+y)=1\).
</details>

**N10.** Using N9, compute the marginal density of \(X\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(f_X(x)=\int_0^1(x+y)dy=x+1/2\) for \(0<x<1\). It integrates to \(1/2+1/2=1\). The joint does
not factorise as \((x+1/2)(y+1/2)\), so \(X\) and \(Y\) are dependent.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** Ad spend and sales have correlation 0.85 across 40 regions. A manager concludes that
doubling spend doubles sales. Identify three problems.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
(1) Correlation is not causation: larger regions spend more and sell more. (2) A correlation of
0.85 says nothing about the slope; doubling spend never doubles sales unless the relationship
passes through the origin with slope 1. (3) With \(n=40\), the correlation has uncertainty
(roughly ±0.13), and the relationship may be non-linear or confounded.
</details>

**P2.** Two features have correlation 0.98. What are the consequences for regression, and what
would you do?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Near-collinearity inflates coefficient standard errors and makes signs unstable, though
predictions may be fine. Diagnose with VIF, drop or combine one feature, use regularisation
(ridge), or interpret the pair jointly rather than individually.
</details>

**P3.** A model predicts click-through and conversion separately, but the residuals are
correlated. How would a joint model change the uncertainty of predicted revenue?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
With correlated residuals, the variance of revenue (a product) includes a covariance term that
separate models set to zero, so the interval is too narrow. Model the two outcomes jointly (for
example with a copula or multivariate model) and propagate the dependence by simulation.
</details>

**P4.** In a study of hospitals, mortality is lower at high-volume hospitals overall, but within
each severity band low-volume hospitals do better. Explain.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Simpson's paradox: high-volume hospitals treat sicker patients, so their crude mortality is
higher despite better outcomes within severity. The aggregate comparison is confounded by case
mix; condition on severity or standardise before comparing.
</details>

**P5.** You have 10,000 records of (age, income) and must estimate the probability of a purchase
given both. Outline two approaches and their trade-offs.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Parametric: logistic regression with age and log-income, interpretable and efficient but assumes
a functional form. Nonparametric: a 2-D density or tree ensemble, flexible but data-hungry and
less interpretable. Validate by calibration and out-of-sample log-loss; check for non-linearity
and interactions.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Derive \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\) and
explain the sign of the covariance term.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Let \(a=X-E[X]\), \(b=Y-E[Y]\). Then
\(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=E[(a+b)^2]=E[a^2]+E[b^2]+2E[ab]\), which is
\(\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\). A positive covariance means the
deviations tend to share sign, so they reinforce; a negative one means they offset.
</details>

**C2.** Prove the law of total variance.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \(Y-\mu_Y=(Y-E[Y\mid X])+(E[Y\mid X]-\mu_Y)\). The two terms are uncorrelated because the
first has conditional mean zero given \(X\). Squaring and taking expectations gives
\(E[(Y-E[Y\mid X])^2]+E[(E[Y\mid X]-\mu_Y)^2]\), i.e.
\(E[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)]+\mathrm{Var}(E[Y\mid X])\).
</details>

**C3.** For jointly normal \(X,Y\) with correlation \(\rho\), show that the conditional variance
of \(Y\) given \(X\) is \(\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)\) and interpret the factor.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
The conditional mean is linear, and the residual \(Y-E[Y\mid X]\) is uncorrelated with \(X\);
for joint normality uncorrelated implies independent. Taking variances in
\(Y=E[Y\mid X]+\text{residual}\) gives
\(\sigma_Y^2=\sigma_Y^2\rho^2+\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)\), since
\(\mathrm{Var}(E[Y\mid X])=\rho^2\sigma_Y^2\). Hence
\(\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)=\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)\). The factor \(1-\rho^2\) is the fraction of
variance unexplained by the linear relationship.
</details>
