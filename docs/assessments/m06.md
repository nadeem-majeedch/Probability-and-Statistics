## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. For any two events \(A\) and \(B\),
A) \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\)
B) \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\)
C) \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)P(B)\)
D) \(P(A\cup B)=1-P(A\cap B)\)
* B
Why: Adding the probabilities double-counts the overlap, so it must be subtracted once. The simple sum is correct only when the events are disjoint.

Q2. If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent with \(P(A)=0.3\) and \(P(B)=0.5\), then \(P(A\cap B)\) is
A) 0.2
B) 0.15
C) 0.35
D) 0.8
* B
Why: Independence multiplies: \(0.3\times0.5=0.15\). Adding would be the union of disjoint events, which is a different situation.

Q3. \(P(A^c)\) equals
A) \(1-P(A)\)
B) \(P(A)-1\)
C) \(1/P(A)\)
D) \(P(A)\)
* A
Why: An event and its complement partition the sample space, whose probability is 1, so their probabilities sum to 1. This is the basis of every "at least one" calculation.

Q4. Two events with strictly positive probability that are mutually exclusive
A) are independent if their probabilities sum to 1
B) are independent only if equally likely
C) must be independent
D) cannot be independent
* D
Why: Disjointness gives \(P(A\cap B)=0\), whereas independence would require \(P(A)P(B)>0\), a contradiction. Mutually exclusive and independent are opposite ideas.

Q5. The conditional probability \(P(A\mid B)\) is defined as
A) \(P(A\cap B)/P(B)\)
B) \(P(A\cap B)/P(A)\)
C) \(P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\)
D) \(P(B)/P(A)\)
* A
Why: Conditioning restricts attention to the outcomes in \(B\) and rescales by \(P(B)\) so the new probabilities sum to 1. Dividing by \(P(A)\) would condition on the wrong event.

Q6. The probabilities assigned to the outcomes of a sample space must
A) sum to 1 and be non-negative
B) sum to the sample size
C) be less than 0.5
D) all be equal
* A
Why: Kolmogorov's axioms require non-negativity, normalisation to 1, and additivity for disjoint events. Equal probabilities are a special case (equally likely outcomes), not a requirement.

Q7. If \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint, then \(P(A\cap B)\) is
A) \(P(A)P(B)\)
B) 0
C) 1
D) \(P(A)+P(B)\)
* B
Why: Disjoint events cannot occur together, so their intersection is empty and has probability zero. Their union probability is the sum.

Q8. Independence of \(A\) and \(B\) is equivalent to
A) \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\)
B) \(P(A\cap B)=0\)
C) \(P(A\cup B)=1\)
D) \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint
* A
Why: Knowing \(B\) occurred does not change the probability of \(A\). This is equivalent to \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\) when \(P(B)>0\).

Q9. Three fair coins are tossed. The probability of three heads is
A) 1/8
B) 3/8
C) 1/3
D) 1/6
* A
Why: The eight outcomes are equally likely and only HHH is favourable, so \(1/8\). The \(3/8\) answer counts exactly two heads.

Q10. Two fair coins are tossed. The probability of at least one head is
A) 1/4
B) 1
C) 1/2
D) 3/4
* D
Why: \(1-P(\text{TT})=1-1/4=3/4\). Listing HT, TH, HH confirms three of four outcomes.

Q11. If \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint with \(P(A)=0.3\) and \(P(B)=0.4\), then \(P(A\cup B)\) is
A) 0.12
B) 0.7
C) 0.58
D) 0.1
* B
Why: For disjoint events the union probability is the sum, \(0.3+0.4=0.7\). Multiplying gives the intersection, which is empty here.

Q12. Drawing two cards without replacement makes the draws
A) complementary
B) independent
C) dependent
D) disjoint
* C
Why: Removing the first card changes the composition of the deck, so the second draw's probability depends on the first. Independence requires replacement or an effectively infinite population.

Q13. The complement of "at least one success" is
A) "exactly one success"
B) "no successes"
C) "at most one success"
D) "all successes"
* B
Why: Between "at least one" and "none" every outcome is covered exactly once. "At most one" is a different event that includes exactly one.

Q14. If the odds in favour of an event are 3 : 1, its probability is
A) 3
B) 0.25
C) 0.33
D) 0.75
* D
Why: Odds \(a:b\) correspond to probability \(a/(a+b)=3/4=0.75\). A probability cannot exceed 1, so 3 is impossible.

Q15. For a partition \(B_1,\dots,B_k\) of the sample space and any event \(A\),
A) \(P(A)=\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)\)
B) \(P(A)=\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)\)
C) \(P(A)=\prod_i P(A\mid B_i)\)
D) \(P(A)=P(A\mid B_1)\)
* B
Why: The law of total probability weights each conditional probability by the probability of its conditioning event. Without the weights, the sum can exceed 1.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** State Kolmogorov's three axioms and explain what each one rules out.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
(i) \(P(A)\ge0\) rules out negative probabilities; (ii) \(P(\Omega)=1\) fixes the scale so the
whole space has probability one; (iii) countable additivity for disjoint events,
\(P(\cup A_i)=\sum P(A_i)\), makes probabilities of combined disjoint events consistent. Together
they imply \(P(\varnothing)=0\) and \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\).
</details>

**S2.** Explain why mutually exclusive events are not independent, using a numerical example.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Take a fair die, \(A=\{1\}\), \(B=\{2\}\). They are disjoint, so \(P(A\cap B)=0\), but
\(P(A)P(B)=1/36\ne0\). Knowing that \(B\) occurred tells you \(A\) did not, which is maximal
dependence, not independence.
</details>

**S3.** What does conditioning mean operationally, and why is \(P(B)>0\) required?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Conditioning restricts the sample space to the outcomes consistent with \(B\) and rescales by
\(P(B)\) so the restricted probabilities again sum to 1. If \(P(B)=0\) the rescaling is
undefined; conditioning on a probability-zero event needs a limiting argument or a different
tool (such as densities).
</details>

**S4.** Why does \(P(A\cup B)\le P(A)+P(B)\)?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because the union counts the overlap once while the sum counts it twice, so
\(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\le P(A)+P(B)\). Equality holds exactly when the events are
disjoint.
</details>

**S5.** Give an example of three events that are pairwise independent but not mutually
independent.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Toss two fair coins; let \(A\) = first is H, \(B\) = second is H, \(C\) = the two coins match.
Each pair is independent (probability \(1/4\) of both), but
\(P(A\cap B\cap C)=1/4\ne P(A)P(B)P(C)=1/8\). Pairwise independence does not imply joint
independence.
</details>

**S6.** Explain the difference between "independent" and "identically distributed".

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Independent means the outcomes carry no information about each other; identically distributed
means they follow the same probability law. Independent draws from different distributions
(e.g. a coin and a die) are independent but not identically distributed; draws from the same
distribution without replacement are identically distributed but dependent.
</details>

**S7.** Why is the multiplication rule for independent events not generally valid?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
\(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\) holds only under independence. In general
\(P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)\), and \(P(A\mid B)\ne P(A)\) whenever the events are dependent —
for example, drawing two aces without replacement.
</details>

**S8.** What is a partition, and why does the law of total probability need one?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A partition is a collection of disjoint events whose union is the whole sample space. Every
outcome of \(A\) lies in exactly one part, so \(A\) splits into disjoint pieces \(A\cap B_i\);
summing their probabilities, each rewritten as \(P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)\), gives \(P(A)\) with no
double counting.
</details>

**S9.** How would you simulate \(P(A\cup B)\) for arbitrary dependent events?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Draw many pairs of outcomes from the joint distribution, count the proportion in which at least
one event occurs, and report that proportion with a simulation standard error
\(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\). This handles dependence automatically, unlike the formula, which
needs \(P(A\cap B)\).
</details>

**S10.** Why is it wrong to add the probabilities of "rain on Saturday" and "rain on Sunday" to
get "rain this weekend"?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because rain on the two days is positively associated, so the events overlap: it can rain on
both. The sum double-counts the overlap and can exceed 1; the correct form subtracts
\(P(\text{both})\).
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** A fair die is rolled. Find the probability that the result is even or greater than 4.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Even = {2,4,6}, greater than 4 = {5,6}; the union is {2,4,5,6}, four outcomes, so the
probability is \(4/6=2/3\). Adding \(3/6+2/6\) would double-count the 6.
</details>

**N2.** One card is drawn from a standard deck. Find the probability it is a heart or a king.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(\heartsuit)=13/52\), \(P(\text{king})=4/52\), and the king of hearts is in both, so
\(P=13/52+4/52-1/52=16/52=4/13\approx0.3077\).
</details>

**N3.** Two independent events have probabilities 0.4 and 0.7. Find the probability that both
occur and that neither occurs.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Both: \(0.4\times0.7=0.28\). Neither: complements are independent, so
\(0.6\times0.3=0.18\). Note that \(1-0.28=0.72\) is the probability of at least one, not of
neither.
</details>

**N4.** \(P(A\cap B)=0.12\) and \(P(B)=0.3\). Find \(P(A\mid B)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(A\mid B)=0.12/0.3=0.4\). If instead \(P(A)=0.5\), the events are not independent, since
\(0.12\ne0.5\times0.3=0.15\).
</details>

**N5.** A component fails independently on each of three tests with probability 0.2. Find the
probability it fails at least one test.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(\text{passes all})=0.8^3=0.512\), so \(P(\text{at least one failure})=1-0.512=0.488\).
Summing the three individual failure probabilities would double-count multiple failures.
</details>

**N6.** Machine A makes 60 % of items with a 2 % defect rate; machine B makes 40 % with a 5 %
defect rate. Find the overall defect rate.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Total probability: \(0.6\times0.02+0.4\times0.05=0.012+0.020=0.032\), i.e. 3.2 %. The
unweighted average of 2 % and 5 % (3.5 %) would be wrong because the machines differ in volume.
</details>

**N7.** \(P(A)=0.5\), \(P(B)=0.4\), \(P(A\cap B)=0.2\). Find \(P(A\cup B)\) and \(P(A\mid B)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(A\cup B)=0.5+0.4-0.2=0.7\). \(P(A\mid B)=0.2/0.4=0.5\), which equals \(P(A)\), so
these events happen to be independent.
</details>

**N8.** \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint with \(P(A)=0.3\), \(P(B)=0.5\). Find \(P(A\cup B)\) and
\(P(A\mid B)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(P(A\cup B)=0.3+0.5=0.8\) and \(P(A\mid B)=0/(0.5)=0\): given \(B\), \(A\) is
impossible. Disjointness is the strongest possible negative dependence.
</details>

**N9.** Three fair coins are tossed. Find the probability of exactly one head.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
There are \(2^3=8\) equally likely outcomes and three with exactly one head (HTT, THT, TTH), so
\(3/8=0.375\). The binomial coefficient \(\binom{3}{1}=3\) counts the positions.
</details>

**N10.** An event has probability 0.2. Express it as odds in favour and as odds against.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Odds in favour \(=0.2/0.8=1:4\) (one for, four against). Odds against \(=0.8/0.2=4:1\). Odds
are a ratio of probabilities and are unbounded above, unlike probability.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A spam filter flags 2 % of legitimate emails and 95 % of spam. Spam is 30 % of traffic.
Find the probability that a flagged email is legitimate before considering content.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Flagged = spam flagged or legitimate flagged:
\(0.3\times0.95+0.7\times0.02=0.285+0.014=0.299\). Share legitimate among flagged:
\(0.014/0.299=0.0468\), about 4.7 %. Even a good filter produces mostly false alarms when
the flagged group is dominated by the base rate, which is the point of Module 07.
</details>

**P2.** Two servers each fail independently with probability 0.01 per day. What is the
probability that at least one fails on a given day, and why is redundancy useful?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(P(\text{at least one})=1-0.99^2=0.0199\), about 2 %. Redundancy does not reduce the
failure probability of an individual server; it converts "both fail" (probability 0.0001) into
the event that matters for downtime. Availability is \(1-0.0001=0.9999\).
</details>

**P3.** A diagnostic question: a patient has a 1 % prior probability of disease; a test is 90 %
sensitive and 95 % specific. Without computing, say whether a positive result means the patient
probably has the disease.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
No. With 10,000 people, 100 have the disease and 90 test positive; 9,900 are healthy and 495
test positive falsely. So 90 of 585 positives are true, about 15 %. The base rate dominates: the
false positives from the large healthy group outnumber true positives.
</details>

**P4.** A survey asks "Do you support the policy?" and 62 % say yes. 70 % of respondents are
from group A (support 45 %) and 30 % from group B. What support rate does group B need to
explain the result?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Solve \(0.7\times0.45+0.3p=0.62\): \(0.315+0.3p=0.62\), so \(p=0.305/0.3=1.017\), which is
impossible. The reported overall figure is inconsistent with the stated subgroup rates, so
either the weights, the subgroup rates or the total are wrong — a useful internal-consistency
check.
</details>

**P5.** In a lottery, 1 in 1,000 tickets wins. A player buys 10 tickets. Is the winning
probability 10/1,000? Explain.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Only if the tickets are distinct and exactly one winning ticket exists among 1,000: then the
probability is 10/1,000 = 1 %. If the draws are independent (with replacement), the probability
is \(1-(0.999)^{10}=0.00996\), slightly less than 1 %. The two models agree only
approximately.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Prove that if \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, then \(A\) and \(B^c\) are independent.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(P(A\cap B^c)=P(A)-P(A\cap B)=P(A)-P(A)P(B)=P(A)(1-P(B))=P(A)P(B^c)\). Since
\(0\le P(A)\le1\), this is the definition of independence for \(A\) and \(B^c\). The same
argument iterates to complements of either event.
</details>

**C2.** Prove the inclusion–exclusion formula for three events and verify it on a simple
example.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Write \(P(A\cup B\cup C)=P((A\cup B)\cup C)=P(A\cup B)+P(C)-P((A\cup B)\cap C)\). Expanding the
last term by distributivity gives \(P(A\cap C)+P(B\cap C)-P(A\cap B\cap C)\), and substituting
\(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\) yields
\(P(A)+P(B)+P(C)-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)+P(A\cap B\cap C)\).
Check with a die: \(A=\{1,2\}\), \(B=\{2,3\}\), \(C=\{3,4\}\) gives
\(2/6+2/6+2/6-1/6-0-1/6+0=4/6\), and the union \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) indeed has probability 4/6.
</details>

**C3.** Show that \(P(A\mid B)>P(A)\) if and only if \(P(B\mid A)>P(A)\), and interpret.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\) and \(P(B\mid A)=P(A\cap B)/P(A)\). The first exceeds \(P(A)\)
iff \(P(A\cap B)>P(A)P(B)\); the second exceeds \(P(B)\) iff the same inequality holds. So
either event raises the probability of the other exactly when they are positively associated —
the symmetry behind "if A makes B more likely, B makes A more likely".
</details>
