## A. Problem statement

A university service centre runs a satisfaction survey at the end of each semester.
Responses arrive on a five-point scale, together with waiting time, department and
whether the issue was resolved at first contact. Management wants a service-quality
dashboard: which departments are underperforming, whether long waits explain
dissatisfaction, and whether first-contact resolution matters.

## B. Background and context

Satisfaction data are ordinal, bounded and often skewed: most respondents cluster at the
top, a dissatisfied minority sits at the bottom, and the "average" of 3.4 tells you very
little. Treating a 1–5 Likert item as if it were a continuous measurement is common and
sometimes defensible for group comparisons, but the analyst must know when it is not. This
case uses the survey to practise descriptive summaries for ordinal data, group comparison,
and the discipline of testing a plausible hypothesis that turns out to be unsupported.

## C. Dataset description

`satisfaction.csv` has 180 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `respondent_id` | integer | Anonymous identifier |
| `department` | text | Registration (55), Library (40), IT Desk (46), Finance (39) |
| `wait_minutes` | float | Waiting time before service, 1–90 |
| `resolved_first_contact` | text | `yes` / `no` |
| `satisfaction` | integer | 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied) |

**Generation method.** Department is multinomial with probabilities 0.30/0.25/0.25/0.20.
Waiting time is gamma(shape 2.5, scale 8), so it is right-skewed with mean about 20
minutes. Satisfaction starts from a department baseline (Library 4.0, IT 3.5, Registration
3.2, Finance 3.1), falls by 0.04 per waiting minute, and adds normal(0, 0.9) noise before
being rounded and clipped to 1–5. First-contact resolution is drawn independently of
satisfaction (70 % "yes"), which makes it a deliberate null result for the analyst to
discover rather than a confirmatory story.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What is the distribution of satisfaction scores, and what does the mean hide?
2. Which departments differ, and by how much?
3. How strong is the association between waiting time and satisfaction?
4. Do respondents whose issue was resolved at first contact report higher satisfaction?
5. Is a t-test appropriate for a 1–5 ordinal outcome, and what should be reported
   alongside it?
6. What would you put on a one-page service-quality dashboard?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Frequency tables and proportions for ordinal data.
- Median, IQR and mode for bounded scales.
- Grouped means and standard deviations; box plots.
- Pearson and Spearman correlation (and why they can differ).
- Two-sample t-test, Welch correction, and its assumptions.
- One-way ANOVA for four group means.
- Effect size (difference in means relative to the standard deviation).
- The meaning of a non-significant result.

## F. Mathematical formulation

For department \(j\) with \(n_j\) responses, the group mean and standard deviation are

\[
\bar{y}_j=\frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{i\in j} y_i,\qquad
s_j=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_j-1}\sum_{i\in j}(y_i-\bar{y}_j)^2}.
\]

Welch's two-sample statistic for resolved versus not:

\[
t=\frac{\bar{y}_1-\bar{y}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}},
\qquad \nu=\frac{(s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2)^2}
{\frac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\frac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}.
\]

One-way ANOVA decomposes total variation into between- and within-group parts:

\[
F=\frac{\mathrm{SS_{between}}/(k-1)}{\mathrm{SS_{within}}/(n-k)},\qquad
\mathrm{SS_{between}}=\sum_j n_j(\bar{y}_j-\bar{y})^2 .
\]

A standardised mean difference (Cohen's \(d\)) is
\(d=(\bar{y}_1-\bar{y}_2)/s_{\text{pooled}}\).

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Load the file; check the scale values are 1–5 and department labels are clean.
2. Tabulate the satisfaction distribution overall and by department.
3. Compare departments with medians, means, IQRs and box plots.
4. Correlate waiting time with satisfaction, using both Pearson and Spearman.
5. Test first-contact resolution with Welch's t-test; report the difference and its CI.
6. Run one-way ANOVA across departments; follow with a note on which pairs differ.
7. Assemble the dashboard panel and interpret it without over-claiming.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

survey = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/satisfaction.csv")
print(survey.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print("rows:", len(survey), "| missing:", int(survey.isna().sum().sum()))

print("\nsatisfaction frequencies:")
print(survey["satisfaction"].value_counts().sort_index().to_string())

print("\nby department:")
by_dept = survey.groupby("department")["satisfaction"].agg(
    ["count", "median", "mean", "std"]).round(2)
print(by_dept.to_string())

pearson = stats.pearsonr(survey["wait_minutes"], survey["satisfaction"])
spearman = stats.spearmanr(survey["wait_minutes"], survey["satisfaction"])
print(f"\nPearson r = {pearson[0]:.3f} (p = {pearson[1]:.2e})")
print(f"Spearman rho = {spearman.statistic:.3f} (p = {spearman.pvalue:.2e})")

resolved = survey[survey.resolved_first_contact == "yes"]["satisfaction"]
unresolved = survey[survey.resolved_first_contact == "no"]["satisfaction"]
welch = stats.ttest_ind(resolved, unresolved, equal_var=False)
print(f"\nresolved: n={len(resolved)}, mean={resolved.mean():.2f}")
print(f"not resolved: n={len(unresolved)}, mean={unresolved.mean():.2f}")
print(f"Welch t = {welch.statistic:.3f}, p = {welch.pvalue:.3f}")

anova = stats.f_oneway(*[g["satisfaction"].values for _, g in survey.groupby("department")])
print(f"\nANOVA across departments: F = {anova.statistic:.2f}, p = {anova.pvalue:.2e}")

model = smf.ols("satisfaction ~ wait_minutes", data=survey).fit()
print(f"slope of satisfaction on waiting time: {model.params['wait_minutes']:.4f} "
      f"(p = {model.pvalues['wait_minutes']:.2e})")
```

## J. Visualization and interpretation

```python
            ylabel="Satisfaction")
fig.tight_layout()
```

**How to read it.** Every department sits below the scale midpoint: means run from 2.21
(Finance) to 3.05 (Library), and the overall mean is 2.55 with median 3. The box plot's
overlap is partial — Library is clearly above Finance and Registration, while the IT Desk
sits in between. The scatter shows a downward drift with substantial vertical spread at
every waiting time: waiting matters, but it is far from the whole story.

## K. Expected conclusions

- Overall satisfaction is low: mean 2.55, median 3, SD 1.02 on a five-point scale.
- Departments differ significantly (F = 7.64, p < 0.001): Library 3.05 and IT Desk 2.76
  are above Finance 2.21 and Registration 2.25.
- Waiting time is negatively associated with satisfaction (r = −0.435, Spearman similar),
  and each extra 10 minutes corresponds to about 0.34 of a scale point.
- First-contact resolution shows **no** detectable difference (2.55 vs 2.56, p = 0.96);
  the hypothesis is not supported, and the report should say so.
- Reporting a bare average hides the fact that nearly half of respondents score 1 or 2.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — distribution.** The frequencies are concentrated at 2 and 3, with a meaningful
block at 1 and relatively few 5s; 47.8 % of respondents score 1 or 2. The mean (2.55) sits below the median (3) because the
low scores pull it down; for ordinal data the median plus the full frequency table is the
honest summary, and the mean is reported only because group comparisons need it.

**Step 2 — departments.** Means: Finance 2.21 (n = 39), Registration 2.25 (n = 55),
IT Desk 2.76 (n = 46), Library 3.05 (n = 40). Standard deviations are all close to 1
(0.95–0.99), so the department differences are shifts in the whole distribution rather
than differences in consistency. The largest gap, Library minus Finance, is 0.84 scale
points, about 0.85 standard deviations — a large effect by conventional standards.

**Step 3 — ANOVA.** F = 7.64 on 3 and 176 degrees of freedom, p = 7.9 × 10⁻⁵. At least
one department differs. The largest pairwise gap is Library minus Finance, 0.84 scale
points, or d = 0.87 standard deviations — a large effect. A follow-up Tukey HSD (M17)
would show Library significantly above Finance and Registration; IT Desk overlaps with
both. The dashboard should highlight Finance and Registration, not the Library.

**Step 4 — waiting time.** Pearson r = −0.435 and Spearman ρ is close to it, so the
association is monotone and roughly linear; the ordinal nature of the outcome does not
distort the conclusion here. The regression is satisfaction = 3.24 − 0.034 × wait: ten
minutes less waiting is worth about 0.34 of a scale point, roughly the gap between
Registration and IT Desk. Because both variables are bounded, the linear model must not
be extrapolated: it would predict satisfaction of zero beyond about 96 minutes.

**Step 5 — the null result.** Resolved at first contact: mean 2.55 (n = 117); not
resolved: mean 2.56 (n = 63). t = −0.05, p = 0.96. This is a genuine "no evidence of a
difference" and the report must resist the temptation to spin it. The 95 % confidence
interval for the difference is −0.34 to +0.32 scale points, so an effect smaller
than a third of a point cannot be excluded; the study is informative about large effects
only. Note also that the generator made resolution independent of satisfaction, so this
is the correct answer by construction — a reminder that a null result is a result.

**Step 6 — dashboard.** Headline 1: overall satisfaction 2.55/5, with 48 % scoring 1–2.
Headline 2: Finance and Registration average below 2.3; Library reaches 3.05. Headline 3:
each 10 minutes of waiting costs about 0.4 scale points. Caveat: 180 responses, a single
semester, no information on the reason for the visit, and self-selection in who answers
surveys.

**Methodological notes.** A t-test on a five-point ordinal scale is a compromise: it
assumes equal intervals between categories and roughly symmetric residuals, neither of
which is exactly true. For group comparisons with n ≈ 50 per group the t-test is robust,
but a Mann-Whitney U test or an ordinal logistic model is the more defensible primary
analysis, and both would reach the same conclusion here. Always report the median and the
distribution alongside any mean.
