## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. The derivative of \(f(x)=\ln(3x)\) with respect to \(x\) is
A) \(1/x\)
B) \(3\ln x\)
C) \(1/(3x)\)
D) \(3/x\)
* A
Why: \(\ln(3x)=\ln 3+\ln x\), and the constant differentiates to zero, leaving \(1/x\). The \(3/x\) answer forgets the chain rule's cancellation; \(1/(3x)\) cancels the wrong way.

Q2. \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\infty} e^{-\lambda x}\,dx\) for \(\lambda>0\) equals
A) \(\lambda^2\)
B) \(1\)
C) \(\lambda\)
D) \(1/\lambda\)
* D
Why: The antiderivative is \(-e^{-\lambda x}/\lambda\); evaluating at 0 and \(\infty\) gives \(1/\lambda\). This integral is exactly why the exponential density carries the normalising constant \(\lambda\).

Q3. \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k\) equals
A) \((n+1)^2/2\)
B) \(n^2\)
C) \(n(n+1)/2\)
D) \(n(n-1)/2\)
* C
Why: The sum of the first \(n\) integers is the arithmetic series with mean \((n+1)/2\) over \(n\) terms. \(n(n-1)/2\) is the sum up to \(n-1\), a common off-by-one error.

Q4. For \(a,b>0\), \(\log(ab)\) equals
A) \(\log a+\log b\)
B) \(\log(a)/\log(b)\)
C) \(b\log a\)
D) \(\log a\cdot\log b\)
* A
Why: Logarithms turn multiplication into addition — the property that makes log-likelihoods additive across independent observations.

Q5. If \(f''(x)>0\) on an interval, then \(f\) is
A) constant
B) decreasing
C) concave on that interval
D) convex on that interval
* D
Why: A positive second derivative means the slope is increasing, which is convexity. Concavity corresponds to \(f''<0\), and monotonicity is a statement about \(f'\), not \(f''\).

Q6. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^n\) equals
A) \(1\)
B) \(2\)
C) \(e\)
D) \(\infty\)
* C
Why: This limit defines Euler's number \(e\approx2.718\). It is the continuous-compounding limit, and it underlies the Poisson and exponential distributions.

Q7. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{a}\) for real \(a\) is
A) \(x^{a}/a\)
B) \(a x^{a}\)
C) \(a x^{a-1}\)
D) \(x^{a-1}\)
* C
Why: The power rule, valid for any real exponent, including negative and fractional ones. It is used to differentiate likelihoods of gamma and beta densities.

Q8. \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} r^k\) converges to
A) \(\infty\) for all \(r\)
B) \(1/(1-r)\) for \(|r|<1\)
C) \(1/(1+r)\) for all \(r\)
D) \(r/(1-r)\) for \(|r|<1\)
* B
Why: The geometric series converges only when the ratio is smaller than 1 in absolute value; the sum is \(1/(1-r)\). This identity produces the geometric distribution's mean.

Q9. \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 3x^2\,dx\) equals
A) \(1/3\)
B) \(1\)
C) \(3\)
D) \(2\)
* B
Why: The antiderivative is \(x^3\), which is 1 at \(x=1\) and 0 at \(x=0\). A density integrates to 1 over its support, which is what this calculation verifies.

Q10. The expression \(\ln(x-2)\) is defined for
A) \(x>2\)
B) \(x\ge 2\)
C) \(x<2\)
D) all real \(x\)
* A
Why: A logarithm needs a strictly positive argument, so \(x-2>0\). At \(x=2\) the argument is zero and the log diverges.

Q11. For \(f(x)=x^2\) and \(g(x)=x+1\), \(f(g(x))\) equals
A) \(x^2+1\)
B) \((x+1)^2\)
C) \(x^2+x+1\)
D) \(x^2+x\)
* B
Why: Composition applies \(g\) first, then \(f\), so every \(x\) in \(f\) is replaced by \(x+1\). \(x^2+1\) applies the functions in the wrong order.

Q12. \(\exp(\ln 5)\) equals
A) \(\ln 5\)
B) \(e^5\)
C) \(1/5\)
D) \(5\)
* D
Why: The exponential and natural logarithm are inverse functions, so they cancel. This inverse relationship is why log and exp are the standard link functions.

Q13. The identity \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\) follows from
A) linearity of expectation after expanding \((X-\mu)^2\)
B) the central limit theorem
C) assuming \(X\) is normal
D) the law of total probability
* A
Why: Expand \((X-\mu)^2=X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2\) and take expectations; the cross term becomes \(-2\mu^2\). No distributional assumption is involved.

Q14. If \(g\) is strictly increasing, then \(a<b\) implies
A) \(g(a)>g(b)\)
B) \(g(a)<g(b)\)
C) \(g(a)=g(b)\)
D) nothing about \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\)
* B
Why: That is the definition of strict increase, and it is why quantiles transform monotonically — the basis of the probability integral transform.

Q15. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{2x}\) equals
A) \(e^{2}\)
B) \(e^{2x}\)
C) \(2e^{2x}\)
D) \(2xe^{2x}\)
* C
Why: The chain rule brings down the inner derivative, 2. Forgetting it gives \(e^{2x}\), the most common error with exponential models.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain why a probability density may exceed 1 while a probability may not.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A density is probability per unit of \(x\), so its height depends on the units and on how
concentrated the distribution is; a narrow uniform density on an interval of length 0.1 has
height 10. Probability is the area under the density over an interval, and areas are bounded by
1 because the total area is 1.
</details>

**S2.** State the two properties that make a function a probability mass function.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Every value is non-negative, \(p(x)\ge0\), and the values sum to one, \(\sum_x p(x)=1\). Any
assignment satisfying these two conditions describes a valid discrete distribution.
</details>

**S3.** Why do logarithms appear so often in statistical work?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Three reasons: they turn products of likelihoods into sums, which are easier to differentiate
and numerically stable; they linearise multiplicative relationships (\(y=ae^{bx}\) becomes
\(\ln y=\ln a+bx\)); and they symmetrise right-skewed positive data such as incomes or
latencies.
</details>

**S4.** What does it mean for a function to be convex, and what does Jensen's inequality say
about it?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Convex means the chord between any two points lies above the graph, equivalently \(f''\ge0\).
Jensen's inequality states \(f(E[X])\le E[f(X)]\) for convex \(f\). For \(f(x)=x^2\) it gives
\((E[X])^2\le E[X^2]\), which is exactly the non-negativity of variance.
</details>

**S5.** Explain the difference between a derivative and an integral in the context of a
probability density.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The integral accumulates area and gives probability: \(P(a<X<b)=\int_a^b f(x)dx\). The
derivative goes the other way: the density is the derivative of the cumulative distribution
function, \(f(x)=F'(x)\). Integration moves from density to probability, differentiation from
CDF to density.
</details>

**S6.** Why is \(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)=0\), and what does that imply about the deviations?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Expanding, \(\sum x_i-n\bar x=n\bar x-n\bar x=0\) because \(\bar x\) is defined as the sum
divided by \(n\). The positive and negative deviations exactly cancel, which is why we square
them (or take absolute values) to measure spread — the raw sum carries no information.
</details>

**S7.** Explain why \(\ln x\) grows more slowly than any positive power of \(x\).

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
For large \(x\), \(\ln x/x^a\to0\) for every \(a>0\); equivalently \(e^{x}\) eventually
outgrows every polynomial, so its inverse grows more slowly than every root. This is why
log-likelihoods of heavy-tailed models stay finite when raw likelihoods underflow.
</details>

**S8.** What is the geometric series used for in probability?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Summing probabilities of repeated failures: in a geometric distribution,
\(P(X=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p\), and the total probability is \(p\sum(1-p)^{k-1}=p\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-p)}=1\).
The same identity gives the mean \(1/p\) after differentiating.
</details>

**S9.** Why does a change of units \(y=a+bx\) leave a correlation unchanged but change a
covariance?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Covariance scales: \(\mathrm{Cov}(a+bX,c+dY)=bd\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\). Correlation divides by
the standard deviations, which scale by \(|b|\) and \(|d|\), so the factors cancel and the
correlation is unit-free. That is why correlation is comparable across variables and covariance
is not.
</details>

**S10.** Explain the role of a normalising constant in a probability density, using
\(f(x)=c\,e^{-x}\) on \(x>0\).

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The constant \(c\) is chosen so the total area is 1:
\(\int_0^\infty c e^{-x}dx=c=1\), so \(c=1\). In Bayesian work the same idea appears as the
marginal likelihood, which is the constant that makes the posterior integrate to one; it can
often be ignored when only relative posterior probabilities matter.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** Compute \(\sum_{k=1}^{10}k\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^{10}k^2\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
First sum \(=10(11)/2=55\). Second sum \(=10\cdot11\cdot21/6=385\), using
\(\sum k^2=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6\). These two sums appear whenever you expand
\(\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\).
</details>

**N2.** Evaluate \(\binom{8}{3}\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\binom{8}{3}=\frac{8\cdot7\cdot6}{3\cdot2\cdot1}=\frac{336}{6}=56\). Equivalently
8!/(3!5!) = 40,320/(6×120) = 56.
</details>

**N3.** Evaluate \(\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
The antiderivative is \(x^3\); evaluating gives \(2^3-0^3=8\). Geometrically it is the area
under \(3x^2\) from 0 to 2.
</details>

**N4.** Find \(c\) such that \(f(x)=c e^{-2x}\) for \(x>0\) is a density.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Require \(\int_0^\infty c e^{-2x}dx=1\). The integral is \(c/2\), so \(c=2\). Check:
\(\int_0^\infty 2e^{-2x}dx=[-e^{-2x}]_0^\infty=1\).
</details>

**N5.** Evaluate \(\log_{10}1000\) and \(\ln(e^4)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\log_{10}1000=\log_{10}10^3=3\); \(\ln(e^4)=4\). These identities are used constantly
when converting between log and natural-log scales.
</details>

**N6.** Differentiate \(f(x)=x^2\ln x\) and evaluate \(f'(1)\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Product rule: \(f'(x)=2x\ln x+x^2(1/x)=2x\ln x+x\). At \(x=1\), \(\ln1=0\), so
\(f'(1)=0+1=1\). The log term vanishes at 1, leaving only the power term's contribution.
</details>

**N7.** Evaluate \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(1/3)^k\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Geometric series with ratio \(r=1/3\), so the sum is \(\frac{1}{1-1/3}=\frac{1}{2/3}=1.5\).
The first few terms 1, 1/3, 1/9, … confirm the total is between 1 and 2.
</details>

**N8.** Evaluate \(\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+2/n)^n\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Rewrite as \(\left[(1+2/n)^{n/2}\right]^2\to e^{2}\), since
\((1+a/n)^n\to e^a\). So the limit is \(e^2\approx7.389\).
</details>

**N9.** For \(f(x)=\frac{x-1}{x+2}\), compute \(f(0)\), \(f(2)\) and the value of \(x\) where
\(f(x)=0\).

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(f(0)=-1/2=-0.5\); \(f(2)=1/4=0.25\); \(f(x)=0\) requires the numerator to vanish,
so \(x=1\) (the denominator is non-zero there). The function is undefined at \(x=-2\).
</details>

**N10.** For the values 2, 4, 6 compute \(\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\) and the sample variance.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(\bar x=4\); deviations \(-2,0,2\); squared deviations 4, 0, 4, so the sum is \(8\). Sample
variance \(=8/(3-1)=4\), and the sample standard deviation is 2.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A model's log-likelihood is \(\ell(\theta)=\sum_i \ln f(x_i;\theta)\). A colleague
suggests maximising the product of the densities instead. Explain why the answers are the same
and why the log version is used.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The logarithm is strictly increasing, so it preserves the location of the maximum: the
\(\theta\) maximising the product also maximises the sum of logs. The log version is used
because products of hundreds of small densities underflow to zero in floating point, and
because sums differentiate term by term, giving the score equations.
</details>

**P2.** You are modelling claim sizes and find that \(\ln(\text{amount})\) is roughly normal.
Write the implied model for the amount and explain what the parameters mean.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
If \(\ln A\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) then \(A\) is lognormal with median \(e^\mu\) and mean
\(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\). \(\mu\) is the median on the log scale (a multiplicative location), and
\(\sigma\) controls multiplicative dispersion: a one-unit rise in \(\sigma\) widens the ratio
between the 84th and 50th percentiles by a factor \(e^\sigma\).
</details>

**P3.** A variate is transformed as \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\). Show that \(E[z]=0\) and
\(\mathrm{Var}(z)=1\), and explain why this standardisation is used before comparing
measurements on different scales.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(E[z]=(E[x]-\mu)/\sigma=0\) by linearity. \(\mathrm{Var}(z)=\mathrm{Var}(x)/\sigma^2=1\). The
transformation removes location and scale, so a value of 2 means "two standard deviations above
the mean" regardless of whether the variable is in millimetres or dollars — the basis of
z-scores and of many distance metrics.
</details>

**P4.** A numerical routine returns a probability of \(1.2\) for an event. List two possible
causes and how you would diagnose each.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Either the density was mistaken for a probability (a density can exceed 1), or the computation
is wrong — an unnormalised likelihood, a missing normalising constant, or numerical overflow in
an exponent. Diagnose by checking whether the quantity is a density (integrate it over its
support) and whether probabilities sum or integrate to 1; inspect intermediate values for
overflow.
</details>

**P5.** An analyst writes code that computes \(\exp(-x)\) for \(x\) around 800 and gets 0.
Explain what happened and give a numerically stable alternative for a log-probability.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
\(e^{-800}\) is about \(10^{-347}\), below the smallest representable double, so it underflows
to 0. Work on the log scale instead: keep \(\ln p=-800\) and combine log-probabilities by
addition; when a probability is genuinely needed, use a log-sum-exp trick or a library routine
that returns log-probabilities (for example `scipy.special.logsumexp`).
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Prove \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\) from the definition
\(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Expand the square: \((X-\mu)^2=X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2\). Taking expectations and using linearity,
\(E[X^2]-2\mu E[X]+\mu^2=E[X^2]-2\mu^2+\mu^2=E[X^2]-\mu^2\). No independence or distributional
assumption is used.
</details>

**C2.** Prove by induction that \(\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Base case \(n=1\): left side 1, right side \(1\cdot2/2=1\). Inductive step: assume the formula
for \(n\); then \(\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}k=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+(n+1)=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}\), which is the
formula with \(n+1\). Hence it holds for all positive integers.
</details>

**C3.** Using the tangent-line argument, show Jensen's inequality
\(f(E[X])\le E[f(X)]\) for a twice-differentiable convex \(f\), and apply it to \(f(x)=-\ln x\)
to obtain the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality for two positive numbers.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Convexity gives \(f(x)\ge f(\mu)+f'(\mu)(x-\mu)\) for all \(x\), where \(\mu=E[X]\): the graph
lies above its tangent. Taking expectations, \(E[f(X)]\ge f(\mu)+f'(\mu)(E[X]-\mu)=f(\mu)\).
For \(f(x)=-\ln x\) (convex on \(x>0\)) this gives \(-\ln E[X]\le -E[\ln X]\), i.e.
\(\ln E[X]\ge E[\ln X]\). With \(X\) uniform on \(\{a,b\}\), \(E[X]=(a+b)/2\) and
\(E[\ln X]=(\ln a+\ln b)/2=\ln\sqrt{ab}\), so \(\frac{a+b}{2}\ge\sqrt{ab}\).
</details>
