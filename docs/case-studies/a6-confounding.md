## A. Problem statement

A university introduced optional tutoring. Tutored students average 80.3 marks against 67.0
for untutored students — a 13.3-mark gap — and the difference is overwhelmingly significant.
The dean wants to make tutoring compulsory for everyone on the strength of that number. A
statistician must determine how much of the gap is the tutoring and how much is the kind of
student who chooses tutoring.

## B. Background and context

This is the textbook confounding problem, and it is the most consequential idea in applied
statistics. Students who seek tutoring are, by construction, more motivated, better
prepared and more likely to attend class. The observed difference therefore mixes the effect
of tutoring with the effect of motivation. Regression adjustment, stratification and
propensity methods are all attempts to compare like with like; none is a substitute for
randomisation. This case builds the intuition numerically, then shows the limits of what
adjustment can claim.

## C. Dataset description

`student_outcomes.csv` has 300 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `student_id` | integer | 4001–4300 |
| `prior_gpa` | float | Prior GPA, 0–4 |
| `motivation_score` | float | Standardised motivation measure |
| `tutoring` | integer | 1 if the student attended tutoring (122 students) |
| `attendance_pct` | float | Attendance, 30–100 |
| `final_mark` | float | Final mark, 0–100 |

**Generation method.** Motivation is standard normal. Prior GPA is
\(2.6+0.5\cdot\text{motivation}+N(0,0.35)\). Tutoring is chosen when
\(\text{motivation}+N(0,0.9)>0.3\), so motivated students self-select into tutoring.
Attendance is \(70+8\cdot\text{motivation}+N(0,10)\). The final mark is
\(45+6\cdot\text{GPA}+3.5\cdot\text{tutoring}+0.15\cdot\text{attendance}
+4\cdot\text{motivation}+N(0,6)\).

The true causal effect of tutoring is therefore **3.5 marks**, while the naive difference is
13.3 — a four-fold overstatement built entirely from confounding.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What is the naive difference in mean marks between tutored and untutored students?
2. How do the groups differ on prior GPA, motivation and attendance?
3. Is the naive difference statistically significant?
4. What happens to the tutoring coefficient when prior GPA, motivation and attendance are
   added to the regression?
5. Which covariate removes the most confounding?
6. What would a randomised trial have measured?
7. How much of the remaining 3.5-mark estimate could still be confounded?

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Confounding and the backdoor criterion (informal).
- Selection bias in observational comparisons.
- Multiple regression as adjustment; omitted-variable bias.
- Mediation versus confounding: attendance may be a *consequence* of motivation and a
  *cause* of marks.
- Standardised effect sizes.
- The limits of adjustment and the case for randomisation.
- Sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding.

## F. Mathematical formulation

Naive difference:

\[
\hat\Delta=\bar y_{\text{tutored}}-\bar y_{\text{untutored}}.
\]

Adjusted model:

\[
y_i=\beta_0+\beta_T T_i+\beta_1\text{GPA}_i+\beta_2 M_i+\beta_3 A_i+\varepsilon_i,
\]

where \(T\) is tutoring, \(M\) motivation and \(A\) attendance. The coefficient \(\beta_T\) is the
tutoring effect *conditional on* the covariates; it is unbiased only if no unmeasured
variable affects both \(T\) and \(y\).

Omitted-variable bias for a single omitted confounder \(M\):

\[
\text{bias}=\beta_M\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(T,M)}{\mathrm{Var}(T)}\quad\text{(in the simple case)}.
\]

The naive difference decomposes approximately as
\(\hat\Delta\approx\beta_T+\text{selection on GPA}+\text{selection on motivation}\).

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Compare group means for marks and for every covariate.
2. Test the naive difference.
3. Fit the unadjusted regression of marks on tutoring.
4. Add covariates one at a time, recording how the tutoring coefficient moves.
5. Fit the full model; report the adjusted effect with a confidence interval.
6. Standardise the effect (divide by the residual SD) for interpretability.
7. Discuss which covariates are confounders and which are mediators.
8. State what a randomised trial would add and estimate the required sample size.

## H. Student tasks

1. Report the naive difference and its t-test p-value.
2. Compare motivation and prior GPA between groups. Which is more imbalanced?
3. Fit marks ~ tutoring. Report the coefficient.
4. Add prior_gpa only. How much does the coefficient fall?
5. Add motivation. How much further does it fall?
6. Add attendance. Does the coefficient change again? Explain why attendance is ambiguous.
7. Report the final adjusted estimate with a 95 % interval.
8. Write a paragraph for the dean distinguishing the naive and adjusted claims.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

data = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/student_outcomes.csv")
print(data.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print(f"n = {len(data)}, tutored = {int(data.tutoring.sum())}")

tutored = data.loc[data.tutoring == 1, "final_mark"]
untutored = data.loc[data.tutoring == 0, "final_mark"]
print(f"\nmean marks: tutored {tutored.mean():.2f}, untutored {untutored.mean():.2f}, "
      f"difference {tutored.mean() - untutored.mean():+.2f}")
print("Welch t-test:", [round(float(x), 3) for x in
                        stats.ttest_ind(tutored, untutored, equal_var=False)])

print("\ncovariate balance:")
print(data.groupby("tutoring")[["prior_gpa", "motivation_score", "attendance_pct"]]
      .mean().round(2).to_string())

models = {
    "unadjusted": "final_mark ~ tutoring",
    "+ prior GPA": "final_mark ~ tutoring + prior_gpa",
    "+ motivation": "final_mark ~ tutoring + prior_gpa + motivation_score",
    "+ attendance": "final_mark ~ tutoring + prior_gpa + motivation_score + attendance_pct",
}
print("\ntutoring coefficient as covariates are added:")
rows = []
for label, formula in models.items():
    fit = smf.ols(formula, data=data).fit()
    lo, hi = fit.conf_int().loc["tutoring"]
    rows.append({"model": label, "coef": round(fit.params.tutoring, 2),
                 "ci_low": round(lo, 2), "ci_high": round(hi, 2),
                 "p": f"{fit.pvalues.tutoring:.1e}", "r2": round(fit.rsquared, 3)})
print(pd.DataFrame(rows).to_string(index=False))

full = smf.ols(models["+ attendance"], data=data).fit()
effect = full.params.tutoring / np.sqrt(full.mse_resid)
print(f"\nadjusted effect = {full.params.tutoring:.2f} marks "
      f"(standardised {effect:.2f} SD), p = {full.pvalues.tutoring:.2e}")
print(f"correlation(tutoring, motivation) = "
      f"{np.corrcoef(data.tutoring, data.motivation_score)[0, 1]:.3f}")
print(f"reduction from naive to adjusted: "
      f"{(1 - full.params.tutoring / (tutored.mean() - untutored.mean())) * 100:.0f}%")
```

## K. Expected conclusions

- Naive difference: 13.26 marks (80.26 vs 66.99), Welch t = 13.2, p < 10⁻¹⁵.
- Group imbalance is large: motivation 0.71 vs −0.50, prior GPA 2.95 vs 2.38, attendance
  77.3 vs 65.6. Correlation between tutoring and motivation is 0.613.
- Adjusting for prior GPA alone cuts the coefficient to 6.19; adding motivation takes it to
  3.61; adding attendance gives **3.45 marks** (95 % CI 1.84–5.06).
- The adjusted effect is a quarter of the naive one — and close to the 3.5-mark causal effect
  built into the generator.
- Attendance is both a confounder and a mediator (motivation raises attendance, attendance
  raises marks), so adjusting for it changes the interpretation: 3.45 is the effect of
  tutoring *holding attendance fixed*.
- The adjusted model still cannot rule out unmeasured confounding; a randomised trial is the
  only design that identifies the causal effect cleanly.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — the naive comparison.** Tutored 80.26, untutored 66.99, difference 13.26. The
t-statistic of 13.2 with 300 students is overwhelming; there is no doubt that tutored
students score higher. The question is why.

**Step 2 — balance.** Tutored students have motivation 0.71 versus −0.50 (a difference of
1.21 standard deviations), prior GPA 2.95 versus 2.38 and attendance 77.3 versus 65.6. In an
experiment these would be balanced by randomisation; here they differ sharply, and each is
independently related to marks. The correlation between tutoring and motivation is 0.613,
which is very strong for a binary–continuous pair.

**Step 3 — adjustment, one covariate at a time.** Adding prior GPA drops the coefficient
from 13.26 to 6.19, because GPA is a strong predictor and is 0.57 points higher in the
tutored group. Adding motivation takes it to 3.61 — motivation was doing most of the
remaining work. Adding attendance gives 3.45, the estimate closest to the true 3.5. This stepwise shrinkage is the most persuasive way to show
confounding — the coefficient moving as controls enter is the visible fingerprint of omitted
variables.

**Step 4 — interpretation of 3.45.** Holding prior GPA, motivation and attendance constant,
tutoring is associated with 3.45 extra marks, with a 95 % interval of 1.84 to 5.06. That is a
modest but real educational effect — 0.63 of a residual standard deviation, a small-to-medium
standardised effect. It is not nothing; it is simply not
13 marks.

**Step 5 — the attendance ambiguity.** Attendance is a *mediator* if tutoring improves
attendance, and a *confounder* if motivation raises both. Controlling for it answers "what
is the effect of tutoring for students who attend the same amount?" — which is not the
policy question. The policy question, "what happens if we make everyone attend tutoring?",
includes any effect tutoring has on attendance. Reporting both the model with and without
attendance, and explaining the difference, is the honest approach.

**Step 6 — what remains.** Unmeasured confounding could still bias 3.45. A sensitivity
analysis asks how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain the effect
away; with an effect this small and a strong observed confounder already accounted for,
plausible unmeasured variables (e.g. prior statistics background) could reduce it further,
possibly to zero. The credible claim is "a small positive association, plausibly causal but
not established".

**Step 7 — the trial.** To measure the effect properly, randomise an invitation to tutoring
among volunteers. With a true effect of 3.5 marks and a residual SD of 6, detecting it at
80 % power requires about 48 students per arm (\(n=16\sigma^2/\delta^2\)); with imperfect
uptake, more. That is a cheap, decisive study — far cheaper than making tutoring compulsory
on the basis of a confounded 13-mark gap.

**What the dean should hear.** "Students who attend tutoring score 13 marks higher, but they
also arrive more motivated and better prepared. Comparing like with like, the advantage is
about 3.5 marks, and even that could be partly selection. Before making tutoring compulsory,
run a randomised pilot; the evidence does not support a claim of a 13-mark benefit."
