## Multiple-choice questions

```quiz
Q1. Which measure of centre is most resistant to a single extreme observation?
A) Mean
B) Median
C) Standard deviation
D) Range
* B
Why: The median depends only on rank position, so one very large value cannot move it beyond the middle. The mean moves with the value's size, and the standard deviation and range are inflated even more.

Q2. The sample variance uses \(n-1\) in the denominator because
A) it is easier to compute
B) it makes the estimator unbiased for the population variance
C) it always gives a smaller number
D) the sample mean is unknown
* B
Why: Dividing by \(n\) underestimates the population variance on average because deviations are measured from the sample mean, which is itself fitted to the data. The \(n-1\) correction removes that downward bias.

Q3. The coefficient of variation is
A) \(\bar x/s\)
B) \(s-\bar x\)
C) \(s/\bar x\times100\%\)
D) \(s^2/\bar x\)
* C
Why: It expresses the standard deviation as a percentage of the mean, giving a unit-free measure of relative variability that can compare datasets with different units or very different means.

Q4. A distribution has mean 70 and median 55. Its shape is most likely
A) uniform
B) symmetric
C) left-skewed
D) right-skewed
* D
Why: When the mean exceeds the median, the long tail is on the right and pulls the mean upward. Left skew reverses the relationship, and symmetry makes the two nearly equal.

Q5. The interquartile range contains
A) the middle 95 % of observations
B) the middle 50 % of observations
C) all but the outliers
D) one standard deviation either side of the mean
* B
Why: By construction Q1 and Q3 cut off the lowest and highest quarters, leaving the central half. The 95 % range would be roughly mean ± 1.96 SD for normal data.

Q6. A z-score of \(-1.5\) means the observation is
A) 1.5 standard deviations below the mean
B) 1.5 units below zero
C) 1.5 % below the mean
D) an outlier
* A
Why: Standardisation divides the deviation by the standard deviation, so the units cancel. It says nothing about being an outlier, which depends on the shape of the distribution.

Q7. The mean of grouped data is computed using
A) the class limits only
B) the median class
C) the largest value in each class
D) class midpoints weighted by frequencies
* D
Why: Each class is represented by its midpoint, and the mean is the frequency-weighted average of those midpoints. Using limits ignores the distribution inside classes.

Q8. Adding the constant 10 to every observation changes the standard deviation by
A) multiplying by 10
B) adding 100
C) nothing
D) adding 10
* C
Why: A shift moves the whole distribution without changing its spread, so deviations from the mean are unchanged. Multiplying by 10 would multiply the standard deviation by 10.

Q9. The range is a weak measure of spread mainly because
A) it has squared units
B) it ignores the median
C) it is hard to compute
D) it depends on only two observations
* D
Why: Two extreme values determine it entirely, so a single outlier can double it while the rest of the data is unchanged.

Q10. The standard deviation of a dataset measured in kilograms is expressed in
A) kilograms per observation
B) kilograms squared
C) kilograms
D) no units
* C
Why: The variance has squared units, but the square root returns the standard deviation to the original units, which is why it is preferred for reporting.

Q11. The 90th percentile of a variable is the value
A) below which 90 % of observations fall
B) equal to 0.9 times the mean
C) that occurs 90 times
D) exceeded by 90 % of observations
* A
Why: Percentiles describe position in the ordered data: 90 % of values lie at or below the 90th percentile. It is not a multiple of the mean.

Q12. A bimodal distribution is best detected by
A) the mean
B) a histogram or density plot
C) the standard deviation
D) the range
* B
Why: Two peaks can have any mean or standard deviation; only a plot of the distribution reveals the two clusters, which may correspond to two subpopulations.

Q13. Under the 1.5 × IQR rule, an observation is flagged as a potential outlier when it lies
A) beyond \(Q_1-1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}\) or \(Q_3+1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}\)
B) outside the range
C) beyond the 99th percentile
D) beyond the mean ± SD
* A
Why: The fences are built from the quartiles, so they adapt to the spread of the middle half. The rule is a convention, not a test, and it flags points for inspection rather than deletion.

Q14. The variance of a constant \(c\) is
A) \(c\)
B) \(c^2\)
C) 0
D) undefined
* C
Why: A constant never deviates from its mean, so every squared deviation is zero. This is why constants drop out of variance expressions.

Q15. For a categorical variable the appropriate descriptive summary is
A) a frequency table and proportions
B) a five-number summary
C) a correlation coefficient
D) a mean and standard deviation
* A
Why: Categories have no numerical distance, so counts and proportions are the only meaningful summaries. A mean of category codes would be an artefact of the coding.
```

## Conceptual short questions

**S1.** Explain why the mean and median coincide for a symmetric distribution but differ for a
skewed one.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
In a symmetric distribution the left and right tails balance, so the balancing point (mean) and
the middle position (median) coincide. Skew puts more mass in one tail: a few extreme values
drag the mean toward that tail while the median stays at the positional centre.
</details>

**S2.** When would you prefer the coefficient of variation to the standard deviation?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
When comparing variability across variables or groups with different units or very different
means — for example, variability of weights in grams versus heights in centimetres, or
consistency of two machines producing parts of different nominal sizes. The CV is unit-free,
whereas comparing raw standard deviations would be misleading.
</details>

**S3.** What does the IQR tell you that the standard deviation does not?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The IQR describes the spread of the middle half of the data and is unaffected by the size of
extreme values, so it remains informative when the distribution is skewed or has outliers. The
standard deviation uses every value and can be dominated by a few extremes.
</details>

**S4.** Why is the sample standard deviation not an unbiased estimator of the population
standard deviation, even though the sample variance is unbiased?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Because the square root is a concave function, Jensen's inequality gives
\(E[\sqrt{S^2}]\le\sqrt{E[S^2]}=\sigma\), so the sample SD tends to underestimate \(\sigma\).
The bias is small for large \(n\), and the variance is the quantity that is unbiased.
</details>

**S5.** Explain the difference between variance and mean absolute deviation, and give one reason
each is used.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
Variance averages squared deviations; MAD averages absolute deviations. Variance is
mathematically convenient — it decomposes into between- and within-group parts and appears in
least squares — while MAD is in the original units, less sensitive to outliers, and easier to
explain to a non-specialist.
</details>

**S6.** A colleague says "the average is 5, so most values are near 5". Give two distributions
where this is false.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
(i) A bimodal distribution with half the values at 0 and half at 10 has mean 5 but no
observations near 5. (ii) A right-skewed distribution such as incomes has a mean above almost
every observation, with most values well below it.
</details>

**S7.** What is the effect of multiplying every observation by \(-1\) on the mean, variance and
skewness?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The mean changes sign; the variance and standard deviation are unchanged because deviations
change sign but their squares do not; skewness changes sign because the tails swap sides. This
is a useful check when transforming data.
</details>

**S8.** Why can a z-score be misleading when computed within a mixture of two groups?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
The pooled mean and SD blend two distributions, so a value may have a moderate z-score even
though it is extreme within its own group, or vice versa. Standardising within group, or
modelling the mixture explicitly, gives a more meaningful comparison.
</details>

**S9.** Explain what "robust statistic" means and give two examples.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A statistic is robust if a small fraction of arbitrary changes in the data — including extreme
values — cannot move it much. The median and the IQR are robust; the mean, range and standard
deviation are not. Robustness matters when data contain errors or genuine heavy tails.
</details>

**S10.** Why is the mode rarely used as the main measure of centre for continuous data?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>
A continuous variable has no repeated values, so the mode would depend on how the data are
binned or smoothed. It is useful for categorical data and for identifying peaks in a density,
but not as a general measure of centre.
</details>

## Numerical problems

**N1.** For 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 compute the mean, the sample variance and the sample standard
deviation.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Sum = 40, n = 8, mean = **5**. Squared deviations: 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16 → total 32. Sample
variance = 32/7 = **4.571**; standard deviation = **2.138**. Using n instead of n−1 would give
4.0 and 2.0, the population values for this dataset.
</details>

**N2.** Dataset A has mean 50 and SD 5; dataset B has mean 20 and SD 5. Compare their relative
variability.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
CV(A) = 5/50 = **10 %**; CV(B) = 5/20 = **25 %**. The same absolute spread is far larger
relative to B's mean, so B is more variable in relative terms. The SDs alone would have hidden
this.
</details>

**N3.** A test has mean 70 and SD 8. Compute the z-score of a mark of 78 and of 54, and
interpret both.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
\(z=(78-70)/8=1\), one SD above the mean. \(z=(54-70)/8=-2\), two SDs below. Under a
normal model about 16 % score above z = 1 and about 2.3 % below z = −2.
</details>

**N4.** Grouped data: class 0–10 has frequency 5, 10–20 has 8, 20–30 has 7. Compute the grouped
mean.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Midpoints 5, 15, 25. Weighted sum = 5×5 + 8×15 + 7×25 = 25 + 120 + 175 = 320; total frequency
= 20; mean = **16**. Note that the grouped mean is an approximation that assumes each class's
values sit at its midpoint.
</details>

**N5.** For 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 compute the median, Q1, Q3 and the IQR using the
median-of-halves rule.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
n = 8, so the median is (4 + 5)/2 = **4.5**. Lower half 1–4 has median **2.5**; upper half 5–8
has median **6.5**. IQR = 6.5 − 2.5 = **4**. The fences are 2.5 − 6 = −3.5 and 6.5 + 6 = 12.5,
so no value is flagged.
</details>

**N6.** A distribution has mean 82 and median 75. State the direction of skew and say which
summary you would report for a general audience.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Mean > median indicates **right (positive) skew**. Report the median and IQR (for example 75
with the middle half between the quartiles) because they describe the typical case without being
pulled by the right tail; the mean can be mentioned with an explanation of the skew.
</details>

**N7.** Forty employees earn a mean of 30,000; ten managers earn a mean of 50,000. Compute the
combined mean salary.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Total = 40×30,000 + 10×50,000 = 1,200,000 + 500,000 = 1,700,000 over 50 people, so the mean is
**34,000**. The unweighted average of the two means, 40,000, would overstate it.
</details>

**N8.** A variable has variance 9. What is the variance after (a) adding 5 to every value and
(b) multiplying every value by −4?

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
(a) Adding a constant leaves variance unchanged: **9**. (b) Multiplying by −4 multiplies variance
by \((-4)^2=16\): **144** (SD 12). Shifts do not affect spread; scaling does, quadratically.
</details>

**N9.** Compute the 20th percentile of 1, 2, …, 10 using linear interpolation.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Position \(=0.2\times(10+1)=2.2\), i.e. 0.2 of the way from the 2nd to the 3rd value:
\(2+0.2(3-2)=2.2\). Software varies in its percentile convention, so always state the rule
when the answer matters.
</details>

**N10.** A dataset has SD 4. Give the variance, and the approximate width of the interval
containing about 95 % of a normal sample.

<details><summary>Worked solution</summary>
Variance = \(4^2=16\). For normal data, 95 % lies within ±1.96 SD ≈ **±7.8** around the
mean, an interval of width about 15.7. This is a description of the data's spread, not a
confidence interval for the mean.
</details>

## Scenario problems

**P1.** A dashboard reports mean order value 92 and median 48. The product team wants one number
for a slide. What do you give them, and what else must appear?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Report the median (48) as the typical order, with the mean (92) labelled as inflated by a small
number of large orders, plus the 90th percentile for the tail. State the sample size and period.
A single mean would misrepresent the typical customer and invite the wrong pricing decision.
</details>

**P2.** Two classes take different tests. Class A: mean 65, SD 12. Class B: mean 78, SD 9. A
parent claims Class B is more consistent. Evaluate.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Compare CVs: A = 12/65 = 18.5 %, B = 9/78 = 11.5 %. B is indeed more consistent in relative
terms, and also has the higher mean. But consistency is not the same as quality, and the tests
differ, so scores are not directly comparable without a common scale.
</details>

**P3.** A salary dataset has one entry of 9,999,999 that looks like a data-entry error. Describe
the effect on the mean and median, and the correct handling.

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
The mean rises by roughly 9,999,999/n; the median barely moves. Do not silently delete: check
the source record, correct it if it is a typo, and document the decision. Report robust
summaries and state how many records were changed and why.
</details>

**P4.** You must compare variability of delivery times across three cities with very different
average volumes. Which statistics do you compute and why?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Median and IQR for each city (delivery times are skewed), plus the coefficient of variation if a
single relative measure is needed; report the 90th percentile because late deliveries matter
operationally. Absolute SDs would be hard to compare if the cities' typical times differ
substantially.
</details>

**P5.** A survey asks satisfaction on a 1–5 scale. A colleague computes the mean and reports
"3.4". What is missing, and what would you add?

<details><summary>Outline answer</summary>
Missing: the full distribution. Add the frequency or percentage in each category, the median,
and the proportion in the bottom two boxes (dissatisfied). For ordinal data the median and the
distribution are more honest than a mean that assumes equal spacing between categories.
</details>

## Challenging analytical problems

**C1.** Prove that \(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)=0\) and use it to show that the sum of squared
deviations is minimised at \(c=\bar x\).

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
The first identity follows from expanding: \(\sum x_i-n\bar x=0\). For the second, write
\(\sum(x_i-c)^2=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2+n(\bar x-c)^2\) (expand and use the zero-sum identity for the
cross term). The added term is non-negative and vanishes exactly at \(c=\bar x\), so the sum is
minimised there, with minimum \(\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\).
</details>

**C2.** Derive the effect of the transformation \(y_i=a+bx_i\) on the mean, the sample variance
and the coefficient of variation, and state when the CV is unchanged.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
\(\bar y=a+b\bar x\). Deviations satisfy \(y_i-\bar y=b(x_i-\bar x)\), so
\(s_y^2=b^2s_x^2\) and \(s_y=|b|s_x\). The CV becomes
\(\mathrm{CV}_y=|b|s_x/(a+b\bar x)\). For \(a=0\) and \(b>0\) it equals \(\mathrm{CV}_x\), so the
CV is invariant to pure rescaling but not to a shift — a reason not to use it for variables with
an arbitrary zero.
</details>

**C3.** Show that the sample variance can be written
\(s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum x_i^2-n\bar x^2\right)\) and use it to compute \(s^2\) for
3, 5, 7 without computing deviations.

<details><summary>Derivation</summary>
Expand \(\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=\sum x_i^2-2\bar x\sum x_i+n\bar x^2=\sum x_i^2-n\bar x^2\) using
\(\sum x_i=n\bar x\). For 3, 5, 7: \(\sum x_i^2=9+25+49=83\), \(n\bar x^2=3\times25=75\), so
\(s^2=(83-75)/2=4\), matching the deviation calculation.
</details>
