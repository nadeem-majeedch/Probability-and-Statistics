## A. Problem statement

A motor insurer holds 400 policies for one year. Most policyholders made no claim;
those who did claimed amounts ranging from a few thousand to over forty thousand. The
actuarial team needs a risk summary: how often claims occur, how large they are when they
occur, what the total and average cost per policy are, and how much capital should be held
for unusually large claims.

## B. Background and context

Insurance is the archetypal two-part random variable: a Bernoulli event (claim or no
claim) multiplied by a positive, heavy-tailed amount. The resulting distribution is a
spike at zero plus a long right tail, and its mean is a poor guide to its risk. Actuaries
separate frequency from severity, model severity on a log scale, and use quantiles (the
99th percentile, the "value at risk") to size reserves. Students who learn to handle
zero-inflated, heavy-tailed data here will recognise the same shape in hospital costs,
e-commerce order values and network traffic.

**Generation method.** The claim probability rises with vehicle age and is 3 points
higher in urban areas, clipped at 0.6. Given a claim, the amount is lognormal with log-mean
9.2 and log-SD 0.7, so the median claim is about 9,900 and the distribution is strongly
right-skewed. The realised dataset has 80 claiming policies (20 %) and 320 zeros.

## D. Questions to investigate

1. What proportion of policies made a claim? What does the distribution look like?
2. Compare the mean, median and standard deviation of claim amounts overall and among
   claimants only.
3. How skewed is the severity distribution, and does a log scale help?
4. What is the total claim cost, and what is the average cost per policy?
5. What is the 95th and 99th percentile of claim size? Why do insurers care about these?
6. Is claim amount related to vehicle age? Is the relationship strong?
7. Estimate the probability that a claim exceeds 20,000.

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Bernoulli and two-part (zero-inflated) distributions.
- Expectation of a mixture: \(E[X]=P(\text{claim})\cdot E[X\mid\text{claim}]\).
- Lognormal distribution: median, mean, and heavy tail.
- Skewness and the mean–median gap.
- Quantiles, value at risk and tail risk.
- Correlation with a heavily skewed variable.
- Sample variability of a total; standard error of a mean.

## F. Mathematical formulation

Let \(C\) be the claim indicator and \(S\) the severity. Then

\[
X = C\cdot S,\qquad
E[X]=P(C=1)\,E[S\mid C=1],\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[C]\,\mathrm{Var}(S)+E[C](1-E[C])\,E[S]^2 .
\]

For lognormal severity with parameters \(\mu,\sigma\):

\[
\text{median}=e^{\mu},\qquad
E[S]=e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2},\qquad
Q(p)=e^{\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)}.
\]

The sample mean claim cost per policy is \(\bar X=\frac1n\sum_i X_i\) with standard error
\(s/\sqrt n\), and the total is \(n\bar X\) with standard error \(n\,s/\sqrt n=\sqrt n\,s\).

## G. Step-by-step analytical approach

1. Load the file; count zeros and positives.
2. Summarise claim amounts overall; then among claimants only.
3. Compute skewness before and after a log transformation.
4. Estimate the claim frequency and the mean severity; multiply to get the pure premium.
5. Compute the total and the average cost, with a standard error for the total.
6. Compute the 95th and 99th percentiles; interpret as reserve requirements.
7. Estimate \(P(S>20{,}000)\) empirically and from the fitted lognormal.
8. Correlate vehicle age with claim amount and comment on the weakness of the relationship.

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

policies = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/insurance_claims.csv")
claims = policies["claim_amount"]
positive = claims[claims > 0]

print(policies.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print(f"policies: {len(policies)}, claiming: {len(positive)} "
      f"({len(positive) / len(policies):.1%}), zeros: {int((claims == 0).sum())}")

print("\nall policies:")
print(f"  mean = {claims.mean():,.2f}, median = {claims.median():,.2f}, "
      f"sd = {claims.std(ddof=1):,.2f}, skew = {stats.skew(claims):.2f}")
print("claimants only:")
print(f"  mean = {positive.mean():,.2f}, median = {positive.median():,.2f}, "
      f"sd = {positive.std(ddof=1):,.2f}, skew = {stats.skew(positive):.2f}")

log_claims = np.log(positive)
mu, sigma = log_claims.mean(), log_claims.std(ddof=1)
print(f"\nlog severity: mu = {mu:.3f}, sigma = {sigma:.3f}, "
      f"skew after log = {stats.skew(log_claims):.2f}")
print(f"lognormal median = {np.exp(mu):,.2f}, mean = {np.exp(mu + sigma**2 / 2):,.2f}")

frequency = len(positive) / len(policies)
pure_premium = frequency * positive.mean()
se_total = np.sqrt(len(policies)) * claims.std(ddof=1)
se_mean = claims.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(policies))
print(f"\nfrequency = {frequency:.3f}, mean severity = {positive.mean():,.2f}, "
      f"pure premium = {pure_premium:,.2f}")
print(f"total cost = {claims.sum():,.2f} (SE {se_total:,.0f})")
print(f"mean cost per policy = {claims.mean():,.2f} (95% CI "
      f"{claims.mean() - 1.96 * se_mean:,.2f} to {claims.mean() + 1.96 * se_mean:,.2f})")

for p in (95, 99):
    print(f"severity p{p} = {np.percentile(positive, p):,.0f}; "
          f"all-policy p{p} = {np.percentile(claims, p):,.0f}")

threshold = 20_000
print(f"\nP(claim > {threshold:,}) empirical = {(claims > threshold).mean():.4f}")
print(f"P(claim > {threshold:,}) lognormal = "
      f"{1 - stats.norm.cdf((np.log(threshold) - mu) / sigma):.4f}")

top_share = positive.nlargest(int(len(positive) * 0.05)).sum() / claims.sum()
print(f"share of total cost from the 5% largest claims = {top_share:.1%}")
print(f"corr(vehicle age, claim amount) = "
      f"{np.corrcoef(policies.vehicle_age_years, claims)[0, 1]:.3f}")
```

**How to read it.** The left panel is dominated by the spike at zero: 80 % of policies
produce no claim, so the histogram of all policies is uninformative about severity. The
right panel, restricted to claimants and plotted on a log scale, looks like a symmetric
bell — the lognormal signature. Any analysis that mixes the two populations (for example
a mean of all policies) is really reporting a frequency-weighted average, not a typical
claim.

## K. Expected conclusions

- 20 % of policies claimed (80 of 400); 320 policies had zero claims.
- Overall mean 2,273, median 0, SD 6,331 — the mean is a frequency-weighted artefact.
- Among claimants: mean 11,366, median 8,563, SD 9,888, skew 2.70.
- The 95th percentile of severity is about 26,700 and the 99th about 48,300; the top 5 %
  of claims account for about 19 % of total cost.
- Total cost is 909,285, giving an average of 2,273 per policy with a 95 % interval of
  roughly 1,652 to 2,894.
- Empirical \(P(\text{claim}>20{,}000)\approx 0.0275\) across all policies, i.e. 0.1375
  among claimants, close to the lognormal prediction of 0.135.
- Vehicle age is essentially uncorrelated with amount (r = −0.04): frequency, not
  severity, is where age matters.

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — the two-part structure.** With 80 claimants and 320 zeros, the "average claim"
of 2,273 is not a typical claim; it is the expected cost per policy. The correct summary
of severity uses claimants only: mean 11,366, median 8,563. The median is well below the
mean because severity is right-skewed (skew 2.70).

**Step 2 — frequency and severity.** Frequency 0.20, mean severity 11,366, so the pure
premium is 0.20 × 11,366 = 2,273 per policy, matching the observed mean exactly (by
construction of the identity \(E[X]=P(C)E[S]\)). This decomposition is the core actuarial
insight: frequency and severity can be modelled separately and multiplied.

**Step 3 — lognormal fit.** Log severity has \(\hat\mu = 9.046\) and \(\hat\sigma = 0.778\).
The fitted median is \(e^{9.046} = 8,488\) (observed 8,563) and the fitted mean is
\(e^{9.046+0.778^2/2} = 11,460\) (observed 11,366). Both agree closely, so the lognormal is
an adequate severity model. After the log transformation the skewness falls from 2.70 to
about −0.1, i.e. essentially symmetric.

**Step 4 — tail risk.** p95 of severity is about 26,700 and p99 about 48,300. The top 5 %
of claims (four policies) contribute about 19 % of the 909,285 total. An insurer
pricing only on the mean would under-reserve; the 99th percentile is the quantity that
determines capital. Note that this is a small sample: the empirical p99 sits close to the
second largest observation, so it is itself highly variable.

**Step 5 — uncertainty.** The standard error of the mean cost per policy is
6,331/√400 = 317, so the 95 % interval is 2,273 ± 621, i.e. 1,653 to 2,894. The standard error of the total is
√400 × 6,331 = 126,620 — the total is much less precisely estimated than the mean,
relative to its size, because it scales with \(n\) while its standard error scales with
\(\sqrt n\). This is why insurers hold capital proportional to portfolio size and why small
books are volatile.

**Step 6 — the age relationship.** Correlation between vehicle age and claim amount is
−0.044, essentially zero. Yet the generator makes older cars more likely to claim — the
effect is on frequency, not on amount. Pooling the two (as a correlation of amount with
age) dilutes the frequency effect into zeros and produces a misleading "no relationship".
A correct analysis models frequency (logistic regression on claim/no claim) and severity
separately.

**Step 7 — practical recommendation.** Price at the pure premium of 2,273 per policy plus
expenses, hold capital for the 99th percentile of aggregate cost, and investigate whether
the four largest claims share a cause (they do not, by construction). Report severity on
the log scale with median and interquartile range, because the mean is unstable from year
to year in a heavy-tailed portfolio.

**Common errors.** (i) Reporting the mean of all policies as "the average claim". (ii)
Applying normal-based intervals to raw severity, which will produce negative lower bounds.
(iii) Ignoring the zeros when modelling severity, or ignoring severity when modelling
frequency. (iv) Treating the empirical p99 from 80 claims as a stable estimate.
