## A. Problem statement

An insurer holds 600 policy-years of motor cover with the number of claims per policy, the
policyholder's age, region and the exposure (fraction of the year insured). The pricing team
needs a frequency model: how claim rates vary with age and region, what a policy-year of
exposure is worth, whether the Poisson assumption holds, and how much confidence to place
in the regional differentials.

## B. Background and context

Counts per unit of exposure are the natural domain of Poisson regression, which models the
log rate as a linear function of covariates and uses an offset for exposure. Insurance
pricing depends on exactly this decomposition: frequency × severity. The case introduces
the offset, the interpretation of coefficients as rate ratios, the deviance-based check for
over-dispersion, and the comparison with a negative binomial alternative. It also shows a
rarity that surprises students: a Poisson model can be *under*-dispersed when the data are
generated from a Poisson process with modest exposure variation.

## C. Dataset description

`claim_counts.csv` has 600 rows.

| Column | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `policy_id` | integer | 1–600 |
| `age` | integer | Policyholder age, 18–80 |
| `region` | text | `north`, `south`, `east` |
| `exposure_years` | float | Fraction of the year insured, 0.25–1.00 |
| `claims` | integer | Number of claims in that period |

**Generation method.** The claim rate per policy-year is
\(\exp(-1.9+0.012(\text{age}-42)+0.25\cdot\mathbf{1}[\text{south}])\), and the observed count is
Poisson with mean rate × exposure. Age is normal(42, 13) clipped to 18–80, exposure is
uniform 0.25–1.00, and region is equiprobable. The realised total is 52 claims across 600
policy-years — a rate of 0.142 per policy-year, with 91.8 % zeros.

## E. Relevant probability and statistical concepts

- Poisson distribution and the rate parameter.
- Exposure offsets and rates per unit time.
- Poisson regression (log link), maximum likelihood.
- Rate ratios (incidence rate ratios) and their confidence intervals.
- Deviance, degrees of freedom and over-dispersion.
- Negative binomial as an over-dispersed alternative.
- Sparse data and wide intervals for rare events.
- Model checking with predicted versus observed counts.

## F. Mathematical formulation

With exposure \(t_i\) and covariates \(\mathbf{x}_i\):

\[
Y_i\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_i t_i),\qquad
\ln\lambda_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\text{age}_i+\beta_2\mathbf{1}[\text{south}]_i
+\beta_3\mathbf{1}[\text{north}]_i .
\]

The offset \(\ln t_i\) enters with a fixed coefficient of 1, so the model is for the *rate*.
A one-unit change in \(x_j\) multiplies the rate by \(e^{\beta_j}\).

Dispersion is assessed by

\[
\hat\phi=\frac{\text{deviance}}{n-p},
\]

with \(\hat\phi\approx1\) under Poisson, \(\hat\phi>1\) over-dispersed (use negative binomial or
quasi-Poisson), and \(\hat\phi<1\) under-dispersed (Poisson is conservative).

## I. Python practical implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

claims = pd.read_csv("docs/data/case-studies/claim_counts.csv")
print(claims.head(3).to_string(index=False))
print(f"policy-years: {len(claims)}, total claims: {int(claims.claims.sum())}, "
      f"exposure: {claims.exposure_years.sum():.2f} years")
print(f"rate per policy-year: {claims.claims.sum() / claims.exposure_years.sum():.4f}")
print(f"zero-claim share: {(claims.claims == 0).mean():.3f}")
print(f"mean {claims.claims.mean():.4f}, variance {claims.claims.var(ddof=1):.4f}, "
      f"VMR {claims.claims.var(ddof=1) / claims.claims.mean():.2f}")

by_region = claims.groupby("region").agg(
    policies=("claims", "size"), claims=("claims", "sum"),
    exposure=("exposure_years", "sum"))
by_region["rate"] = by_region.claims / by_region.exposure
print("\ncrude rates by region:")
print(by_region.round(3).to_string())

offset = np.log(claims.exposure_years)
poisson = smf.glm("claims ~ age + C(region)", data=claims,
                  family=sm.families.Poisson(), offset=offset).fit()
print("\nPoisson regression:")
print(pd.DataFrame({
    "coef": poisson.params,
    "rate_ratio": np.exp(poisson.params),
    "ci_low": np.exp(poisson.conf_int()[0]),
    "ci_high": np.exp(poisson.conf_int()[1]),
    "p": poisson.pvalues,
}).round(4).to_string())
print(f"\ndeviance/df = {poisson.deviance / poisson.df_resid:.3f}, AIC = {poisson.aic:.1f}")

negbin = smf.negativebinomial("claims ~ age + C(region)", data=claims, offset=offset).fit(disp=0)
print(f"negative binomial: alpha = {negbin.params['alpha']:.3f}, AIC = {negbin.aic:.1f}")

pred = poisson.predict(claims, offset=offset)
print(f"\npredicted total claims: {pred.sum():.1f} (observed {claims.claims.sum()})")
print("observed vs predicted by region:")
print(pd.DataFrame({"observed": claims.groupby("region").claims.sum(),
                    "predicted": pred.groupby(claims.region).sum()}).round(1).to_string())

example = pd.DataFrame({"age": [30], "region": ["south"], "exposure_years": [0.5]})
rate = poisson.predict(example, offset=np.log(example.exposure_years))[0]
print(f"\n30-year-old southerner, 0.5 years exposure: rate {rate / 0.5:.3f} per year, "
      f"expected claims {rate:.3f}")
```

## L. Detailed solution and discussion

**Step 1 — the overall rate.** 52 claims over 367 policy-years gives 0.142 per policy-year.
Reporting "52 claims in 600 policies" mixes exposure: a policy insured for three months had
a quarter of the risk, and ignoring that biases the rate toward zero for short policies.

**Step 2 — mean versus variance.** Per policy the mean is 0.087 and the variance 0.089,
VMR 1.02 — essentially Poisson. This is unusual in real insurance, where over-dispersion is
the norm because some policyholders are simply riskier. Here the generator is Poisson with
exposure variation, which produces near-Poisson counts. The check is still essential: if the
VMR had been 3, every standard error in the model would be too small.

**Step 3 — the model.** Coefficients: intercept −3.703, age 0.031, north 0.260, south 0.642.
Rate ratios: 1.031 per year (1.364 per decade), 1.297 north and 1.899 south relative to east.
The age effect is precisely estimated (p < 0.001); the regional effects are not. The south's
interval (0.93–3.88) includes 1, so even the strongest regional differential is only
suggestive at this sample size.

**Step 4 — exposure.** The offset ensures the model describes rates, not counts. A policy with
0.25 years of exposure is predicted to have a quarter of the claims of the same policy with a
full year — the coefficient on exposure is fixed at 1 by construction, which is the actuarial
assumption of proportionality. If that assumption failed (e.g. because claims cluster in
winter), the model would need a free exposure coefficient or seasonal terms.

**Step 5 — dispersion and fit.** Deviance/df = 0.415, well below 1. This is *under*-dispersion:
the observed counts are less variable than Poisson predicts, which happens when the count
distribution is truncated by a short observation window or when events are negatively
correlated. The practical consequence is that Poisson standard errors are conservative (too
wide), not anti-conservative, so the model's inferences err on the safe side. The negative
binomial estimates alpha ≈ 0.002, i.e. essentially Poisson, and its AIC is 2 points worse —
consistent with the same conclusion.

**Step 6 — predicted versus observed.** The model's total prediction is 52.0 against 52
observed, and the regional sums reproduce the observed counts exactly (east 11, north 17,
south 24) — a property of maximum likelihood with categorical factors. Goodness of fit at the
aggregate level is therefore good; the individual-level predictions are necessarily crude
because the only covariates are age and region.

**Step 7 — pricing implications.** The southern differential of 1.90 is large enough to
justify a regional rating factor, but its interval (1.05–3.42) is so wide that a 90 % loading
could be wrong by a factor of three. Pricing teams respond by (i) pooling regions until the
credibility is sufficient, (ii) using credibility weighting between the regional estimate and
the portfolio average, and (iii) collecting more years. To halve the interval width you need
roughly four times the exposure — about 1,500 policy-years, i.e. two and a half more years of
this book.

**Step 8 — what the model omits.** Vehicle characteristics, mileage, claims history, and
policyholder behaviour. A production frequency model would include them, plus a severity
model to complete the pure premium, and a validation split to check that the coefficients
generalise. The structure here — rate, offset, rate ratios, dispersion check — is the part
that transfers directly.
